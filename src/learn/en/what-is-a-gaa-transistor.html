A GAA transistor is a transistor whose "gate," the part that turns it on and off, wraps all the way around the path for electrons. This makes it easier to stop current leaking while the transistor is off, even when it is very small. Today, a version made of stacked thin plates a few nm thick (nanosheets) is coming into use. It appears in leading-edge chips, such as the "2 nm class."

In everyday life: "GAA" in the news

"Samsung begins 3 nm chip production with GAA." "TSMC starts volume production at 2 nm." Since around 2022, the term GAA has appeared in news like this. In October 2025, Intel also unveiled a new chip for laptops, code-named Panther Lake. It is made with a technology called "Intel 18A," which uses GAA.

News stories often mention "2 nm" and "GAA" together, but they are two separate things. "2 nm" is the name of a generation of chipmaking technology. It does not mean that any part is 2 nm long (see "What Does 2nm or 3nm Mean?"). GAA, on the other hand, is the name of a transistor shape.

This article starts with why transistors needed a new shape. Then it looks at what GAA looks like, how it is made, and what may come next.

A transistor must switch off reliably

First, a quick review. The transistors inside a chip are tiny switches flipped by electricity. Electrons enter at the source and leave at the drain, with a path for them in between. The gate sits above this path. A voltage on the gate opens the path and turns the switch on. Lowering the voltage turns it off.

When the switch is off, something like a "wall" between the source and drain blocks the electrons. The gate uses its voltage to raise or lower this wall, which stops or allows the flow. It is not a real wall. It is a difference in energy levels for the electrons, and calling it a wall makes it easier to picture.

An ideal switch lets no current through at all when it is off. Real transistors leak a little current even when off. The leak from one transistor is tiny, but a chip holds billions of them. Added together, the leaks drain the battery and make heat even while the chip is idle (see "What Is CMOS?").

Shrink it, and the gate can no longer guard the wall

Making a transistor smaller also shortens the distance between source and drain. The drain voltage then reaches deep into the path. It pushes the wall down, no matter what the gate is doing. Engineers call this {{term:DIBL|DIBL}}, short for "drain-induced barrier lowering" (the barrier is the wall).

As a result, current leaks even when the gate is set to off, because the wall is lower. The voltage where the transistor flips between on and off also shifts a lot if the path length differs only slightly. Engineers call the path for electrons the channel. These problems of a short channel are grouped together as the {{term:短チャネル効果|short-channel effect}}.

The idea behind the fix is simple. Make the gate touch the channel more closely and over more area, so it controls the wall more strongly than the drain does. Making the channel thinner works the same way, because the gate's influence then reaches all the way through. The history of transistor shapes is a story of covering more sides with the gate.

When the channel gets even shorter, down to a few nm, electrons also start to slip through the wall. This is a separate limit, and a cleverer shape can hardly prevent it. "Quantum Tunneling in Transistors and Flash Memory" explains it.

An analogy

The gate and the drain are like two teams in a tug-of-war over the height of the wall. The gate tries to keep the wall high. The drain voltage tries to pull it down. While the channel is long, the drain is far away and its pull barely reaches. As the channel gets shorter, the drain comes closer and the gate is more likely to lose.

Covering more sides with the gate is like giving the gate's team more hands on the rope. The two match on one point only. Two sides pull on the same thing, and a side is stronger when it is closer and has more grip.

Where the analogy breaks down: A real transistor has no rope and no contest of strength. The electrical influences (electric fields) from the gate and drain voltages simply overlap inside the silicon. Also, even if the gate wins the tug-of-war completely, leakage does not drop to zero. Some electrons, for example, still get over the wall with heat energy.

Planar → FinFET → GAA: more sides covered

The flat shape, called planar, was used for a long time. In it, the gate touched only one side of the channel: the top. The lower part of the channel was far from the gate and easily affected by the drain.

The next shape stands the channel upright like a thin plate. The gate then covers it on three sides: the top and both sides. The upright plate looks like a fish's fin, so this is called a {{term:FinFET|FinFET}}. In 2011, Intel announced it would use this shape in mass production, starting with its 22 nm chips (Intel calls it Tri-Gate).

As shrinking went on, even three sides were no longer enough. The base of a FinFET fin is attached to the silicon underneath, and the gate cannot wrap around that part. So engineers lifted the channel off the base and wrapped the gate all the way around it. This is {{term:GAA(ゲートオールアラウンド)|GAA}}, short for gate-all-around: the gate is on every side.

Comparison pages set out the differences among the three shapes in tables: "Planar MOSFET vs FinFET" and "FinFET vs GAA Transistors."

Nanosheets: stacking thin plates for more current

A thinner channel lets the gate's influence reach all the way through. But a single thin channel can carry only a small current. With little current, the circuits it drives cannot run fast.

So GAA stacks several thin plates of silicon, with gaps between them. The gate wraps each plate, and every plate connects to the same source and drain. When the transistor turns on, current flows through all the plates at once. Their total is the transistor's current. These thin plates are called {{term:ナノシート|nanosheets}}.

Let's look at an example of the sizes. In 2021, IBM announced a "2 nm" prototype. According to reports that read IBM's images, the sheets were stacked three high, and each was about 5 nm thick. That is only about 20 times the spacing between silicon atoms (about 0.235 nm).

Each company uses its own name. Samsung named its version MBCFET (a transistor with several bridge-like channels). Intel calls it RibbonFET (a transistor with ribbon-shaped channels), and TSMC calls it "nanosheet." The details of how they are made differ. But the basic idea is the same: stack plates and wrap the gate around them.

Choosing the channel width is another benefit

Some parts of a circuit need a large current to run fast. Other parts should use less current to save power. So it helps if each transistor can have its own channel width.

In a FinFET, all the fins on a chip are made with the same height and thickness. To get more current, you place two or three fins side by side. So current can only be chosen in steps: one fin's worth, two fins' worth, and so on.

With nanosheets, designers can choose the sheet width when they draw the circuit layout. Circuits where speed matters can use wide sheets. Circuits that must save power or area can use narrow ones. In its 2022 announcement, Samsung also named this as a benefit: changing the sheet width can tune power and performance. In practice, though, the width can only be chosen within the manufacturing rules.

How is it made? Stack two layers, then remove one

Many plates must float in the air, and the gate must fill even the gaps between them. How is that done? The basic idea is to build a sandwich of two kinds of layers, one after the other, and later remove only one kind. Here we cover only the outline and skip many of the steps.

One material is silicon. The other is {{term:SiGe(シリコンゲルマニウム)|SiGe}} (silicon-germanium), a crystal made by mixing the element germanium into silicon. Its atomic spacing is close to that of silicon, so thin layers stack neatly. Yet the two wear away at different rates, so one can be removed later while the other stays.

First, SiGe and silicon layers a few nm thick are stacked in turn. They go on a round silicon plate (a wafer), which is the base for the chips. Each layer is grown so it continues the atomic pattern of the crystal below. This method is called {{term:エピタキシャル成長|epitaxial growth}}. Next, the stack is cut into narrow strips, and the source and drain are built at both ends. Patterns are drawn with light (lithography), and material is carved away by etching.

Now only the SiGe is removed. This uses etching under conditions that remove SiGe quickly but barely touch silicon. How large this difference is gets called the selectivity. Once the SiGe is gone, the silicon plates remain like bridges, held up by the source and drain at both ends.

Finally, a very thin insulating film and the gate metal go into the gaps around the plates. The gaps are very narrow, so the film is built one atomic layer at a time, a method called ALD (see "What Is Thin-Film Deposition?"). In practice, more steps are needed. For example, insulating spacers go at the ends of the gaps so the gate does not touch the source and drain.

Stacking two kinds of layers, removing only one of them, and filling the gaps between the plates with the gate: none of these steps existed for FinFETs. More steps and harder ones also make the chips more costly to make. "How Are Chips Made?" explains the whole chipmaking flow.

What comes after GAA? CFET stacks nMOS on pMOS

Most chip circuits combine two kinds of transistors that act in opposite ways: nMOS and pMOS (see "What Is CMOS?"). Today, the two sit side by side on the chip. Stacking one on top of the other would let the same circuit fit in less area. This shape is called {{term:CFET(相補型FET)|CFET}} (complementary FET).

CFET is still at the research and development stage. In December 2023, at the international conference IEDM, Intel, Samsung and TSMC each presented CFET research. IEEE, a society of electrical and electronics engineers, covered all three in its magazine. In June 2024, imec, a research institute in Belgium, also announced a prototype of stacked transistors that worked with their contacts connected.

In a December 2024 announcement, imec set out its own technology outlook. In it, imec plans to use CFET from a generation it calls "A7." The "A" stands for the angstrom, a unit of length (0.1 nm), so A7 is a name that corresponds to 0.7 nm. But this, too, is a generation name, not a dimension. For the generation before that, imec is considering a shape called a "forksheet." It brings nMOS and pMOS sheets close together, with a thin wall between them.

Common misconceptions

Myth: With GAA, there is no leakage when the transistor is off

In fact, GAA only reduces leakage. Even when the gate fully surrounds the channel, some electrons still get over the wall with heat energy. Electrons can also slip through the gate's thin insulating film. That is why today's chips still use other tricks, such as cutting power to parts that are not in use. The myth comes from how absolute the phrase "all around" sounds.

Myth: "2 nm" is the thickness of a GAA sheet or the length of its gate

In fact, it is a name that tells generations apart. Even in IBM's "2 nm" prototype, the sheets were about 5 nm thick and the gate was about 12 nm long. Also, Samsung began using GAA at "3 nm," while TSMC uses it from "2 nm." Generations with the same number can use different shapes, so the number does not describe the transistor's shape either.

Myth: Now that GAA is here, FinFET and planar transistors are no longer used

In fact, the switch has happened only in some leading-edge generations. Generations that use FinFETs, such as TSMC's 3 nm generation, are still in mass production. Chips for cars and home appliances often use older, cheaper generations or planar transistors. New shapes need more steps and cost more, so they are used first where they are really needed.
