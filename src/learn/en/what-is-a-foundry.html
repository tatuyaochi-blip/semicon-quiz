A foundry is a company that makes semiconductor chips designed by other companies, to order. Leading-edge factories have become so expensive that few companies can pay one off with their own products alone. So the industry split into "companies that design" and "companies that make." The best-known foundry is TSMC in Taiwan, and Japan's Rapidus is also aiming for this model.

Prerequisites: it helps to know that a chip (integrated circuit) is "an electronic circuit that packs tiny parts and wiring onto one piece of silicon." "What Is an Integrated Circuit?" explains this in detail.

In the news: TSMC and Rapidus

"TSMC's fab in Kumamoto, Japan, has started volume production." "Rapidus has made 2 nm prototypes in Hokkaido, Japan." Headlines like these often use the word foundry. Both companies make chips for other companies.

Your phone is a good example. Apple designs the chips in the iPhone itself. But Apple has no factory that makes chips. In an August 2025 announcement, Apple said it is the "largest customer" of TSMC's factory in Arizona.

In other words, the company that draws a chip's blueprint and the company that builds it are different. This article looks at how that happened, and at what the building side actually sells.

Three jobs that turn a design into a chip

Making a chip involves three broad jobs. The first is design. Engineers decide what the circuit should do and create its blueprint on a computer.

The second is the front end. Here the circuit from the blueprint is built into a round slice of silicon called a wafer. Patterns are printed with light, films are added, and material is etched away, again and again, dozens of times. This stage needs the largest and most costly equipment.

The third is the back end. The wafer is cut into individual chips. Each chip goes into a protective case with terminals that connect it to the outside, called a package, and is then tested. "How Are Chips Made?" walks through the steps inside the factory.

The four types of semiconductor company

In the industry, a chip factory is called a "fab." The word is short for fabrication, which means manufacturing. Knowing this makes the names of the company types easier to follow.

A company that only designs and sells chips, with no factory of its own, is called {{term:ファブレス|fabless}}. The name simply means "without a fab." NVIDIA and Qualcomm are typical examples, and they sell chips under their own names.

On the other side, a company that takes other companies' blueprints and does the front-end manufacturing is a foundry. The English word originally meant a workshop where metal is melted and poured into molds. A foundry does not sell its own products. It sells the work of making them.

A company that designs, manufactures and sells all by itself is called an {{term:IDM(垂直統合型デバイスメーカー)|IDM}}, short for integrated device manufacturer. "Integrated" here means one company covers the whole chain, from design through manufacturing to sales. Intel and memory maker Samsung Electronics are typical examples.

A company that takes on back-end assembly and testing is called an {{term:OSAT|OSAT}}. A common split is that the foundry makes the wafers and the OSAT turns them into finished chips.

A company does not always fit just one type, though. Samsung Electronics is an IDM that makes its own memory. At the same time, it runs a foundry business that makes chips for other companies.

Why design and manufacturing split

In the past, most chip companies designed their own chips and made them in their own factories. In other words, the IDM was the norm. A big reason the split spread is that factories became extremely expensive.

A factory pays off best when it runs nonstop after it is built. The share of a factory's capacity that is actually in use is called the utilization rate. If one company's products are the only thing in the factory, utilization drops whenever sales fall. Costly equipment then sits idle.

A foundry fills its factory with orders from many companies. If one company orders less, orders from others can more easily fill the gap. This way, the huge cost of equipment is shared among many customers.

The pioneer of this model is TSMC, founded in Taiwan in 1987. The company says it "pioneered the business model of manufacturing customers' designs only." It does not design or sell chips under its own brand, so it does not compete with its customers. Customers can hand over their blueprints with confidence.

Once there was a partner to take on manufacturing, even companies without factories could make leading-edge chips. This is how fabless companies, which compete on design, grew large.

What does a foundry sell?

What a foundry sells to its customers falls into three broad groups. To keep things simple, this section focuses on front-end manufacturing.

1. Manufacturing technology (process)

Which materials and steps are used, and how fine the circuits can be. This full recipe is called a "process." Besides the newest generation, there are processes for each use: low power, high voltage, sensors and more. TSMC reports that in 2025 it used 305 technologies to make 12,682 products for 534 customers.

2. Production capacity

Being able to make a set amount by a set date is also a key product. Capacity is measured by how many wafers a fab can process per month or per year. Slots for popular generations fill up, so customers try to book them early.

3. Design tools and support

A design company must draw its blueprint to fit the foundry's process. It needs rules, such as the minimum width of a line and the minimum gap between lines. It also needs data that lets a computer simulate how the transistors behave. The foundry hands all of this over as one package, called a {{term:PDK(プロセスデザインキット)|PDK}} (process design kit).

A blueprint that follows the PDK rules can be expected to be buildable in that fab and to work as predicted. Foundries also partner with design-software companies and with companies that sell ready-made designs for common circuits. Since 2008, TSMC has run this kind of partnership program under the name "OIP."

The flip side is that a blueprint is tied to one foundry's process. Moving to another foundry usually means reworking the design. This is one reason customers cannot easily switch.

An analogy

A foundry is a bit like a book printer. A publisher creates the book's content, then pays a printer with expensive presses to print it. The printer takes jobs from many publishers, so its machines never sit idle. The one thing that matches is this: content and costly equipment belong to separate companies, and many customers share that equipment.

Where the analogy breaks down: A printer copies the manuscript onto paper almost exactly as it is. In a foundry, the process itself strongly affects how well the chip performs, and the blueprint is drawn to fit that process. Also, making a chip is not a one-pass print job. The front end alone repeats its steps dozens of times and takes weeks to months.

Why are leading-edge fabs so expensive?

One reason is that the tools that print the finest patterns are very expensive, and a fab needs many of them. The main example is the {{term:EUV露光|EUV lithography}} tool, which uses light with a wavelength of 13.5 nm. A fab also needs rooms with almost no dust, and many tools to add films, etch them and measure them.

Let's look at some figures. In 2025, TSMC spent US$40.9 billion on factories and equipment, known as capital expenditure. That is up again from US$29.8 billion in 2024, according to the company's earnings call.

In March 2025, TSMC announced a plan to expand its investment in the United States to a total of US$165 billion. The plan includes six fabs, two advanced packaging plants and a research and development center. Note that this is a planned amount, not money already spent.

Only a few companies can earn back costs on this scale. In 2018, GlobalFoundries in the United States announced that it was halting development of its 7 nm generation, then the leading edge, indefinitely. It judged that too few customers needed the leading edge to justify the investment (IEEE Spectrum, 2018). Since then, it has focused on specialized processes, such as those for communications.

How to read foundry news

Market share: who counted what, and when

News such as "TSMC's share tops 70%" often quotes figures from TrendForce, a market research firm in Taiwan. According to TrendForce, foundry revenue shares for April–June 2026 were as follows. TSMC had 72.5%, Samsung Electronics 5.9%, and SMIC 5.4%.

These figures are based on revenue for each three-month quarter. Each research firm decides differently which companies, and which revenue, to count. So you cannot compare them directly with another firm's figures. When you see a number in a headline, check where it came from and what period it covers.

"X nm" is a generation name

Numbers such as "2 nm volume production" are names given to process generations. They do not mean that some part of the chip measures 2 nm. Between companies, the same number does not mean the same thing. "What Does 2nm or 3nm Mean?" explains this in detail.

Plan, prototype or volume production?

In news about fabs, notice which stage the story is about. A "plan" or "investment pledge" means the money has not yet been spent. A "prototype" means a small batch made to check that things work. "Volume production" means making large numbers as real products. Even for the same fab, the meaning changes a lot with the stage.

Common misconceptions

Myth: A foundry is just a subcontractor that builds whatever it is told

In fact, a foundry develops and provides the process itself. How fine a chip can be, and how little power it can run on, depend heavily on the foundry's technology. Design companies design with that technology as their starting point. The phrase "contract manufacturing" makes it easy to assume that only the customer has the technology. That is where the myth comes from.

Myth: Fabless companies, with no factories, are not real chip companies

In fact, they do the design work that decides what a chip does and how well it performs, and they sell chips under their own names. NVIDIA, for example, has no factories, yet it is one of the world's leading companies in chips for AI. The myth comes from picturing a "maker" as a company that builds things in its own factory.

Myth: "TSMC has a 70% share" means TSMC makes 70% of the world's chips

In fact, it is a share of revenue within the foundry market only. Chips that IDMs make in their own fabs and sell, such as memory, are not included. Also, the count is by revenue, not by number of chips. Companies that make many expensive leading-edge chips therefore show up larger.
