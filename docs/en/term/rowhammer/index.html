What is Rowhammer?

A DRAM phenomenon in which repeatedly activating the same row in a short time drains charge from cells in neighboring rows and flips their data. Because it can be exploited in attacks, countermeasures are built into DRAMs and memory controllers.

Japanese name: ロウハンマー

Rowhammer appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Hardware Security, DRAM.

Field: DRAM

Why hammering flips bits

DRAM cells store data as charge that slowly leaks, which is why each row is refreshed within a fixed window, typically 64 ms. Activating a row swings its word line, and repeated activation of the same aggressor row couples electrically into physically adjacent victim rows and promotes charge migration that speeds their leakage. If an aggressor is activated tens of thousands of times before the victims are refreshed, some victim cells lose enough charge to flip. Hammering the two rows on either side of a victim (double-sided) is more effective, and denser cells are more susceptible.

Why it matters for security

Rowhammer needs no special privileges, only ordinary memory reads issued fast enough that they reach DRAM instead of hitting in cache, for example by flushing or evicting the cache lines. After it was widely reported in 2014, researchers showed it could cross isolation boundaries: flipping bits in page-table entries to gain kernel privileges, corrupting data belonging to other processes or virtual machines, and triggering flips from sandboxed code. A hardware reliability flaw thus became a software-exploitable vulnerability.

Countermeasures

Target row refresh (TRR) tracks frequently activated rows inside the DRAM or controller and refreshes their neighbors early. DDR5 adds refresh management (RFM), in which the controller counts activations and grants the DRAM extra time for such refreshes. Simply raising the refresh rate helps but costs bandwidth and power. Error-correcting code (ECC) can detect and fix single-bit flips, but several flips in one word can defeat common schemes. Research has shown that simple trackers can be bypassed by hammering many rows at once, so layered defenses are used.

Rowhammer vs. random soft errors

Soft errors from alpha particles or cosmic-ray neutrons are random in time and location, and ECC with scrubbing is designed around that statistical behavior. Rowhammer is different: it is induced by a specific access pattern, it tends to recur in the same vulnerable cells, and an attacker can aim it at chosen addresses. That repeatability is what makes it dangerous, and why defenses focus on detecting suspicious activation patterns rather than only correcting errors after they occur.

Rowhammer is the phenomenon in which activating a row many times in a short period causes electrical interference that drains charge from cells in neighboring rows, flipping bits. It could let unprivileged programs alter other processes' or kernel data. Countermeasures combine mechanisms that additionally refresh neighbors of heavily activated rows, higher refresh rates and ECC to detect and correct flips.

FAQ

Does ECC memory stop Rowhammer?

ECC makes attacks harder by correcting single-bit flips and detecting some multi-bit ones, but it is not a complete fix. Multiple flips in one word can escape correction, so ECC is combined with refresh-based mitigations.

What is TRR in DRAM?

Target row refresh is a mitigation that identifies rows activated unusually often and refreshes their neighboring rows before enough charge leaks to flip bits.

Is Rowhammer still a problem with DDR5?

DDR5 adds mechanisms such as refresh management to help, but cells keep shrinking and susceptibility generally rises with density. Mitigation is an ongoing effort shared between DRAM makers, controllers and system software.
