What is Segregation coefficient?

The ratio of impurity concentration in the crystal to that in the melt during solidification. For most impurities it is below 1, so they concentrate in the melt and the later part of a CZ crystal has higher impurity levels.

Japanese name: 偏析係数

Field: Silicon wafers

Definition and typical values

When a crystal solidifies from a melt, an impurity is incorporated at a concentration different from that in the liquid. The equilibrium segregation coefficient k0 is the ratio of concentration in the solid to concentration in the liquid at the growth interface. For dopants in silicon, boron is about 0.8, phosphorus about 0.35, arsenic about 0.3 and antimony about 0.023. Transition metals such as iron have coefficients many orders of magnitude below 1, so they are strongly rejected into the melt, which is why crystal growth itself purifies silicon.

Why the tail of a CZ ingot is more heavily doped

In Czochralski (CZ) growth, the rejected dopant accumulates in a shrinking volume of melt, so the melt concentration rises and each later slice of crystal is more heavily doped. Under ideal mixing this follows the normal-freezing (Scheil) relation, Cs = k C0 (1 − f)^(k − 1), where C0 is the initial melt concentration and f the fraction already solidified. With k near 0.8, boron gives a fairly flat resistivity along the ingot, while phosphorus and especially antimony vary much more. Only the length within the resistivity specification becomes product.

Effective versus equilibrium coefficient

The equilibrium value assumes the rejected impurity mixes instantly into the bulk melt. In reality a thin boundary layer forms at the interface, the impurity piles up in it, and the crystal incorporates more than k0 predicts. The resulting effective coefficient lies between k0 and 1 and rises with faster pulling and weaker melt stirring. Crystal and crucible rotation rates and pull speed therefore influence both the axial dopant profile and radial uniformity, and unsteady growth can leave periodic dopant variations known as striations.

Segregation at the Si/SiO2 interface

The same idea applies across the interface between silicon and its thermal oxide, where the coefficient m is usually defined as concentration in silicon divided by concentration in oxide. Boron has m of about 0.3, so the growing oxide takes it up and the silicon surface is depleted, an effect called pile-down. Phosphorus and arsenic, with m around 10, are rejected by the oxide and accumulate in the silicon just below the interface, called pile-up. Device designers account for these shifts when setting channel doping and threshold voltage.

  2. BArsenic (As)

Answer: C. Boron (B)

Adding group-III boron to the melt makes the crystal p-type. Boron's segregation coefficient of about 0.8, close to 1, keeps its concentration relatively uniform from top to bottom of the ingot, making it easy to work with. For n-type, group-V elements such as phosphorus, arsenic or antimony are used. Resistivity is set by the amount added, and IC wafers are commonly p-type at about 1–20 Ω·cm.

Answer: B. Because many dopants have segregation coefficients below 1, so dopants that are not readily incorporated become concentrated in the melt

During solidification most impurities are rejected into the melt (segregation coefficient k < 1): phosphorus has k ≈ 0.35, arsenic about 0.3 and antimony about 0.023. As growth proceeds the melt concentration rises, so later portions of the crystal are more heavily doped and lower in resistivity. Only the part of each ingot within the resistivity specification becomes product; boron, with little segregation, is easier to keep uniform.

FAQ

Why is boron used for p-type CZ silicon?

Boron is a group III acceptor with a segregation coefficient of about 0.8, close to 1, so its concentration stays relatively uniform from the top to the bottom of the ingot. That makes it easy to hit a resistivity target over most of the crystal.

What is the segregation coefficient of phosphorus in silicon?

It is about 0.35 for melt growth, so phosphorus concentrates in the melt and later portions of an n-type CZ ingot have noticeably lower resistivity than the seed end.

What is dopant pile-up during oxidation?

Dopants such as phosphorus and arsenic that prefer silicon over oxide are pushed ahead of the moving Si/SiO2 interface and accumulate in the silicon beneath it. Boron does the opposite and is depleted from the silicon surface.
