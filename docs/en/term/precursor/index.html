What is Precursor?

A source compound that reacts to form the desired film in CVD or ALD, such as TEOS or metal-organic compounds.

Japanese name: プリカーサ(前駆体)

Precursor appears in 15 practice questions across 4 topics, including Deposition (CVD and PVD), Epitaxial growth, Process materials (gases and chemicals).

Field: Process materials (gases and chemicals)

How a precursor becomes a film

In chemical vapor deposition (CVD), precursor vapor flows over a heated wafer, adsorbs on the surface and decomposes or reacts with a co-reactant, leaving a solid film while volatile byproducts are pumped away. Silane (SiH4) decomposes to silicon and hydrogen, tungsten hexafluoride (WF6) is reduced to tungsten with HF as a byproduct, and TEOS breaks down to SiO2. In atomic layer deposition (ALD) the precursor chemisorbs on surface sites until they are filled, the excess is purged, and a separate reactant such as water, ozone or ammonia completes the reaction, giving about 0.1 nm per cycle.

What makes a good precursor

A useful precursor must be volatile enough to deliver as vapor; liquids such as TEOS are vaporized by bubbling a carrier gas through them or by direct liquid injection. It must survive transport without decomposing in the gas phase, yet react readily at the surface. Its ligands should leave cleanly: chlorides such as HfCl4 are thermally stable around 300 °C but can leave chlorine in the film, while metal-organics such as TEMAHf react at lower temperature but can leave carbon. Safety matters too, which is why liquid TEOS is often preferred over pyrophoric silane for oxide.

Reaction-limited versus transport-limited growth

At low temperature the surface reaction is slow and sets the deposition rate, which then follows the Arrhenius law and depends exponentially on temperature, so wafer temperature uniformity controls thickness uniformity. Because precursor molecules can diffuse deep into trenches before reacting, this regime gives good step coverage. At high temperature the reaction is fast and the arrival rate of precursor limits growth, so gas-flow uniformity matters and trench openings receive more film and can pinch off. Large exposed areas consume more precursor and slow deposition, an effect known as loading.

Common precursors in a fab

Silicon films and epitaxy use silane, dichlorosilane (SiH2Cl2) and trichlorosilane (SiHCl3), usually diluted in hydrogen that also keeps the surface reduced and carries away chlorine as HCl. Oxide uses TEOS, tungsten plugs use WF6, and titanium nitride often uses TiCl4. High-k ALD uses HfCl4 or TEMAHf with H2O or O3, and Al2O3 uses trimethylaluminum (TMA) with water inside an ALD window of about 150 to 300 °C. Dopant gases such as phosphine and diborane are added for in-situ doping, and focused ion beam tools inject precursor gases to deposit local platinum or tungsten.

In epitaxial growth by CVD, the growth rate depends strongly on temperature in the relatively low-temperature regime. Why?

  1. ABecause the growth rate is determined only by the source gas supply
  2. BBecause the rate of the chemical reaction at the surface determines the growth rate (surface-reaction limited)
  3. CBecause the growth rate does not depend on temperature
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Because the rate of the chemical reaction at the surface determines the growth rate (surface-reaction limited)

At low temperature, surface reactions are slow and limit the growth rate (reaction-limited). Reaction rates follow the Arrhenius law, depending exponentially on temperature, so growth rate does too, and temperature uniformity across the wafer translates directly into thickness uniformity. At high temperature reactions are fast and the arrival rate of precursor gas limits growth (mass-transport-limited), making gas-flow uniformity critical.

In silicon epitaxial growth, what is the main role of the large flow of hydrogen (H2) supplied along with the source gas?

  1. AIt is the silicon source itself
  2. BIt is the n-type dopant
  3. CIt is a carrier gas for the source and provides a reducing atmosphere that keeps the surface clean
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. It is a carrier gas for the source and provides a reducing atmosphere that keeps the surface clean

In silicon epitaxy, precursors such as trichlorosilane are diluted in large flows of hydrogen. Hydrogen carries the precursor, participates as a reducing agent, maintains a reducing atmosphere that prevents surface oxidation, and removes chlorine as HCl. It also terminates dangling bonds on surface silicon atoms, keeping the surface clean.

FAQ

What is the difference between a precursor and a reactant in ALD?

The precursor carries the element that ends up in the film, such as the hafnium in HfCl4 or the aluminum in trimethylaluminum. The reactant, often water, ozone or ammonia, is dosed in a separate step to convert the adsorbed precursor layer into oxide or nitride and to restore reactive surface sites for the next cycle.

Why is TEOS often used instead of silane for silicon oxide CVD?

TEOS is a liquid at room temperature and is not pyrophoric, so it is safer to store and handle than silane. Its surface-driven decomposition also gives more conformal oxide with better step coverage in narrow gaps.

Is a carrier gas the same as a precursor?

No. A carrier gas such as hydrogen, nitrogen or argon only transports and dilutes the precursor vapor and does not supply atoms to the film, although hydrogen in silicon epitaxy also acts as a reducing agent that keeps the surface clean.
