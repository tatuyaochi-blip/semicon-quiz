What is Inversion layer?

A layer formed at the semiconductor surface by the gate voltage, where carriers of the type opposite to the substrate gather. It becomes the MOSFET's channel.

Japanese name: 反転層

Field: MOSFET

From accumulation to strong inversion

Consider an n-channel MOS structure on a p-type substrate. A negative gate voltage pulls holes to the surface (accumulation). A small positive voltage pushes holes away and leaves a depletion region of ionized acceptors. At larger positive voltage the bands bend so far that electrons, the minority carriers, gather at the surface. Strong inversion is conventionally defined as the point where the surface potential reaches twice the bulk Fermi potential, so the surface electron density equals the bulk hole density. The gate voltage at that point is the threshold voltage.

The inversion layer as the MOSFET channel

In a MOSFET the inversion layer connects the heavily doped source and drain, which supply its carriers almost instantly. Its sheet charge is roughly Cox·(VGS − Vth), so drain current grows with gate overdrive. An nMOS forms an electron inversion layer and conducts when VGS exceeds a positive Vth; a pMOS forms a hole inversion layer in an n-well and has a negative Vth, for example −0.4 V. Electrons have higher mobility than holes, so an nMOS of the same size drives more current.

What C-V measurements reveal

In a MOS capacitor without source and drain, inversion electrons must be generated thermally, which is slow. In a high-frequency C-V measurement, typically at 1 MHz, the inversion charge cannot follow the signal, so capacitance stays at its minimum: Cox in series with the depletion capacitance at maximum depletion width. In a low-frequency or quasi-static measurement the inversion layer responds and capacitance returns to Cox. The ratio of minimum to maximum capacitance gives the substrate doping.

Quantum confinement in thin-oxide devices

The inversion layer is only a few nanometers thick and the strong interface field confines carriers in a narrow potential well. Quantum mechanics places the peak of the electron distribution about 1 nm inside the silicon rather than at the interface. This centroid offset adds roughly 0.3–0.4 nm of equivalent SiO2 thickness, lowering gate capacitance and drive current. Together with polysilicon gate depletion, it makes the electrical thickness (CET) larger than the physical equivalent oxide thickness (EOT), which matters more as EOT shrinks.

FAQ

What is the difference between depletion and inversion in a MOS structure?

In depletion the gate has pushed majority carriers away, leaving fixed ionized dopants but few mobile carriers. In inversion the bending is strong enough that minority carriers gather at the surface in large numbers, forming a conductive layer of the opposite type.

Is the inversion layer the same as the channel?

In an enhancement-mode MOSFET, yes: the inversion layer formed under the gate is the conducting channel between source and drain. Below threshold it is too weak to carry significant current.

Why does high-frequency C-V stay at minimum in inversion?

Without a source and drain, inversion carriers come only from slow thermal generation, which cannot follow a signal around 1 MHz. Only the depletion edge responds, so the measured capacitance stays at its minimum.
