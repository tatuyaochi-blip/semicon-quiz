What is Stiction?

The sticking of tiny moving MEMS parts to the substrate through surface tension or surface adhesion, so they no longer move. It often happens during drying after sacrificial-layer removal.

Japanese name: スティクション

Stiction appears in 5 practice questions across 3 topics, including MEMS and sensors, Etching, Packaging and assembly.

Field: MEMS and sensors

Why small structures stick

Stiction is a consequence of scaling. Adhesive forces, such as capillary forces from liquid films, van der Waals attraction, electrostatic attraction from trapped charge and hydrogen bonding between hydrophilic surfaces, grow with contact area. The restoring force of a thin, compliant beam or membrane is comparatively weak, and the gap beneath a released MEMS structure is often only about a micrometre. Large, flat, smooth surfaces separated by such small gaps are therefore easy to pull into contact, and once they touch, adhesion can exceed the spring force that should pull them apart.

Release stiction during fabrication

The classic case occurs at release. A sacrificial layer, often SiO2, is etched away in liquid HF and rinsed. As the rinse liquid evaporates from the narrow gap, its meniscus pulls the structure toward the substrate, and the surfaces stay stuck after drying. The cure is to avoid a liquid-gas interface. Supercritical CO2 drying passes from liquid to gas without a meniscus, vapor-phase HF removes oxide without liquid, and XeF2 gas etches silicon sacrificial layers isotropically without plasma, with selectivity above 1000 to oxide, nitride and many metals.

In-use stiction and countermeasures

Stiction can also appear after a device ships. An accelerometer's proof mass may hit its stops during a shock, RF MEMS switch contacts close millions of times, and humidity or trapped charge can make a contact that once released stay stuck. Designers reduce contact area with small bumps (dimples) on the moving part, apply hydrophobic self-assembled monolayer coatings that lower surface energy, design stops with enough restoring force, and seal the device in a dry, hermetic package so moisture cannot build up capillary bridges.

Stiction vs. pull-in

Stiction is often confused with pull-in, an electrostatic instability. In a parallel-plate electrostatic actuator, once the movable electrode travels about one third of the gap, the electrostatic force grows faster than the spring force and the electrode snaps down; this happens even with perfectly clean, non-adhesive surfaces. Pull-in is reversible when the voltage is removed unless the surfaces then adhere, in which case the device has failed by stiction. Pull-in is set by voltage, gap and stiffness; stiction depends on surface forces.

When removing an SiO2 sacrificial layer to free a MEMS structure, which method is suitable for preventing stiction?

  1. ARinsing with large amounts of water and letting it dry naturally
  2. BRemoving it by polishing
  3. CVapor-phase etching with hydrogen fluoride (HF) vapor
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Vapor-phase etching with hydrogen fluoride (HF) vapor

Removing a sacrificial oxide with liquid HF can leave liquid trapped under the structure, whose surface tension pulls it down to stick during drying. Vapor-phase HF etching uses no liquid, so no such force arises; small amounts of water or alcohol vapor control the reaction, and temperature is managed so the water produced does not condense. Supercritical CO2 drying solves the same problem by eliminating the liquid-gas interface.

FAQ

Where does the word stiction come from?

Stiction is a blend of static friction. In MEMS it refers more broadly to any adhesion that holds a moving part against a surface so that it cannot move freely.

How is stiction prevented in MEMS?

During release, liquid-free or meniscus-free methods such as vapor HF, XeF2 etching and supercritical CO2 drying are used. During operation, dimples, hydrophobic anti-stiction coatings and dry hermetic packaging keep surfaces from sticking.

Why is stiction a bigger problem for MEMS than for larger machines?

Surface forces scale with area while inertial and restoring forces scale with volume and stiffness, so at micrometre scale surface forces dominate. In large machines the same forces are negligible compared with weight and spring forces.
