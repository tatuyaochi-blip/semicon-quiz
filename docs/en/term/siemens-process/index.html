What is Siemens process?

A method that reduces trichlorosilane with hydrogen to deposit high-purity polycrystalline silicon on heated silicon rods. It is the main way to make raw silicon for semiconductors.

Japanese name: シーメンス法

Siemens process appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Silicon wafers, Process materials (gases and chemicals).

Field: Silicon wafers

From metallurgical silicon to trichlorosilane

The starting material is metallurgical-grade silicon, about 98% pure, made by reducing quartz with carbon in an electric arc furnace. Reacting it with hydrogen chloride produces trichlorosilane (SiHCl₃), which is liquid at room temperature and boils at about 32 °C. Converting silicon to a volatile compound is the key step, because it allows purification by fractional distillation, separating out the chlorides of boron, phosphorus and metals. Boron and phosphorus are especially important to remove, since they act as dopants in the final silicon.

Inside the deposition reactor

Purified trichlorosilane is mixed with hydrogen and fed into a bell-jar reactor containing thin silicon rods, typically arranged as inverted U shapes. Electric current passed through the rods heats them to about 1100 °C, and trichlorosilane is reduced by hydrogen on their surfaces, depositing silicon and releasing hydrogen chloride. The rods thicken slowly over days, and the reactor walls are cooled so silicon deposits only on the rods. By-products such as silicon tetrachloride are converted back into trichlorosilane and recycled.

Purity and energy cost

The resulting polysilicon reaches about 11N, 99.999999999% purity, with total impurities around 0.01 parts per billion, because even trace metals shorten carrier lifetime and increase leakage. Solar-grade silicon tolerates roughly 6N to 9N. The process is very energy intensive: the rods must be held near 1100 °C for long periods while they radiate heat to the cooled walls. The rods are broken into chunks to charge Czochralski (CZ) crystal growth, or used whole as feed rods for float-zone (FZ) growth.

Siemens process vs. fluidized-bed reactors

The main alternative uses a fluidized-bed reactor, in which silane (SiH₄) or a chlorosilane decomposes onto small seed granules kept suspended by flowing gas. It runs continuously, uses considerably less energy and produces granular polysilicon that pours easily into crucibles. Its product has been used mostly for solar cells, because controlling contamination and surface quality is harder than with Siemens rods. For electronic-grade silicon, the Siemens process remains the standard route.

Answer: B. Siemens process (deposition by hydrogen reduction of trichlorosilane)

The Siemens process reacts metallurgical-grade silicon (about 98% pure) with hydrogen chloride to make trichlorosilane (SiHCl3, boiling point about 32 °C), purifies it by distillation, then reduces it with hydrogen onto silicon rods heated to about 1100 °C, depositing high-purity silicon. The resulting polysilicon, about 11N (99.999999999%) pure, feeds CZ growth. The process is very energy-intensive.

Answer: C. About 99.999999999% (11N)

Semiconductor-grade polysilicon must reach about eleven nines (11N), meaning total impurities of only around 0.01 ppb. Even trace metals shorten carrier lifetime and increase leakage. It is made by the Siemens process, in which trichlorosilane is reduced with hydrogen and deposited on heated rods. Solar-grade silicon can be 6N–9N; 99% or 99.99% is far from adequate for semiconductors.

FAQ

Why is silicon converted to trichlorosilane in the Siemens process?

Trichlorosilane is a volatile liquid that can be purified by distillation far more effectively than solid silicon can be refined. It is then reduced back to silicon in pure form.

What does 11N purity mean for polysilicon?

It means eleven nines, 99.999999999% silicon, leaving impurities at roughly 0.01 parts per billion. This level is required for semiconductor-grade crystal growth.

Why is the Siemens process so energy intensive?

Silicon rods must be kept around 1100 °C for days while deposition proceeds slowly, and much of that heat is lost by radiation to the cooled reactor walls.
