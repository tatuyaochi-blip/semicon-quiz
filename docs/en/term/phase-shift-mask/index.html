What is Phase-shift mask?

A photomask that shifts the phase of light passing through neighboring openings, using interference to improve image contrast and resolution.

Japanese name: 位相シフトマスク

Phase-shift mask appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Lithography.

Field: Lithography

Why shifting the phase improves resolution

An ordinary binary mask forms its pattern with a chrome absorber on a quartz substrate and builds the image only from areas that pass or block light. As patterns get finer, light passing through neighboring openings spreads by diffraction and overlaps, so light enters areas that should be dark and contrast falls. A phase-shift mask shifts the phase of light passing through part of the mask by 180°. Where light of opposite phase overlaps at a boundary, the amplitudes cancel, so dark areas become darker and pattern edges become sharper. This gives finer patterns or a wider process window at the same wavelength and NA.

Halftone (attenuated) type

The type now widely used is the halftone (attenuated) phase-shift mask, which replaces the absorber with a film that transmits a little light and shifts its phase by 180°. Molybdenum silicide (MoSi)-based materials are commonly used for the film, and for ArF exposure (193 nm) a transmittance of about 6% is typical. The small amount of light through the shifter overlaps in opposite phase with light spreading from the openings and cancels it, raising contrast at pattern edges. Because its structure is close to a binary mask and it can be applied to almost any pattern, it is widely used in ArF lithography. Care is needed because if the transmitted light is too strong, unwanted images (sidelobes) form in areas that should be dark.

Levenson (alternating) type

The Levenson (alternating) type alternately inverts the phase of light through neighboring openings, for example by etching into the quartz under one of each pair. Light cancels strongly between the two openings, giving higher contrast than the halftone type and favoring the formation of narrow lines. To produce a 180° phase difference, the difference in quartz thickness d traversed by the light is set from the wavelength λ and refractive index n as d = λ / (2(n − 1)). On the other hand, some layouts contain places where phases cannot be assigned alternately (phase conflicts), which may require double exposure with a second mask to erase unwanted images, complicating both design and mask making.

Relation to other resolution enhancement techniques

The phase-shift mask is one of the resolution enhancement techniques that lower the coefficient k1 in the resolution formula R = k1 × λ / NA. It is used together with off-axis illumination, which lights the mask from oblique angles, and OPC, which pre-corrects the mask for expected pattern distortion; together these have brought k1 in volume production down to about 0.27–0.3. The theoretical limit for resolving periodic lines and spaces in a single exposure is 0.25. EUV lithography uses reflective masks, so the ArF structure cannot be used, but phase-shift EUV masks that shift the phase with the absorber film are also under development.

Answer: A. To change the phase of light passing through adjacent openings and use interference to improve resolution and contrast

A phase-shift mask shifts the phase of light through certain regions by 180°, so amplitudes from neighboring features cancel at their boundaries, darkening dark regions and sharpening edge contrast. Types include alternating (Levenson) masks, which invert the phase of adjacent openings, and attenuated (halftone) masks, whose absorber is a slightly transmissive phase-shifting film such as MoSi; the latter are widely used in ArF lithography.

FAQ

What is a phase-shift mask, in simple terms?

A photomask that shifts the phase of light through part of the mask by 180° so that interference makes dark areas darker and raises contrast at pattern edges. It allows finer patterns to be formed stably with the same exposure tool.

What is the difference between the halftone and Levenson types?

The halftone type replaces the absorber with a film that transmits a little light and inverts its phase; it can be applied to almost any pattern and is widely used. The Levenson type alternately inverts the phase of neighboring openings; it gives higher contrast, but some layouts cannot be assigned phases, making design and mask making more difficult.

Why shift the phase by 180°?

Because light waves 180° out of phase have opposite amplitudes and cancel when they overlap. Making this happen at pattern edges creates dark regions where the intensity is nearly zero, sharpening the contrast between light and dark.
