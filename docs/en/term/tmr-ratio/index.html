What is TMR ratio?

In an MTJ, the difference between the resistances in the parallel and antiparallel states divided by the parallel-state resistance. The larger it is, the easier it is to tell 0 from 1; MgO barriers give large values.

Japanese name: TMR比

Field: Emerging memories

Definition and calculation

A magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) has two ferromagnetic layers separated by an insulating barrier about a nanometer thick. Its resistance is low when the magnetizations are parallel and high when they are antiparallel. The tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) ratio is (R_AP − R_P)/R_P: with R_P = 2 kΩ and R_AP = 5 kΩ it is 150%. Equivalently, R_AP/R_P = 1 + TMR, so 100–200% means the two states differ by only two to three times. An older pessimistic definition divides by R_AP instead and can never exceed 100%.

Why MgO barriers give high values

Early MTJs with amorphous aluminum-oxide barriers reached TMR ratios of tens of percent at room temperature. Crystalline magnesium oxide (MgO) barriers grown with matching cobalt-iron-boron (CoFeB) electrodes changed this: the ordered barrier preferentially transmits electron states of a particular symmetry, which are strongly spin-polarized in the electrodes, a process called coherent tunneling. After an anneal crystallizes the electrodes against the MgO, ratios of several hundred percent become possible, and values above 600% at room temperature have been reported in laboratory devices.

Why production values are lower

Memory products typically operate around 100–200%. Cells are tiny, so edge damage and variation matter, and the stack must survive back-end processing temperatures. The barrier must also be very thin to keep the resistance-area product low, because spin-transfer-torque (STT) writing drives current through it. TMR also falls as bias voltage rises, noticeably so at a few hundred millivolts, and it decreases with temperature. The ratio quoted for a material stack is therefore an upper bound on the signal a circuit actually sees.

Read margin and the TMR vs. GMR distinction

Reading compares the cell current against a reference set between the two states, using a current well below the write current so the read does not disturb the stored bit. Because the states differ by only two to three times and every cell's resistance varies, the margin is tighter than in DRAM or flash, and sense amplifiers and reference design are critical. Giant magnetoresistance (GMR), which uses a metallic spacer rather than a tunnel barrier, gives much smaller ratios and found its main use in hard-disk read heads.

Answer: A. A small current is passed through the MTJ and its high or low resistance is detected

MRAM is read by passing a current smaller than the write current through the MTJ and comparing against a reference to detect parallel (low resistance) or antiparallel (high resistance) states as a current or voltage difference. Even with a TMR ratio of 100–200%, the resistances differ by only 2–3×, giving smaller read margins than DRAM or flash. Reading does not flip the magnetization, so no write-back is needed.

FAQ

What is a typical TMR ratio in MRAM?

Production MRAM typically operates around 100–200%, meaning the high-resistance state is two to three times the low-resistance state. Laboratory MgO junctions have shown much higher values.

How do you calculate the TMR ratio?

Subtract the parallel-state resistance from the antiparallel-state resistance and divide by the parallel-state resistance. For 2 kΩ and 5 kΩ, (5 − 2)/2 = 150%.

What is the difference between TMR and GMR?

Both are resistance changes that depend on relative magnetization. TMR comes from spin-dependent tunneling through an insulating barrier and gives large ratios, while GMR uses a conducting metal spacer and gives smaller ratios.
