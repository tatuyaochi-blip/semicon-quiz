What is Conductivity modulation?

A large drop in the resistance of a lightly doped layer when many minority carriers are injected into it. PIN diodes and IGBTs use it to combine high breakdown voltage with low on-state voltage.

Japanese name: 伝導度変調

Conductivity modulation appears in 4 practice questions across 2 topics, including PN junctions and diodes, Power semiconductors.

Field: PN junctions and diodes

How conductivity modulation works

A high-voltage device needs a thick, lightly doped layer to block voltage, and in the off state that layer contains few free carriers and is highly resistive. When the device conducts in forward bias, the heavily doped p and n regions on either side inject holes and electrons into it. At high injection their densities far exceed the background doping and stay roughly equal to preserve charge neutrality. Conductivity is proportional to carrier density times mobility, so the layer's resistance falls by orders of magnitude, and the voltage across it depends only weakly on its thickness.

Why power devices rely on it

In a unipolar device such as a power MOSFET or Schottky diode, only the doping supplies carriers in the drift layer, and the ideal specific on-resistance of silicon rises roughly with the 2.5th power of the breakdown voltage. Bipolar devices escape this limit. A PIN diode floods its i-layer with carriers, and an IGBT adds a p+ layer on the collector side of a MOSFET-like structure so that injected holes modulate the drift layer while the MOS gate controls the electron current. This keeps on-state voltage low at ratings from about 600 V to 6.5 kV.

The cost: stored charge

The injected carriers do not vanish instantly when the device is told to turn off. A PIN diode conducts a reverse-recovery current while stored charge is swept out, and an IGBT shows a tail current as holes left in the drift layer recombine, because the MOS channel cannot extract them. Both add switching loss and limit the usable frequency. Carrier-lifetime control, for example by electron irradiation or heavy-metal diffusion, speeds switching but raises on-state voltage, a classic trade-off in power-device design.

Contrast and other uses

Silicon MOSFETs carry only majority carriers, so they show no conductivity modulation, switch fast and dominate below a few hundred volts. Wide-band-gap SiC MOSFETs reach high voltage with a thin drift layer instead, competing with IGBTs at mid-to-high voltage. The effect is also used in PIN diodes as RF switches and attenuators: forward current lowers the i-layer resistance roughly in proportion to the current, while reverse bias leaves a high-resistance, low-capacitance layer.

  1. AIt can amplify current
  2. BIts forward voltage is 0 V
  3. CThe i-layer acts as a wide depletion layer, making it suitable for high breakdown voltage and for fast, sensitive photodetectors
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The i-layer acts as a wide depletion layer, making it suitable for high breakdown voltage and for fast, sensitive photodetectors

In a PIN diode the whole lightly doped i layer acts as the depletion region. Because a wide depletion region can hold high voltage, it suits high-voltage rectifiers; because its absorption region is wide and its capacitance small, it suits fast, sensitive photodetectors. Under forward bias, conductivity modulation lowers the i-layer resistance, and under reverse bias it is highly resistive, so PIN diodes also serve as RF switches and variable attenuators. They do not amplify current.

What is the phenomenon in which forward current through a PIN diode injects many carriers into the i-layer and greatly reduces its resistance?

  1. AEarly effect
  2. BConductivity modulation
  3. CHall effect
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Conductivity modulation

The lightly doped i layer is normally highly resistive, but under forward current, large numbers of electrons and holes are injected from the p and n layers, far exceeding the doping and sharply lowering its resistance. This conductivity modulation lets a thick drift layer provide high blocking voltage while keeping on-voltage low. It is central to IGBTs and high-voltage diodes, but the stored carriers must be removed, which slows switching.

FAQ

Why don't power MOSFETs have conductivity modulation?

A MOSFET conducts only with majority carriers supplied by the drift-layer doping, and no minority carriers are injected. Its drift-layer resistance therefore stays high at high voltage ratings.

Why does conductivity modulation slow down switching?

The injected electrons and holes must be removed or recombine before the device can block voltage, which causes reverse-recovery current in diodes and tail current in IGBTs. This stored charge adds switching loss.

How does an IGBT achieve low on-voltage at high voltage?

Its p+ collector injects holes into the lightly doped drift layer, and together with electrons from the MOS channel they raise the carrier density far above the doping. This conductivity modulation lowers the drift-layer resistance.
