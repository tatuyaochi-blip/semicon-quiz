What is Bosch process (deep RIE)?

A dry-etching technique that alternates short etch and sidewall-passivation steps to cut deep, vertical holes and trenches in silicon. It is used for MEMS and TSVs.

Japanese name: ボッシュプロセス(Deep RIE)

How the Bosch etch cycle works

The Bosch process is a form of deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) that switches between two plasma steps every few seconds, usually in a high-density inductively coupled plasma (ICP) reactor. In the etch step, an SF6 plasma supplies fluorine radicals that etch silicon quickly but isotropically. In the passivation step, C4F8 deposits a thin fluorocarbon polymer over every exposed surface. When etching resumes, ions accelerated toward the wafer strip the polymer from the floor of the feature much faster than from the sidewalls, so the next etch step digs down while the walls stay protected.

Scallops, aspect ratio and other limits

Because each etch step briefly etches sideways before the next passivation, the sidewalls carry small periodic ripples called scallops. Shorter cycles give smoother walls at the cost of lower etch rate. Aspect ratios above 20 are achievable, but narrow features etch more slowly than wide ones because ions and radicals reach the bottom of deep holes less easily, an effect known as aspect-ratio-dependent etching or RIE lag. On silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers, charging at the buried oxide can also cause lateral notching at the bottom of the trench.

Bosch process vs. KOH wet etching

Bulk micromachining, which shapes the substrate itself rather than deposited films, has two main tools. Anisotropic wet etching in KOH follows crystal planes: on a (100) wafer it stops on slow-etching (111) planes, giving V-grooves and cavities with sidewalls at about 54.7°. The Bosch process is a dry etch whose profile is set by the plasma, not the crystal orientation, so it cuts vertical walls of any outline shape. That makes it the choice for dense comb drives, through-wafer holes and high-aspect-ratio trenches, whereas surface micromachining builds micrometer-thick structures from deposited films.

Where you will meet it

In MEMS, deep RIE defines the proof masses and comb fingers of accelerometers and gyroscopes, opens through-wafer holes and shapes membranes. In advanced packaging it etches the holes for through-silicon vias (TSVs), typically with aspect ratios around 10. The hole is then lined with an insulator, barrier and seed layer, filled with copper by plating, and exposed by thinning the wafer from the back. Scallops matter here: rough sidewalls make it harder to deposit a continuous liner, so TSV recipes often trade etch rate for smoother walls.

Answer: B. Alternating etching with deposition of a sidewall passivation film to form deep, vertical holes and trenches

The Bosch process alternates every few seconds between SF6-plasma silicon etching and C4F8 passivation deposition. Sidewalls stay protected while ions clear the film from the bottom, which keeps etching down, producing deep vertical features with aspect ratios above 20. Small periodic ripples (scallops) remain on the sidewalls. Developed by Bosch in the 1990s, it is used for MEMS and TSV fabrication.

FAQ

Why does the Bosch process leave scalloped sidewalls?

Each short SF6 etch step is partly isotropic, so it removes a little silicon sideways before the next passivation layer protects the wall. Repeating this cycle leaves a row of small ripples whose size shrinks with shorter etch steps.

What gases are used in the Bosch process?

SF6 is used for the silicon etch step and C4F8 for the fluorocarbon passivation step. The two are alternated every few seconds.

What is the difference between deep RIE and KOH etching?

KOH is a wet etch that follows crystal planes, so on (100) silicon it produces sloped walls at about 54.7°. Deep RIE is a dry plasma etch that gives vertical walls regardless of crystal orientation.
