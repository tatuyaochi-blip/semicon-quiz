What is KGD (known good die)?

A chip confirmed to be good before packaging. It matters when several chips go into one package, because a single bad chip ruins the whole module.

Japanese name: KGD(ノウングッドダイ)

Field: Testing and inspection

Why compound yield forces KGD

When several dies share one package, the package works only if every die works, so yields multiply. Eight dies that are each 99% good give 0.99⁸, about 92%, chance that all are good; four dies at 95% each give only about 81%. One bad die scraps the whole assembly, including expensive good dies such as a large processor or other HBM layers. Spending more on test before assembly is cheaper than discarding finished modules, which is why multi-chip modules, HBM and chiplet-based processors depend on known good dies.

Why wafer sort is not automatically enough

In a single-chip product, wafer probe screens out obvious failures and the package-level final test does the thorough job, including at-speed tests across temperature and often burn-in. A die is truly known good only after that. For multi-die assembly the thorough test must move before assembly, onto a bare die that is hard to contact: microbumps are tiny and fragile, probe cards have limited high-speed capability, and burn-in is difficult at wafer level. KGD therefore relies on higher-coverage wafer test, built-in self-test and design-for-test features, dedicated probe pads and sometimes wafer-level burn-in.

KGD for photonics and stacks

In co-packaged optics, silicon photonic chips are tested at wafer level before joining a costly switch IC. Fiber probes launch light through grating couplers from above the wafer while electrical probes contact the pads, so waveguide loss and modulator and photodetector performance can be measured automatically. For stacked memory the idea extends to a known good stack, a fully tested die stack, and built-in redundancy lets some defects be repaired after stacking rather than scrapping the part.

KGD does not replace final test

Assembly itself can introduce defects: cracked bumps, poor joints, warpage, contamination or damage from handling and bonding. A final test of the completed package is still indispensable, and its results feed back into deciding how much test effort to spend at the die level. The economic balance is set by die cost, the number of dies per package, defect rates at each stage and the cost of the test steps.

Answer: C. A chip (die) confirmed to be good before mounting

When multiple chips go into one package, a single bad die scraps the whole package, including the good dies. Stacking eight dies that are each 99% good gives only 0.99⁸ ≈ 92% chance that all are good. It is therefore essential to use thoroughly tested known good dies (KGD) before assembly, a point especially important for HBM and chiplets.

FAQ

What does known good die mean?

It is a bare die that has been tested thoroughly enough before assembly to be trusted as good, so it can be combined with other dies in one package without risking the whole assembly.

Why is KGD important for HBM and chiplets?

Package yield is the product of all die yields, so one failing die among many ruins the entire module and every good die in it. Screening dies before assembly keeps multi-die yield economical.

Is final test still needed if all dies are KGD?

Yes. Bonding and assembly can create new defects, so the finished package must still be tested.
