What is Necking?

Growing a thin neck just after the seed at the start of CZ pulling. It lets dislocations created by thermal shock when the seed touches the melt escape, giving a dislocation-free crystal.

Japanese name: ネッキング

Necking appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Silicon wafers.

Field: Silicon wafers

Why necking is done

When a silicon single crystal is pulled by the CZ (Czochralski) method, dipping the seed crystal into melt at the melting point (about 1414 °C) introduces many dislocations at the seed tip through thermal shock from the sudden temperature difference. If the crystal were grown wide right away, those dislocations would carry through the entire crystal. So at the start of pulling, the diameter is narrowed to about 3–5 mm. This is necking, and the thin section is called the neck. Because W. C. Dash reported the method in 1958, it is also called the Dash method or Dash necking, and it remains essential for producing today's dislocation-free silicon crystals.

Why a thin neck removes dislocations

Dislocations in silicon move mainly along {111} planes and extend at an angle to the growth direction. In a thin neck, these angled dislocations reach the side surface of the crystal within a short distance and exit. Once the dislocations are gone partway along the neck, growth can continue dislocation-free even after the diameter is widened. A dislocation-free crystal shows growth lines on its surface (four for a <100> crystal), and whether these lines continue unbroken is monitored as an indicator of dislocation-free growth. If the lines disappear, the crystal is judged to have lost its dislocation-free structure.

Where it fits in the pulling process

CZ pulling proceeds through seed dipping, necking, the shoulder (crown) that widens the diameter to the target, the body grown at constant diameter, and the tail (end cone), in which the diameter is gradually reduced before the crystal is separated from the melt. The tail is tapered because an abrupt separation would introduce dislocations by thermal shock, which could propagate back up into the dislocation-free body. If dislocation-free growth is lost partway through the body, the rest cannot be used for wafers, and the crystal may be remelted and the pull restarted.

Challenges at large diameters, and another meaning

A 300 mm ingot can weigh hundreds of kilograms, yet it hangs from a neck only a few millimeters in diameter while being pulled. As diameters and weights grow further, neck strength approaches its limit, so researchers have explored seeds heavily doped with impurities, which resist thermal-shock dislocations and allow a dislocation-free thick neck, as well as mechanically supporting the crystal. The FZ (float zone) method also forms a neck after the seed to eliminate dislocations. Note that in materials mechanics, necking means something different: the local narrowing of a specimen during a tensile test.

Answer: C. To let dislocations introduced by thermal shock when the seed touches the melt escape, giving a dislocation-free crystal

Dipping the seed into the hot melt introduces dislocations from thermal shock. Narrowing the growth to about 3–5 mm in diameter lets them, propagating at an angle to the growth axis, run out to the surface, after which a dislocation-free crystal can be expanded to full diameter (Dash necking, 1958). Even 300 mm ingots weighing hundreds of kg hang from this thin neck while being pulled.

FAQ

What is necking?

It is the step at the start of single-crystal pulling in which the crystal just after the seed is grown narrowed to a few millimeters in diameter. It lets dislocations introduced by thermal shock when the seed touches the melt escape from the crystal, so that a dislocation-free crystal can then be grown.

What is Dash necking?

It is the method shown by W. C. Dash in 1958 for obtaining dislocation-free silicon single crystals by removing dislocations with a thin neck. It is still used as a basic step in single-crystal production by the CZ and FZ methods.

What happens if the neck is too thin?

Dislocations escape more easily, but the neck's strength to support the crystal's weight drops, and it may be unable to hold a heavy ingot. The diameter is therefore chosen to be thin enough to remove dislocations yet thick enough to bear the weight.
