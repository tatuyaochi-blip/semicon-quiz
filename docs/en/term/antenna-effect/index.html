What is Antenna effect?

Damage to a gate oxide when a connected conductor, such as a long wire, collects charge like an antenna during plasma processing. Design rules limit the area ratio of such wires to prevent it.

Japanese name: アンテナ効果

Antenna effect appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Etching, IC design flow and EDA.

Field: Etching

How the antenna effect works

In processes such as plasma etching, ashing and plasma CVD, ions and electrons flow onto the wafer surface. At that stage, wires and gate electrodes not yet connected to upper layers act as electrically floating conductors that collect charge. If such a conductor is connected to a gate, the only escape path for the collected charge is the thin gate oxide, through which it flows as tunneling current. The larger the conductor's area, the more charge it collects, and because it behaves like an antenna, this is called the antenna effect. Charge builds up more readily when the plasma is non-uniform across the wafer, and the phenomenon is also called plasma charging damage.

Damage to the gate oxide

Current through the gate oxide creates charge traps in the film and increases interface states. As a result, the threshold voltage shifts, mobility drops or gate leakage increases. With enough charge, the oxide can break down on the spot. Even when nothing looks abnormal in tests right after fabrication, latent damage may remain that shortens lifetime against TDDB, NBTI and hot carriers, making it a reliability concern. The damage grows with the ratio of conductor area to gate area (the antenna ratio).

Design countermeasures: antenna rules

Designs must obey antenna rules, which cap the ratio of the area of wiring connected to a gate to the gate area. Because wiring is built layer by layer from the bottom, the check uses the conductor area connected to the gate at the time each layer is processed, limiting the ratio per metal layer or cumulatively from the lower layers. Violations are fixed by hopping up to a higher layer partway along the wire to split the antenna, or by placing a protection diode near the gate to drain charge to the substrate. Layout verification tools detect violations, and place-and-route tools fix them automatically.

Process countermeasures and evaluation

On the process side, damage is reduced by improving plasma uniformity across the wafer to reduce uneven charging and by suppressing charging with pulsed plasma or low bias. To evaluate a process, test structures with large antenna ratios are placed on the wafer, and gate leakage and threshold-voltage shifts are measured. Protection diodes are reverse-biased in normal operation and pass almost no current, but they add a little junction capacitance and leakage, so they are inserted only where needed. Because the antenna effect can occur not only in etching but also in plasma deposition and ashing, those process conditions are evaluated as well.

Answer: C. Antenna effect (plasma charging damage)

Conductors exposed to plasma collect charge, and when it flows as tunneling current through the thin oxide of a connected gate, the oxide degrades or breaks down. Larger conductor area (the antenna) collects more charge, so layouts follow antenna rules limiting the ratio of conductor area to gate area; violations are fixed with protection diodes that bleed off charge or by changing metal layers.

  2. BClock gating
  3. CAntenna rules

Answer: A. OCV (on-chip variation) derating

Even within one chip, threshold voltage, wire dimensions, supply voltage and temperature vary slightly by location, so delays vary. OCV applies separate derating factors to data and clock paths, for example making data slower and the capture clock faster in setup analysis, to cover the worst combination. Refined methods are also used: AOCV accounts for path depth and distance, and POCV models each cell's delay variation statistically. Antenna rules prevent gate damage during processing.

FAQ

What is the antenna effect, in simple terms?

It is damage to the gate oxide that occurs in plasma processes when a conductor connected to a gate, such as a wire, collects charge that then flows through the thin gate oxide. The larger the conductor, the greater the effect, and the name comes from the way it collects charge like an antenna.

What are antenna rules?

Design rules that cap the ratio of the area of wiring connected to a gate to the gate area (the antenna ratio). Violations are fixed by hopping the wire up to a higher layer to split it, or by adding a protection diode that drains the charge.

Why does a protection diode help?

With a diode to the substrate attached to the wire leading to the gate, charge collected during processing flows through the diode to the substrate instead of through the gate oxide. In normal operation the diode is reverse-biased and passes almost no current, so its effect on circuit operation is kept small.
