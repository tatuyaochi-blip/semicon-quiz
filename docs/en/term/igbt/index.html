What is IGBT?

Insulated-gate bipolar transistor. Driven by a MOS gate, it achieves a low on-state voltage even at high voltage ratings through conductivity modulation.

Japanese name: IGBT

Field: Power semiconductors

Structure and conduction

An insulated-gate bipolar transistor (IGBT) looks like a vertical power MOSFET with an extra p+ layer at the back, the collector. The gate forms a MOS channel that supplies electrons into the lightly doped n-drift region, and these act as base current for an internal pnp transistor, so the p+ collector injects holes. The drift region floods with both carriers, and this conductivity modulation sharply lowers its resistance. The price is a junction in the conduction path, which gives the on-state voltage a knee of roughly 0.7 V before current rises.

IGBT vs. power MOSFET

A silicon MOSFET conducts with majority carriers only, so its drift region must be thicker and more lightly doped as voltage rating rises, and on-resistance increases steeply with breakdown voltage. That keeps silicon MOSFETs mostly below a few hundred volts. The IGBT's conductivity modulation largely breaks this link, so it dominates silicon devices from about 600 V up to 6.5 kV, in electric vehicle inverters, rail traction and industrial drives. At low current, though, the IGBT's knee voltage makes a MOSFET more efficient, and silicon carbide MOSFETs now compete in the 650 to 1700 V range.

Switching behaviour

Turn-on is quick, but at turn-off the stored minority carriers must be removed, producing tail current while the voltage is already high. This turn-off energy limits silicon IGBTs to switching frequencies of tens of kHz or less. Device design trades on-state voltage against turn-off loss: more stored charge means lower conduction loss but a longer tail. Trench gates and field-stop layers improve this trade-off. Unlike a MOSFET, an IGBT has no internal body diode for reverse current, so inverter modules pair each IGBT with an antiparallel freewheeling diode.

Driving and protecting it

Because the gate is insulated, an IGBT is voltage-driven, typically switched on with about +15 V and held off at zero or a negative voltage for noise immunity, although the gate capacitance still requires a driver able to supply peak currents. Silicon IGBTs typically survive a short circuit for about 10 µs, so drivers watch for desaturation, a rise in collector voltage during conduction, and switch off within that time. The device also contains a parasitic pnpn thyristor, whose latch-up modern cell designs suppress.

  2. BA device driven by a MOS gate that achieves a low on-state voltage through conductivity modulation from bipolar operation
  3. CA thyristor turned on by light
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A device driven by a MOS gate that achieves a low on-state voltage through conductivity modulation from bipolar operation

An IGBT combines a MOSFET input with a bipolar output, adding a p+ layer on the drain side of a MOSFET-like structure. It is voltage-driven, simplifying gate drive, and when on, holes injected from the p+ layer sharply reduce drift-layer resistance (conductivity modulation), keeping on-voltage low even at 600 V to 6.5 kV ratings. It is the workhorse of EV, rail and industrial inverters, though tail current makes it unsuitable for high frequencies.

At IGBT turn-off, what is the phenomenon called in which the current does not drop to zero immediately but trails off because of stored minority carriers?

  1. AInrush current
  2. BTail current
  3. CDark current
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Tail current

An IGBT lowers drift resistance by injecting minority carriers (holes) when on, so at turn-off current continues until they recombine or are swept out—the tail current. Because it flows while the voltage is already high, it is a major source of turn-off loss and limits IGBT switching frequency to tens of kHz or less. Lifetime control and field-stop structures shorten it. MOSFETs have no tail current.

FAQ

What is the difference between an IGBT and a MOSFET?

Both have an insulated MOS gate, but an IGBT adds bipolar conduction through hole injection, giving lower on-state voltage at high voltage ratings. MOSFETs switch faster and have no tail current, so they dominate at lower voltages and higher frequencies.

Why can't IGBTs switch at high frequency?

Minority carriers stored in the drift region during conduction must be removed at turn-off, producing a tail current while voltage is high. The resulting turn-off energy grows with switching frequency, which practically limits silicon IGBTs to tens of kHz or less.

Why does an IGBT need an antiparallel diode?

An IGBT cannot conduct in the reverse direction like a MOSFET body diode, but inductive loads in inverters need a path for freewheeling current. A separate fast diode is therefore placed across each IGBT.
