What is Backside illumination (BSI)?

An image sensor structure in which light enters from the back of the substrate, where there is no wiring. Light is not blocked by wiring, so sensitivity stays high even with small pixels.

Japanese name: 裏面照射型(BSI)

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Front-side vs. backside illumination

In a front-side illuminated (FSI) CMOS image sensor, light enters from the side where transistors and wiring are built, passing through the microlens, color filter and several metal layers before reaching the photodiode. The metal blocks and reflects part of the light, and the tall optical stack loses rays arriving at oblique angles. In a backside-illuminated (BSI) sensor the wafer is flipped after front-side processing, bonded to a support wafer, and its original substrate thinned to a few µm, so light enters from the back and reaches the photodiode without crossing any wiring.

Why BSI matters for small pixels

As pixels shrink toward about 1 µm, the openings between metal lines in an FSI pixel become a small fraction of the pixel area and the stack becomes tall relative to pixel width, so sensitivity and angular response drop sharply. BSI keeps nearly the whole pixel area open to light and shortens the optical path from microlens to silicon. It also frees the wiring from optical constraints, allowing more metal layers. These advantages made BSI the standard structure for smartphone camera sensors.

Manufacturing challenges

Thinning must leave a uniform silicon layer a few µm thick across the wafer. The new back surface must be passivated, because defects there generate dark current; films with fixed negative charge are commonly used to accumulate holes at that surface. Thin silicon also makes optical and electrical crosstalk between neighboring pixels more likely, which deep trench isolation between pixels reduces. Red and near-infrared light has an absorption length of several µm or more in silicon, so very thin layers lose some long-wavelength response.

From BSI to stacked sensors

Because a BSI sensor already needs a bonded support wafer, that wafer can carry useful circuitry instead of being blank silicon. Stacked image sensors bond the pixel wafer to a logic wafer containing readout, analog-to-digital conversion and image processing, each made in a process suited to its function. This allows smaller chips or more processing per pixel area, and it builds directly on the wafer bonding and thinning techniques developed for BSI.

Answer: B. Light reaches the photodiode without passing through the wiring layers, making it easier to increase sensitivity

In front-side illumination, light must pass through gaps in several metal layers above the photodiode, where it is blocked or reflected. Back-side illumination thins the wafer to a few µm and admits light from the side opposite the wiring, collecting it efficiently even at oblique angles. Commercialized by Sony in 2009, it preserves sensitivity in pixels around 1 µm and became standard in smartphone cameras.

FAQ

What is the difference between BSI and FSI image sensors?

In FSI, light passes through the wiring layers before reaching the photodiode; in BSI, the wafer is flipped and thinned so light enters from the back, where there is no wiring. BSI therefore collects more light, especially in small pixels.

Why must the wafer be thinned in a BSI sensor?

Light enters from the back, so it must reach the photodiodes near the front side before being absorbed. Thinning the silicon to a few µm puts the photodiodes within the light's absorption depth.

Do BSI sensors have any disadvantages?

They need extra steps such as wafer bonding, thinning and backside passivation, and the thin silicon increases crosstalk risk and reduces long-wavelength response. Deep trench isolation and careful surface treatment address these.
