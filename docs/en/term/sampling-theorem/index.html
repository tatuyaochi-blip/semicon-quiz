What is Sampling theorem?

The theorem stating that a signal can be reconstructed if it is sampled at a frequency higher than twice its highest frequency component. Also called the Nyquist-Shannon theorem.

Japanese name: サンプリング定理

Field: A/D and D/A conversion

Why twice the bandwidth is enough

Sampling a signal at rate fs makes its spectrum repeat at every multiple of fs. If the signal contains nothing at or above fs/2, the Nyquist frequency, these copies do not overlap, and an ideal low-pass filter can isolate the original spectrum and rebuild the continuous waveform exactly, mathematically by sinc interpolation between samples. The minimum rate, twice the highest frequency, is called the Nyquist rate. Sampling exactly at twice the frequency is not sufficient: a sine at fs/2 could, for example, be sampled at its zero crossings and vanish entirely.

Aliasing: what happens when it is violated

A component above fs/2 does not disappear; it folds back and appears at a lower frequency equal to its distance from the nearest multiple of fs. With fs = 10 kHz, a 7 kHz tone produces exactly the same samples as a 3 kHz tone. Once sampled, the two are indistinguishable, so aliasing cannot be removed afterward by any digital processing. The only cure is prevention: an analog anti-aliasing low-pass filter placed before the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) attenuates everything above the band of interest, including noise, not just signal tones.

Practical margins and oversampling

Real filters need a transition band between passband and stopband, so systems sample well above the bare minimum. Audio CDs use 44.1 kHz for a 20 kHz band, leaving 20 to 22.05 kHz for the filter's roll-off; general-purpose designs often use only about 40% of the sampling rate. Oversampling, sampling many times faster than needed and then filtering and decimating digitally, relaxes the analog filter dramatically and is the basis of delta-sigma converters. On the output side, a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) needs a reconstruction filter to remove spectral images.

Bandpass sampling and other subtleties

Strictly, what must be below fs/2 is the signal's bandwidth, not its highest frequency. A narrowband radio signal occupying, say, a few MHz around a much higher carrier can be sampled below twice the carrier frequency, deliberately aliasing it down to a lower frequency, provided the band fits entirely within one Nyquist zone. This undersampling requires a sample-and-hold whose analog input bandwidth covers the carrier and a bandpass filter to reject other zones. Note also that sampling discretizes time only; rounding each sample to discrete levels is quantization, a separate step.

What sampling frequency is needed to sample an analog signal containing components up to 20 kHz without aliasing?

  1. A10 kHz or more
  2. BExactly 20 kHz
  3. CA frequency higher than 40 kHz

Answer: C. A frequency higher than 40 kHz

The sampling theorem says a signal can be reconstructed if sampled faster than twice its highest frequency, so 20 kHz content requires more than 40 kHz. Since filters cannot cut off instantly, practical systems add margin: audio CDs sample at 44.1 kHz, using 20–22.05 kHz as the filter's transition band. Half the sampling frequency is called the Nyquist frequency.

What does 'MS/s (MSPS)', used to express the conversion speed of an ADC, mean?

  1. AMegasiemens per second
  2. BHow many million times per second it can sample (convert)
  3. CThe number of milliseconds per conversion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. How many million times per second it can sample (convert)

MS/s stands for mega samples per second—how many million conversions per second; 100 MS/s means 100 million per second. By the sampling theorem the usable bandwidth is at most half (50 MHz), though about 40% is typical after filter margin. kS/s and GS/s are also used. Data rate is sampling rate × bits, so 100 MS/s at 12 bits produces 1.2 Gb/s.

FAQ

What is the Nyquist frequency?

It is half the sampling rate, the highest frequency that can be represented without aliasing. For 48 kHz sampling it is 24 kHz.

Why is audio sampled at 44.1 kHz instead of 40 kHz?

The extra margin above twice the 20 kHz audio band gives the anti-aliasing and reconstruction filters a transition band in which to roll off.

Can aliasing be removed after sampling?

No. Once a high-frequency component has folded into the band, its samples are identical to those of a genuine lower-frequency signal, so it must be filtered out before sampling.
