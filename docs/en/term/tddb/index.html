What is TDDB (time-dependent dielectric breakdown)?

A phenomenon in which an insulating film under sustained voltage eventually breaks down even below its breakdown voltage. It is used to evaluate the lifetime of gate oxides and the like.

Japanese name: TDDB(経時絶縁破壊)

Field: Reliability

How breakdown develops over time

Time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) is a wear-out failure. A field too weak to break an insulator immediately still drives carriers through it, and over time these create defects (traps) in the film. The defects accumulate at random positions until a chain of them bridges the two electrodes, at which point current runs away and the film breaks down. A thinner film needs fewer defects to complete such a path, and it also sees a higher field at a given voltage, so scaling makes TDDB harder to manage. Breakdown shorts the gate to the channel and destroys the transistor.

Typical numbers

High-quality thermal SiO2 breaks down instantly at about 10 MV/cm, so a 10 nm film fails near 10 V and a 2 nm film at only a few volts. To survive the usual ten-year life target, devices are designed to run at a few MV/cm or less. In very thin gate oxides, tunneling leakage may become the limiting issue before breakdown does. Between interconnects, about 0.7 V across a spacing of ten-odd nanometers already produces fields of hundreds of kV/cm, enough to matter over years.

Accelerated tests and lifetime models

At use conditions TDDB takes years, so test structures are stressed at elevated voltage and temperature until they fail in hours to weeks. Times-to-failure are fitted statistically, commonly with a Weibull distribution, and extrapolated to the use field. The E model (lifetime ∝ exp(−γE)) and the 1/E model (lifetime ∝ exp(G/E)) can differ by orders of magnitude after a long extrapolation; the E model predicts shorter low-field lives and is often chosen as the conservative option. Power-law voltage models have also been proposed for ultrathin films, and which fits best depends on the dielectric and field range.

Gate TDDB vs. BEOL TDDB and other mechanisms

Gate TDDB concerns the transistor gate dielectric. BEOL (back-end-of-line) TDDB concerns the insulator between neighboring wires, and grows more important as spacing shrinks: low-k films are less robust than oxide, copper ions can migrate into the dielectric, and post-CMP interfaces are often weak, while via misalignment locally narrows spacing. TDDB differs from ESD (electrostatic discharge) damage, which is instantaneous overstress, from NBTI (negative-bias temperature instability), which shifts threshold voltage rather than breaking the film, and from electromigration, which is atom transport in metal lines.

Low-k films are less robust than conventional oxide, and copper-ion migration and post-CMP interface damage also contribute. Answer: C. A phenomenon in which a gate dielectric under a continuously applied electric field breaks down after a certain time

TDDB (time-dependent dielectric breakdown) occurs when a field too weak to cause immediate breakdown, applied for a long time, gradually creates defects in the dielectric until they link up and bridge the electrodes, causing breakdown. Thinner gate dielectrics see higher fields, making it a key reliability concern; it also affects low-k dielectrics between wires. Atom migration in wires is electromigration, and solder fatigue is a temperature-cycling failure.

FAQ

What is the difference between TDDB and dielectric breakdown voltage?

Breakdown voltage describes immediate failure when the field exceeds the insulator's strength, about 10 MV/cm for good SiO2. TDDB is failure after a long time at a lower field, caused by gradual defect build-up.

Why are TDDB tests done at higher voltage than operating voltage?

At operating voltage failures would take years. Higher voltage and temperature make them happen in hours to weeks, and a voltage-acceleration model extrapolates the result back to use conditions.

What is the E model vs. 1/E model in TDDB?

They are two ways of extrapolating lifetime versus field: exponential in E or exponential in 1/E. At low fields the E model predicts much shorter lifetimes, so it is usually treated as the conservative choice.
