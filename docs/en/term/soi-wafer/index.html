What is SOI wafer?

A wafer with a thin silicon layer on a buried oxide (BOX) layer over a silicon substrate. It is made, for example, by bonding a hydrogen-implanted wafer and splitting it. Its low parasitic capacitance suits FD-SOI devices and MEMS.

Field: Silicon wafers

Layer structure

A silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer stacks three layers: a thin single-crystal device layer on top, a buried oxide (BOX) layer of silicon dioxide beneath it, and a thick handle wafer that provides mechanical support. Thicknesses vary widely by application. Fully depleted SOI (FD-SOI) transistors use a device layer of roughly ten nanometers on a thin BOX, silicon photonics commonly uses about 220 nm of silicon on a BOX around 2 µm thick, and micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) may use device layers several micrometers thick or more.

How SOI wafers are made

The Smart Cut process dominates for thin layers. A donor wafer is thermally oxidized and implanted with hydrogen ions to a depth of a few hundred nanometers, then bonded to a handle wafer. Annealing at a few hundred degrees Celsius makes the hydrogen form microcavities that link up and split the donor at the implant depth, leaving a thin silicon film on oxide. Chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) and annealing finish the surface, and the donor can be reused. Older methods include oxygen implantation (SIMOX) and bonding followed by grinding and etch-back.

Why devices benefit

The buried oxide isolates each transistor from the substrate, cutting junction capacitance and leakage and eliminating the latch-up paths of bulk CMOS. In FD-SOI the channel is thin enough to be fully depleted, improving electrostatic control, and a voltage applied beneath the thin BOX acts as a back gate to tune threshold voltage. In silicon photonics, the large refractive-index contrast between silicon and oxide confines light in compact waveguides, and InP laser material can be bonded onto the wafer for heterogeneous integration. MEMS processes use the BOX as an etch stop.

Drawbacks and contrasts with bulk silicon

SOI wafers cost more than bulk wafers, which limits them to applications where the benefits pay off. Silicon dioxide conducts heat roughly a hundred times worse than silicon, so transistors on SOI suffer self-heating. In partially depleted SOI, where the body is thicker than the depletion region, the undepleted body floats electrically and causes history-dependent effects, including the so-called kink in output characteristics. FD-SOI avoids most of the floating-body problem but needs very tight control of device-layer thickness, since it directly affects threshold voltage.

Among methods of incorporating lasers into silicon photonic circuits, which correctly describes heterogeneous integration?

  1. ASilicon waveguides are heavily doped to turn silicon itself into a direct-gap material that emits light
  2. BInP-based compound semiconductor crystal layers are bonded to an SOI wafer and then processed, and the laser light is transferred into silicon waveguides
  3. CInP crystal is epitaxially grown directly on silicon, with no concern about defects from lattice-constant mismatch
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. InP-based compound semiconductor crystal layers are bonded to an SOI wafer and then processed, and the laser light is transferred into silicon waveguides

Silicon is an indirect-gap material and cannot emit light efficiently, so compound semiconductors are used for lasers. In heterogeneous integration, crystals (wafers or dies) with InP-based light-emitting layers are bonded to an SOI wafer, the InP substrate is removed, and lasers are formed with semiconductor processes. Because lithography aligns them with the silicon waveguides, many lasers can be integrated with higher accuracy than placing components one by one, and the light is transferred into the silicon waveguides through tapers or similar structures. Doping does not change the indirect-gap nature, and InP and Si differ in lattice constant by about 8%, so defects are a problem in direct growth.

  3. CA wafer implanted with hydrogen ions is bonded to another wafer and split at the implant depth, leaving a thin silicon layer

Answer: C. A wafer implanted with hydrogen ions is bonded to another wafer and split at the implant depth, leaving a thin silicon layer

In the Smart Cut process, a wafer is thermally oxidized and implanted with hydrogen ions to a depth of a few hundred nm, then bonded to a handle wafer. Annealing at a few hundred °C causes the hydrogen to form microcavities that link up, splitting the wafer at that depth and leaving a thin single-crystal silicon layer on oxide—an SOI wafer. The surface is finished by CMP to nanometer-level thickness control, and the remainder of the source wafer can be repolished and reused.

FAQ

What is the BOX layer in an SOI wafer?

BOX stands for buried oxide, the silicon dioxide layer between the thin device silicon and the handle wafer. It electrically isolates devices from the substrate and serves as optical cladding in silicon photonics.

What is the Smart Cut process?

It is a layer-transfer method in which hydrogen is implanted into an oxidized wafer, the wafer is bonded to a handle, and annealing splits it at the implant depth to leave a thin silicon film on oxide.

Why is SOI used for silicon photonics?

The buried oxide has a much lower refractive index than silicon, so the top silicon layer can act as a tightly confining waveguide core that is compatible with standard fabrication tools.
