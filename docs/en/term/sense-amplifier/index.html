What is Sense amplifier?

A circuit that amplifies the tiny voltage difference read from a memory cell and decides whether it is 0 or 1.

Japanese name: センスアンプ

Sense amplifier appears in 5 practice questions across 2 topics, including DRAM, SRAM and flash memory.

Field: DRAM

Why memories need sense amplifiers

Memory cells are made as small as possible, so they cannot drive long bit lines quickly. In DRAM, opening a cell shares its charge with a bit line whose capacitance is several times larger, producing a swing of only about (VDD/2)·Cs/(Cs + Cb), tens to about a hundred millivolts. In SRAM the cell can discharge its precharged bit line only slowly. A sense amplifier detects this small difference and turns it into a full logic level, so the memory does not have to wait for the bit line to swing all the way.

How a latch-type sense amplifier works

The common form is two cross-coupled inverters connected across a pair of bit lines, one carrying the cell signal and the other a reference. Both lines are first precharged and equalized. When the sense amplifier is enabled, whichever side is slightly higher pulls the other down, and positive feedback regenerates the small difference to the full supply rails in a short time. Timing matters: firing too early, before enough signal develops, risks a wrong decision, while firing too late wastes access time.

DRAM read, restore and precharge

DRAM bit lines are precharged to VDD/2 so a stored 1 or 0 moves them up or down by the same amount. Because charge sharing destroys the stored value, the sense amplifier that resolves the bit line also drives it to full level and rewrites the cell, so activating a row automatically refreshes it. Closing the row and returning bit lines to VDD/2 is precharge, timed by tRP. Folded bit-line arrays place both lines of a pair side by side so coupled noise cancels; open bit-line arrays split them to permit smaller 6F² cells.

Offset and other memory types

Random threshold-voltage mismatch between the latch transistors gives every sense amplifier an input offset, which must stay well below the available signal; this limits how many cells can share a bit line and motivates offset-compensation schemes. Single-ended memories such as flash compare the cell current against a reference current or reference cell, and multi-level cells need several comparisons per read. The same regenerative latch idea appears in the clocked comparators of analog-to-digital converters.

  1. AReading does not change the cell's charge at all
  2. BReading changes the cell's charge, so the data is amplified by a sense amplifier and written back to the cell
  3. CReading uses tunneling current at high voltage
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Reading changes the cell's charge, so the data is amplified by a sense amplifier and written back to the cell

Connecting the cell capacitor to the bit line shares its charge with the bit line's large parasitic capacitance, destroying the original state (destructive read). The sense amplifier therefore amplifies a difference of tens to about a hundred mV to decide 0 or 1 and simultaneously writes the amplified level back into the cell. Because this write-back effectively refreshes the cell, refresh is performed simply by activating and closing a row.

Why is the voltage change on the bit line very small when DRAM is read?

  1. ABecause the supply voltage is very high
  2. BBecause the bit line resistance is zero
  3. CBecause the cell capacitor's capacitance is much smaller than the bit line's parasitic capacitance
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Because the cell capacitor's capacitance is much smaller than the bit line's parasitic capacitance

On readout, the cell's charge is shared with the bit line. The cell capacitance Cs (about 10 fF) is much smaller than the bit-line capacitance Cb (several to about ten times larger), so the swing is only about (VDD/2)·Cs/(Cs + Cb), tens to about a hundred mV. To preserve this transfer ratio, the number of cells per bit line is limited to keep Cb small. The sense amplifier then rapidly amplifies the tiny difference with positive feedback to decide 0 or 1.

FAQ

Why does DRAM need a sense amplifier?

The cell capacitor is much smaller than the bit line capacitance, so reading produces only tens of millivolts of change. The sense amplifier amplifies this to a full logic level and also rewrites the cell, because the read destroys its charge.

Why are DRAM bit lines precharged to VDD/2?

Starting halfway lets a stored 1 raise the bit line and a stored 0 lower it by similar amounts, so the sense amplifier compares the line against an equal reference. It also halves the bit line swing, saving power.

What is the difference between DRAM and SRAM sense amplifiers?

Both use regenerative latches, but DRAM sense amplifiers also restore the destroyed cell data and work from a VDD/2 precharge. SRAM sense amplifiers only need to detect a small differential on bit lines precharged near VDD to speed up reads.
