What is Latch-up?

A condition in which a parasitic thyristor structure in a CMOS circuit turns on and a large current keeps flowing between supply and ground. It is prevented by well-contact placement, guard rings and similar measures.

Japanese name: ラッチアップ

Latch-up appears in 11 practice questions across 8 topics, including Reliability, Automotive Semiconductors and Functional Safety, Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA).

Field: Reliability

The parasitic thyristor inside CMOS

A bulk CMOS inverter places a pMOS in an n-well on a p-type substrate next to an nMOS. The p+ source of the pMOS, the n-well and the p-substrate form a vertical PNP transistor, while the n+ source of the nMOS, the p-substrate and the n-well form a lateral NPN transistor. Each one's collector drives the other's base, making a PNPN thyristor between the supply and ground. Normally it stays off because well and substrate contacts hold both bases at their rail potentials, but the resistance of the well and substrate between those contacts and the transistors leaves room for a local voltage drop.

How latch-up is triggered and sustained

If current is injected into the well or substrate, for example when an input pin is driven above the supply or below ground, by electrostatic discharge, by a radiation strike or by incorrect power sequencing, the voltage drop across the well or substrate resistance can forward-bias one parasitic base-emitter junction. That transistor's collector current then turns on the other, and if the loop gain exceeds one the pair feeds itself. A low-resistance path from supply to ground then stays on even after the trigger disappears and persists until power is removed, often overheating and destroying metal lines or junctions.

Layout and process countermeasures

Most defenses lower the well and substrate resistances or the parasitic gains. In layout, well and substrate contacts are placed frequently, which is why standard-cell libraries include tap cells, and I/O transistors are enclosed in guard rings that collect injected carriers before they reach the core. Increasing the spacing between nMOS and pMOS also helps. In the process, retrograde wells with heavy doping deep below the surface and p/p+ epitaxial wafers with a low-resistance substrate shunt parasitic current. Silicon on insulator (SOI) removes the substrate path entirely, so SOI circuits are essentially immune.

Latch-up vs. other reliability failures

Latch-up is an event, not gradual wear. Hot carrier injection (HCI) and bias temperature instability (NBTI in pMOS, PBTI in high-k nMOS) slowly shift threshold voltage over years, and electromigration slowly erodes metal lines, whereas latch-up can destroy a chip within moments of a single trigger. For this reason latch-up immunity is tested directly: qualification standards such as AEC-Q100 for automotive ICs include latch-up tests, which inject current into pins and apply overvoltage to supplies while checking that the supply current returns to normal afterward.

  2. BBecause at higher temperatures the current gain of the parasitic transistors and the well resistance increase, making latch-up more likely
  3. CBecause ESD protection circuits stop working at high temperature, so the ESD test can be completed at the same time
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Because at higher temperatures the current gain of the parasitic transistors and the well resistance increase, making latch-up more likely

CMOS latch-up is a phenomenon in which parasitic pnp and npn transistors conduct as a thyristor and a large current keeps flowing. As temperature rises, the current gain of the parasitic transistors increases and the well and substrate resistance also increases, so even a small current can trigger conduction. Therefore, under AEC-Q100, tests that inject current into pins or apply overvoltage are performed as JESD78 Class II conditions at the maximum operating temperature set by the temperature grade (ambient; for example, 125 °C for Grade 1). High temperature is not used to relax the conditions, and the test is performed separately from ESD tests.

Which correctly describes latch-up in CMOS circuits?

  1. ANormal operation for holding data
  2. BA phenomenon in which a parasitic thyristor structure turns on and a large current keeps flowing between power and ground
  3. CA phenomenon in which the clock stops
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A phenomenon in which a parasitic thyristor structure turns on and a large current keeps flowing between power and ground

CMOS structures contain a parasitic PNPN thyristor formed by PNP and NPN transistors in the wells and substrate. If noise or input overvoltage injects substrate current and turns it on, the two transistors amplify each other's current, sustaining a large supply-to-ground current that persists until power is removed and can burn out the device. Strong well contacts, guard rings and SOI prevent it.

FAQ

What causes latch-up in CMOS?

A parasitic PNPN thyristor formed by the wells and substrate turns on when injected current, for example from an input overvoltage or ESD, forward-biases one of its junctions. The two parasitic bipolar transistors then sustain each other, keeping a large current flowing from supply to ground.

How do you recover from latch-up?

The latched state persists until the supply voltage is removed or reduced enough to stop the current, so power must be cycled. If the current has already overheated metal or junctions, the damage is permanent.

Why does SOI prevent latch-up?

In silicon on insulator, each transistor sits in a thin silicon layer on a buried oxide, so there is no continuous well and substrate path to form the parasitic thyristor. Without that path, the regenerative loop cannot exist.
