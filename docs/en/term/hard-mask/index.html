What is Hard mask?

A durable mask of nitride, carbon, metal or similar used when photoresist alone cannot survive the etch, for example in deep-hole etching.

Japanese name: ハードマスク

Hard mask appears in 3 practice questions across 2 topics, including Etching, Interconnect processes.

Field: Etching

Why photoresist is not enough

Resist for fine patterns is thin, often around 100 nm, partly because tall narrow resist lines collapse. Etching consumes the mask too, and selectivity, the target etch rate divided by the mask etch rate, sets how much is lost: at 100 nm/min for oxide and 20 nm/min for the mask, selectivity is 5, so etching 500 nm of oxide consumes 100 nm of mask, and with overetch and edge erosion about 150–200 nm is needed. A hard mask, an inorganic or carbon film that resists the main etch far better, removes this limit.

How a hard mask is used

The film stack is deposited first, then lithography patterns the resist. A short etch transfers the pattern into the hard mask, the resist can be stripped, and the hard mask defines the main etch. Multilayer schemes chain this idea: in a tri-layer stack, resist patterns a thin silicon-containing antireflective layer, which in turn masks an oxygen-based etch of a thick carbon layer, because oxygen plasma removes carbon but barely attacks silicon-containing films. The thick carbon then masks the target film.

Choosing the material

The hard mask must resist the chemistry that etches the target. Silicon nitride, oxide or oxynitride mask silicon and polysilicon etches; amorphous carbon or polysilicon masks oxide etches with fluorocarbon gases; titanium nitride has been used as a metal hard mask for trenches in low-k dielectrics. For the very deep, high-aspect-ratio holes of 3D NAND, amorphous-carbon hard masks several micrometers thick are used, so their deposition, etch and removal are major process steps. Carbon is easy to strip by oxygen ashing, while metal masks need wet removal.

Hard masks for self-alignment

Hard masks also define geometry beyond simple etch resistance. In self-aligned double or quadruple patterning, spacers deposited on mandrels and then etched back become the hard mask that sets line width. In self-aligned via schemes, the hard mask used to pattern wiring trenches is kept, or materially distinct caps are placed over lines, so a via etch stays confined within the target line's width even with a few nanometers of misalignment, avoiding shorts to neighboring lines at tight pitches.

Answer: C. Hard mask

Deep etches would consume resist only about 100 nm thick before finishing, so the resist pattern is first transferred into a nitride, oxide or amorphous-carbon layer, which then masks the main etch—a hard mask. For 3D NAND's deep holes, amorphous-carbon hard masks several µm thick are used, making their deposition and removal important steps in their own right.

If the oxide etch rate is 100 nm/min and the mask is consumed at 20 nm/min, what is the selectivity to the mask?

  1. A0.2
  2. B5
  3. C80
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 5

Selectivity is the etch rate of the target film divided by that of the mask (or underlayer): 100 ÷ 20 = 5. Etching 500 nm of oxide consumes 100 nm of mask, so allowing for overetch and edge erosion the mask should be at least about 150–200 nm thick. If selectivity is insufficient, a thicker mask or a hard mask is used.

FAQ

What is the difference between a hard mask and a photoresist mask?

Photoresist is a light-sensitive polymer patterned directly by lithography but erodes quickly in plasma. A hard mask is a durable film such as nitride, oxide, carbon or metal that is patterned using the resist and then survives long, aggressive etches.

Why is amorphous carbon used as a hard mask?

Amorphous carbon etches very slowly in the fluorocarbon plasmas used for oxide and nitride, so a thick layer can last through deep etches, and it is removed easily afterward by oxygen ashing without damaging the patterned film.

How thick should an etch mask be?

Divide the target etch depth by the selectivity to estimate mask consumption, then add margin for overetch and corner erosion. For 500 nm of oxide at selectivity 5, about 100 nm is consumed, so roughly 150–200 nm is a safe thickness.
