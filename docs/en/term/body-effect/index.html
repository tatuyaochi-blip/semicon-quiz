What is Body effect?

An increase in threshold voltage when reverse bias is applied between source and substrate (body), because the depletion charge grows. Also called the substrate bias effect; it is sometimes used to tune the threshold.

Japanese name: 基板バイアス効果

Body effect appears in 3 practice questions across 1 topic, including MOSFET.

Field: MOSFET

Why source-to-body voltage raises Vth

In an n-channel MOSFET the gate must first support the negative charge of the depletion region under it before an inversion layer can form. When the source-to-body junction is reverse-biased (VSB greater than zero, meaning the body is negative relative to the source), the depletion region widens and holds more charge, so a larger gate voltage is needed to reach inversion. The textbook expression is Vth = Vth0 + γ(√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF)), where the body-effect coefficient γ = √(2qεSi·NA)/Cox grows with heavier substrate doping and with thicker gate oxide.

Where the body effect shows up in circuits

In a stack of series nMOS transistors, such as a NAND gate pull-down, only the bottom device has its source at ground. The upper devices see raised source potentials, so their Vth rises and they conduct less current. In a single nMOS pass transistor with its gate at VDD, the output can only rise to about VDD − Vth, and because Vth increases as the source climbs, the final level is lower still. In a source follower, the body effect adds a back-gate transconductance gmb that pulls the voltage gain further below 1.

Typical size of the effect

Analog designers often express the body effect as the ratio gmb/gm, which for bulk transistors is commonly in the range of roughly 0.1 to 0.3 depending on doping and bias. A source follower in a common p-substrate process, where the nMOS body is tied to ground, has its gain limited to about gm/(gm + gmb) even with an ideal load. Placing the transistor in its own well and tying body to source removes the effect, at the cost of extra area and well capacitance.

Body bias as a tuning knob

The same physics is used deliberately. Reverse body bias raises Vth to cut subthreshold leakage in standby, and forward body bias lowers Vth to gain speed, which also lets designers compensate for process variation after fabrication. The knob depends on the device structure. In FinFETs the thin fin is controlled almost entirely by the gate, so body biasing has little effect, whereas FD-SOI (fully depleted silicon-on-insulator) transistors with a thin buried oxide allow a wide back-bias tuning range.

In an n-channel MOSFET, what happens to the threshold voltage when a negative voltage is applied to the substrate (body) relative to the source (body bias)?

  1. AIt rises
  2. BIt falls
  3. CIt does not change

Answer: A. It rises

Reverse-biasing the source-to-body junction widens the depletion region under the gate and increases its charge, so a larger gate voltage is needed to compensate—the threshold voltage rises (the body effect). In stacked transistors the upper devices see raised source potentials, so their Vth increases and they slow down. Conversely, adjusting body voltage to tune Vth is used to cut leakage.

Which is a characteristic of a MOSFET source follower (common-drain) circuit?

  1. AIts voltage gain is slightly less than 1 and its output impedance is low
  2. BIts voltage gain is very large and its output is inverted
  3. CIts current gain is zero
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Its voltage gain is slightly less than 1 and its output impedance is low

In a source follower the source tracks the gate minus VGS, so the voltage gain is slightly below 1 (gmRs/(1 + gmRs)) and non-inverting; the body effect lowers it further. The input impedance is essentially infinite (it is a gate), and the output impedance is low, about 1/gm, so it serves as a buffer. It is the counterpart of the emitter follower, but its level shift (VGS) is larger than VBE and varies more.

FAQ

Is the body effect the same as the substrate bias effect?

Yes. Both names describe the change in MOSFET threshold voltage caused by a voltage between source and body (substrate); reverse bias raises Vth and forward bias lowers it.

Why does the body effect reduce the gain of a source follower?

As the output (source) moves, the source-to-body voltage changes and shifts Vth, which acts like a second transconductance gmb opposing the signal. The gain becomes roughly gm/(gm + gmb) instead of approaching 1.

How can the body effect be eliminated?

Connecting each transistor's body to its own source removes VSB, but this requires the device to sit in a separate well, which costs area and is only possible for the well-type transistor in a given process.
