What is PSRR (power supply rejection ratio)?

A measure of how little supply-voltage variation reaches a circuit's output. The larger it is, the more immune the circuit is to supply noise.

Japanese name: PSRR(電源電圧変動除去比)

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

How PSRR is defined

PSRR compares a change on a supply rail with the change it causes at the circuit's output, and it is quoted in decibels using 20 log₁₀ because it is a ratio of voltages. A PSRR of 60 dB therefore means a supply disturbance is attenuated about 1000 times, and 80 dB means 10,000 times. Datasheets often give separate values for the positive and negative rails, and some refer the result to the input by dividing by the closed-loop gain. Sign conventions differ between vendors, so the same part may be listed as +60 dB or −60 dB; what matters is the magnitude.

Why PSRR falls with frequency

In an op-amp or a low-dropout regulator (LDO), supply rejection comes mainly from the feedback loop, which senses and corrects whatever the supply couples into the signal path. Loop gain drops as frequency rises, so less correction is available, while parasitic capacitances open direct paths from the supply to the output. The result is a curve, not a single number: a device with 80–100 dB at low frequency may offer only 20–30 dB at several hundred kilohertz. That is exactly where switching-regulator ripple and its harmonics tend to sit.

Working with the numbers

To estimate output ripple, convert PSRR to a linear ratio with 10^(PSRR/20) and divide the input ripple by it: 100 mV of 1 kHz ripple through 60 dB leaves about 0.1 mV. Using 10 log₁₀, which applies to power ratios, gives an answer a thousand times too small. Always read the PSRR at the actual ripple frequency from the datasheet graph. When an LC filter precedes an LDO, their attenuations multiply, so in decibels they roughly add, although source and load impedances keep this from being exact.

PSRR vs. CMRR and line regulation

Common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) describes how well an amplifier ignores a voltage that appears equally on both of its inputs; PSRR describes how well it ignores changes on its power pins. The two are often confused because both are rejection ratios in decibels. Line regulation, a regulator specification, is the static or slow-change version of PSRR, usually given as output change per volt of input change. PSRR is the dynamic counterpart that tells you how much ripple and noise get through at each frequency.

Answer: C. 0.1 mV

Voltage ratios in decibels use 20 log₁₀, so 60 dB is 1/1000. 100 mV ÷ 1000 = 0.1 mV appears at the output. 0.1 µV is the mistake of calculating with 10 log₁₀ (power ratio), and about 1.7 mV is the mistake of dividing 100 by 60. PSRR generally decreases at higher frequencies; even with 60 dB at 1 kHz, some products drop to around 20–30 dB at several hundred kHz. When placing an LDO after a switching regulator, check the PSRR at that switching frequency in the graph.

FAQ

How do I calculate output ripple from PSRR in dB?

Divide the input ripple amplitude by 10^(PSRR/20), using the PSRR value at the ripple frequency. For example, 60 dB corresponds to a factor of 1000, so 100 mV of ripple becomes about 0.1 mV.

Why doesn't my LDO remove switching regulator ripple?

LDO PSRR is usually high only at low frequencies and can drop to a few tens of dB at the hundreds of kilohertz where switching ripple sits. Adding an LC or ferrite filter before the LDO, or choosing an LDO specified for high-frequency PSRR, is the usual fix.

Is PSRR positive or negative in dB?

Both forms appear because vendors define the ratio in opposite directions. Treat the magnitude as the rejection: a larger magnitude means less supply noise reaches the output.
