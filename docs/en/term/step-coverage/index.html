What is Step coverage?

A measure of how uniformly a film covers the sidewalls and bottoms of steps and holes compared with flat areas. ALD gives excellent step coverage.

Japanese name: ステップカバレッジ

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

How step coverage is measured

Step coverage compares film thickness on the sidewalls or bottom of a trench or hole with the thickness on the flat top surface, usually as a percentage such as bottom ÷ top. A value near 100% means the film follows the topography uniformly. The figure always depends on aspect ratio, the depth-to-width ratio of the feature, so a method that coats a shallow step well may leave the bottom of a deep contact almost bare. Poor coverage also tends to come with overhang at the opening, which can pinch off and trap a void when the feature is later filled.

Why PVD, CVD and ALD differ

In physical vapor deposition (PVD), such as sputtering or evaporation, atoms fly in nearly straight lines and barely migrate after landing, so surfaces not in line of sight from the opening are shadowed; bottom coverage in deep holes can fall below 10%. Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) delivers precursor gases that diffuse into features and react on the surface, giving much better coverage. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) alternates self-limiting surface reactions, each adding at most about one monolayer per cycle, and reaches close to 100% even in very high-aspect-ratio structures.

Reaction-limited vs. supply-limited CVD

CVD coverage depends on which step is slower. If the surface reaction is slow (reaction-limited), gas penetrates deep into the feature before it reacts, and growth rate is nearly uniform everywhere. If gas supply is slow (supply- or transport-limited), the opening receives more precursor than the bottom, so the top thickens and may close off. Lower temperature slows the reaction and lower pressure speeds gas diffusion, so low-pressure CVD (LPCVD) at tens to hundreds of pascals is conformal. TEOS oxide and WF6-based tungsten CVD are classic examples chosen for their good coverage.

Where step coverage matters

Barrier and liner layers in contacts and vias must be continuous yet thin, so their sidewall and bottom coverage directly sets reliability. Gate dielectrics and metals wrapped around FinFET or gate-all-around channels, and films lining deep 3D NAND holes, rely on ALD-level conformality. Step coverage should not be confused with gap fill: a perfectly conformal film closing a narrow trench from both sides leaves a seam, and void-free filling may need deposition–etch cycles. Sometimes poor coverage is wanted, as in lift-off, where line-of-sight evaporation keeps films on resist sidewalls discontinuous.

Answer: A. Surface-reaction-limited conditions (such as relatively low temperature and low pressure)

Under reaction-limited conditions, precursor gas reaches deep into trenches before reacting slowly at the surface, so deposition rate varies little with location and sidewalls and bottoms are coated uniformly. Lower temperatures slow the reaction and lower pressures speed gas diffusion, both pushing toward this regime. Under supply-limited conditions, by contrast, openings where gas arrives easily get thicker film and tend to pinch off.

FAQ

Which deposition method has the best step coverage?

ALD gives the best, typically close to 100%, because each self-limiting reaction cycle coats every exposed surface equally. CVD comes next, and line-of-sight PVD methods such as sputtering and evaporation are the worst.

Is step coverage the same as conformality?

They are closely related and often used interchangeably. Conformality describes how evenly a film follows topography in general, while step coverage is usually quoted as a specific thickness ratio, such as bottom-to-top or sidewall-to-top.

Why is step coverage poor in sputtering?

Sputtered atoms travel almost in straight lines and stick where they land, so the trench opening shadows the sidewalls and bottom. Film builds up at the top corners instead, forming overhangs.
