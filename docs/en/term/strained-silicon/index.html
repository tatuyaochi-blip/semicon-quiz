What is Strained silicon?

Silicon whose band structure is modified by strain to raise carrier mobility. Tensile strain helps nMOS and compressive strain helps pMOS; it is applied with SiGe source/drain regions, nitride films and the like.

Japanese name: ひずみシリコン

Strained silicon appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Epitaxial growth.

Field: Epitaxial growth

Why strain raises mobility

Strained silicon is silicon whose atomic spacing has been stretched or compressed slightly to raise carrier mobility. Strain changes the band structure: for electrons, the energies of the six conduction-band valleys split, electrons gather in the valleys with lighter effective mass along the current direction, and scattering between valleys decreases. For holes, the shape of the valence band changes and the effective mass falls. For transistors in the usual orientation, tensile strain along the current direction helps nMOS and compressive strain helps pMOS. Strain entered volume production at the 90 nm generation (around 2003), when scaling alone was no longer raising drive current enough.

Biaxial strain across the wafer

The first approach studied was to apply biaxial strain across the whole wafer. When thin silicon is grown on a relaxed SiGe layer, the silicon's lattice constant (0.543 nm), being smaller than SiGe's, is stretched in-plane, producing tensile strain that raises electron mobility 1.5–2×. Strained silicon on insulator (sSOI), with such a layer transferred onto oxide, also exists. However, dislocations and other defects in the relaxed SiGe, Ge diffusion, and the fact that nMOS and pMOS need strain in different directions led production to favor applying strain locally to each transistor.

Uniaxial strain applied per transistor

Production has relied on process steps that apply uniaxial stress along the channel's current direction. For pMOS, the source and drain are recessed and refilled with SiGe by epitaxial growth; the SiGe, with its larger lattice constant, pushes on the channel from both sides and creates compressive strain. For nMOS, the transistor is covered with a nitride film under tensile stress, or SiP heavily doped with phosphorus and similar materials are used for the source and drain. Techniques that 'memorize' stress in the crystal during gate formation have also been used. Stacking several methods increases the effect.

Strain in FinFETs and GAA, and how it is measured

In FinFETs and GAA devices too, strain from source/drain epitaxial layers remains an important performance lever, with high-Ge SiGe for pMOS and SiP for nMOS. As fins and sheets become thinner and shorter, though, strain relaxes more easily and the amount that can be applied depends on the structure. For pMOS, making the channel itself SiGe to raise hole mobility is also used. Strain can be measured from the shift of silicon's Raman peak (about 520 cm⁻¹), which moves higher under compression and lower under tension. Electron diffraction in an electron microscope can also measure atomic spacing.

Answer: C. Tensile strain

Silicon's lattice constant (0.543 nm) is smaller than SiGe's, so a thin silicon layer grown on relaxed SiGe is stretched in-plane to match, producing tensile strain (strained silicon). The strain splits conduction-band valleys, concentrating electrons in valleys with lighter effective mass and reducing scattering, so electron mobility rises 1.5–2× and has been used to boost nMOS performance.

FAQ

Why do nMOS and pMOS need strain in opposite directions?

Because strain changes the band structure differently for electrons and holes. In silicon transistors in the usual orientation, tensile strain along the current direction reduces electron effective mass and scattering, while compressive strain along the current direction reduces hole effective mass. nMOS therefore gets tensile and pMOS compressive strain.

How much does strained silicon improve performance?

It depends on how strain is applied, how large it is and the generation. Biaxial tensile strain on relaxed SiGe raises electron mobility 1.5–2×. In short-channel transistors, however, higher mobility does not translate fully into higher drive current, so the gain is smaller.

Why is SiGe used in pMOS sources and drains?

Ge's lattice constant is about 4% larger than silicon's, so SiGe's is larger too, and SiGe embedded in the source and drain pushes on the channel along the current direction, creating compressive strain. This raises hole mobility, and heavy boron doping in the SiGe also lowers source/drain resistance.
