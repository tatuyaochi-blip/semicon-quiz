What is InP (indium phosphide)?

A compound semiconductor of indium and phosphorus. As a substrate for lattice-matched layers such as InGaAs, it is used for fiber-optic lasers and photodetectors and for high-speed transistors.

Japanese name: InP(リン化インジウム)

Field: Compound semiconductors

Material basics

Indium phosphide is a III-V compound semiconductor with a zinc-blende crystal structure, a direct band gap of about 1.34 eV at room temperature and a lattice constant of about 0.587 nm. Its thermal conductivity, about 68 W/(m·K), is less than half of silicon's, so heat removal needs care in power devices. Growing large, low-defect crystals while preventing phosphorus from evaporating is difficult, so InP wafers are typically 100–150 mm in diameter, far smaller than 300 mm silicon. That keeps cost per chip high and equipment specialized.

Why InP dominates fiber optics

Optical fiber has its lowest dispersion near 1.3 µm and its lowest loss, about 0.2 dB/km, near 1.55 µm. Silicon cannot emit efficiently because its band gap is indirect, and it barely absorbs beyond about 1.1 µm. Alloys grown lattice-matched on InP fill the gap: In0.53Ga0.47As, with a band gap of about 0.75 eV, absorbs out to about 1.7 µm for photodiodes, and InGaAsP compositions set the operating wavelength of lasers and modulators. Vegard's law gives the composition that matches the InP lattice, avoiding dislocations even in thick layers.

InP vs. GaAs

Both are direct-gap III-V substrates, but they serve different niches. GaAs lattice-matches AlGaAs and InGaP, emits at shorter near-infrared wavelengths around 850 nm used in short-reach links, and its InGaP/GaAs HBTs dominate smartphone power amplifiers. InP supports the 1.3 and 1.55 µm telecom bands and higher-speed transistors: InGaAs channels on InP reach electron mobility around 10,000 cm²/V·s for HEMTs, and InP HBTs reach transition frequencies of several hundred GHz, driving optical links beyond 100 Gb/s and millimetre-wave and terahertz circuits.

InP meets silicon photonics

Silicon photonics builds waveguides, modulators and germanium photodetectors in CMOS-style fabs, but still needs a light source. Direct growth of InP on silicon suffers from an approximately 8% lattice mismatch, so InP is integrated in other ways. In heterogeneous integration, InP-based epitaxial layers are bonded onto an SOI wafer, the InP substrate is removed and lasers are patterned by lithography aligned to the silicon waveguides, coupling light through tapers. Alternatively, finished InP laser chips are attached separately. Because indium and phosphorus are contamination concerns, such steps are kept apart from mainstream CMOS lines.

Answer: A. Lasers and photodetectors for optical fiber communication

InGaAsP and related alloys grown on InP can be tuned in composition to emit and detect light in the 1.3 µm band (low dispersion) and the 1.55 µm band (minimum loss, about 0.2 dB/km) of optical fiber. They are used in communication lasers, modulators and photodiodes, growing in importance with demand for data-center optical transceivers. InP also hosts ultra-high-speed HBTs and HEMTs.

  2. BHigh-speed, high-frequency circuits for optical and wireless communication
  3. CGenerating power from solar heat

Answer: B. High-speed, high-frequency circuits for optical and wireless communication

Compound-semiconductor HBTs use a wide-gap emitter to suppress hole back-injection, allowing a thin, heavily doped base, and their high electron mobility makes them fast. GaAs-based (InGaP/GaAs) HBTs run from a single supply with high efficiency and dominate smartphone transmit power amplifiers, while InP HBTs, with fT of several hundred GHz, drive electronics for optical links beyond 100 Gb/s and terahertz research.

FAQ

Why is InP used for 1550 nm lasers and detectors?

Alloys such as InGaAsP and InGaAs grown lattice-matched on InP have band gaps that correspond to the 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm fiber windows, and they have direct gaps that emit light efficiently. Silicon cannot emit efficiently and does not absorb at these wavelengths.

Is InP a direct bandgap semiconductor?

Yes. InP has a direct band gap of about 1.34 eV at room temperature, so electrons and holes can recombine radiatively without needing a phonon.

What is the difference between InP and GaAs?

GaAs is the substrate for shorter-wavelength emitters around 850 nm and for smartphone power-amplifier HBTs, while InP supports the 1.3–1.55 µm telecom wavelengths and the fastest III-V transistors. InP wafers are generally smaller and more expensive.
