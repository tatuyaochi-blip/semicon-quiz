What is Current gain (hFE)?

The ratio of collector current to base current (IC/IB) in a bipolar transistor.

Japanese name: 電流増幅率(hFE)

Current gain (hFE) appears in 22 practice questions across 2 topics, including Bipolar transistors, Automotive Semiconductors and Functional Safety.

Field: Bipolar transistors

From α to β

Current gain can be expressed two ways. The common-base gain α = IC/IE is slightly less than 1 because a small fraction of the emitter current is lost in the base. The common-emitter gain, written β or hFE = IC/IB, follows as β = α/(1 − α). With α = 0.99, β = 99; with α = 0.995, β = 199, so a tiny change in α makes a large change in hFE. In the active region IC ≈ hFE × IB: a 10 µA base current with hFE = 100 gives about 1 mA, provided the collector circuit can supply it.

What sets hFE inside the transistor

Base current consists mainly of holes injected back from the base into the emitter and of electrons that recombine while crossing the base (for an NPN device). A heavily doped emitter makes injection efficiency high by suppressing the back-injected holes, and a base much thinner than the minority-carrier diffusion length lets nearly all electrons reach the collector. Very heavy emitter doping eventually loses effect through bandgap narrowing; heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs) solve this with a wider-bandgap emitter. A thin base also raises base resistance and the risk of punch-through.

Why circuits should not rely on it

hFE is typically tens to hundreds for small-signal transistors and tens for power devices, but it varies widely between units of the same part, often binned into ranges such as 100–400. It also rises with temperature, roughly 0.5–1% per °C, and it peaks at moderate collector current, falling at low current because of depletion-region recombination and at high current because of high injection and the Kirk effect. Robust bias circuits therefore use an emitter resistor and a resistor-divider base so the operating point is set by resistors rather than by hFE.

hFE vs. hfe, and switching

Uppercase hFE is the DC ratio IC/IB, used for bias calculations; lowercase hfe is the small-signal ratio ΔIC/ΔIB at the operating point, used for AC gain and input resistance. They are often close but not equal. In switching, the transistor is driven into saturation, where the collector current is limited by the load and IC/IB, the forced beta, is deliberately below hFE. Designers commonly supply 2–10 times IC/hFE(min) as base current to guarantee saturation, at the cost of more stored charge and slower turn-off.

Answer: B. IC / IB

hFE is the DC current gain in the common-emitter configuration, hFE = IC/IB. It is typically tens to hundreds for small-signal transistors and tens for power transistors. It varies widely between units of the same part number (often binned into ranges such as 100–400) and with temperature and current, so circuits are designed to work without depending on its exact value. The common-base gain α = IC/IE is slightly less than 1.

What is the approximate common-emitter current gain β of a bipolar transistor whose common-base current gain α is 0.99?

  1. A0.99
  2. B9.9
  3. C99
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 99

α and β are related by β = α/(1 − α), so α = 0.99 gives β = 0.99 ÷ 0.01 = 99; conversely α = β/(1 + β). With α = 0.995, β becomes 199—a tiny change in α makes a big change in β. If 1% of the emitter current (1 − α) is lost in the base, the base current is about 1/99 of the collector current, which is why minimizing base losses is central to transistor design.

FAQ

What is the difference between hFE and beta?

In practice they mean the same quantity, the common-emitter current gain IC/IB. hFE is the h-parameter name used on datasheets for the DC value, while β is the symbol common in textbooks and device physics.

What is the difference between hFE and hfe?

hFE is the DC current gain IC/IB at a given operating point, while hfe is the small-signal gain ΔIC/ΔIB around that point. Use hFE for bias design and hfe for AC analysis.

Why does hFE vary so much between transistors?

hFE depends on base width, doping and recombination, which vary from device to device, and it changes further with temperature and collector current. That is why good designs set the operating point with feedback resistors instead of depending on an exact hFE.
