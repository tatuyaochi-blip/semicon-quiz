What is Early effect?

A phenomenon in which raising the collector voltage widens the base-collector depletion layer, narrowing the effective base width and slightly increasing collector current.

Japanese name: アーリー効果

Field: Bipolar transistors

Base-width modulation

In a bipolar junction transistor (BJT) in the active region, the collector-base junction is reverse biased, and its depletion layer extends partly into the base. Raising the collector voltage widens that depletion layer and shrinks the neutral base width. Collector current is set by minority carriers diffusing across the base, and with a uniformly doped base it is inversely proportional to the neutral base width, so a narrower base gives a steeper carrier gradient and slightly more current. Fewer carriers also recombine in the shorter base, so current gain rises slightly as well.

Early voltage and output resistance

On the output characteristics, collector current versus collector-emitter voltage, the active-region lines are nearly horizontal but slope slightly upward. Extended backward, they meet near one point on the voltage axis, at -VA, where VA is the Early voltage. A common model is IC = IS exp(VBE/VT)(1 + VCE/VA), which gives an output resistance ro = VA/IC. The maximum voltage gain of a single transistor, gm·ro, equals VA/VT, so a VA of 100 V allows about 4,000 at room temperature. Early voltages of tens to around a hundred volts are common for small-signal npn transistors.

Effects on analog circuits

Finite output resistance limits amplifier gain and makes current sources imperfect. In a simple current mirror, the reference transistor is diode-connected and sits at about 0.7 V, while the output transistor may have a much larger collector-emitter voltage; the Early effect then makes the copied current larger by roughly the voltage difference divided by VA. Cascode and Wilson mirrors hold the critical transistor's collector voltage nearly constant, raising output resistance by a factor on the order of the transistor's current gain. In SPICE, the Gummel-Poon model represents the effect with the forward Early voltage parameter VAF.

Early effect versus channel-length modulation

MOSFETs show a closely analogous effect: increasing the drain voltage shortens the effective channel, so drain current rises in saturation. It is called channel-length modulation and is modeled with a parameter lambda, which plays the role of 1/VA. In both devices, finite output resistance limits intrinsic gain. Device designers raise the Early voltage with heavier base doping, which keeps the depletion layer from intruding into the base, but that lowers current gain. At the extreme, the collector depletion layer reaches the emitter, causing punch-through and loss of transistor action.

FAQ

What is the Early voltage of a transistor?

It is the voltage magnitude at which the extended active-region output curves of a BJT intersect the voltage axis, written VA. A larger Early voltage means a flatter curve and higher output resistance, ro = VA/IC.

How does the Early effect limit amplifier gain?

It gives the transistor a finite output resistance, so the maximum gain of a single stage is gm·ro = VA/VT. For VA around 100 V this is a few thousand, and any load resistance in parallel lowers it further.

Is the Early effect the same as channel-length modulation?

They are analogous rather than identical. The Early effect narrows the neutral base of a BJT, while channel-length modulation shortens a MOSFET's channel, but both raise output current with output voltage and create finite output resistance.
