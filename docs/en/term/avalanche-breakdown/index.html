What is Avalanche breakdown?

A phenomenon in which carriers accelerated by a strong reverse field strike atoms and create new carriers, which multiply in a chain reaction so that a large current flows.

Japanese name: なだれ降伏

Field: PN junctions and diodes

Impact ionization and multiplication

Under strong reverse bias, electrons and holes crossing the depletion region gain kinetic energy from the field between collisions. When a carrier gains enough energy, a collision with the lattice can break a bond and create a new electron-hole pair, a process called impact ionization. The new carriers are accelerated in turn and can ionize further atoms, so each carrier entering the region produces many. The rate of ionization rises very steeply with field, and in silicon electrons ionize more readily than holes. Breakdown is reached when the multiplication factor grows without bound and current rises sharply at nearly constant voltage.

Critical field and breakdown voltage

In a reverse-biased abrupt junction, the field is zero at the depletion edges and peaks at the metallurgical junction, forming a triangle whose area equals Vbi + V. Avalanche begins roughly when the peak reaches the critical field, about 3×10⁵ V/cm in silicon. For a one-sided junction this gives VBR ≈ εEc²/(2qN), so lighter doping on the low-doped side yields higher breakdown voltage. Silicon carbide's critical field is about ten times higher, allowing a much thinner drift layer for the same rating. At junction edges and corners, curvature concentrates the field, so guard rings and field plates are used.

Temperature dependence and the Zener mechanism

As temperature rises, stronger lattice vibrations scatter carriers more often, so they need a higher field to gain ionizing energy and the avalanche breakdown voltage increases. This positive temperature coefficient helps spread current evenly, because a hotter spot breaks down later. Zener, or tunneling, breakdown in heavily doped junctions behaves the opposite way, since the bandgap shrinks with temperature and tunneling becomes easier. In silicon diodes, tunneling dominates below about 5 V and avalanche above, so the sign of the temperature coefficient tells you which mechanism is at work.

When avalanche is useful, and when it destroys

Avalanche breakdown is not inherently destructive: if current and energy are limited, the junction recovers fully, as in Zener regulators above about 5 V. Avalanche photodiodes bias just below breakdown so each photo-generated carrier is multiplied, giving internal gain, and single-photon avalanche diodes bias above breakdown so one photon triggers a detectable pulse. Power MOSFETs are rated for the avalanche energy they can absorb when switching inductive loads. Damage occurs when the dissipated energy overheats the device or current crowds into a local hot spot.

Answer: A. It increases

As temperature rises, lattice-vibration scattering increases and carriers are less able to gain enough energy for impact ionization, so the avalanche breakdown voltage rises (a positive temperature coefficient). This means hot spots become less likely to break down, which spreads the current out. Zener breakdown, by contrast, drops with temperature as the gap shrinks. The sign of the temperature coefficient is a clue to which mechanism is at work.

FAQ

Is avalanche breakdown destructive?

Not by itself. A junction in avalanche recovers completely as long as the current and energy are limited; damage comes from excessive power dissipation and local overheating.

What is the difference between avalanche breakdown and Zener breakdown?

Avalanche breakdown multiplies carriers through impact ionization in moderately doped junctions and has a positive temperature coefficient. Zener breakdown is quantum tunneling across a very thin depletion layer in heavily doped junctions, has a negative temperature coefficient and dominates below about 5 V in silicon.

Why does avalanche breakdown voltage increase with temperature?

Higher temperature increases lattice scattering, so carriers lose energy more often and need a stronger field to reach the energy required for impact ionization.
