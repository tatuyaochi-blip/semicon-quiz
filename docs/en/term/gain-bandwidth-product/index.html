What is Gain-bandwidth product (GBW)?

The product of an op-amp's gain and bandwidth, which stays roughly constant. With a GBW of 1 MHz, for example, a gain-of-10 circuit has a bandwidth of about 100 kHz.

Japanese name: 利得帯域幅積(GBW)

Why gain times bandwidth is constant

Most general-purpose op-amps are internally compensated so that a single dominant pole sets the open-loop response. The open-loop gain A0, often around 100 dB, stays flat up to a pole frequency fp of a few to tens of hertz and then falls at −20 dB per decade. Above fp, gain is inversely proportional to frequency, so gain multiplied by frequency stays at A0 × fp. That product is the gain-bandwidth product (GBW), and it also approximately equals the unity-gain frequency where open-loop gain reaches 1. For example, 10⁵ gain with a 10 Hz pole gives a 1 MHz GBW.

Predicting closed-loop bandwidth

With negative feedback, the closed-loop bandwidth is roughly GBW divided by the noise gain, which for a voltage-feedback op-amp is 1 + Rf/Rg, the non-inverting gain of the feedback network. A non-inverting amplifier with gain 10 on a 1 MHz part therefore reaches about 100 kHz. An inverting amplifier with gain −10 has a noise gain of 11, so its bandwidth is about 91 kHz. To get high gain and wide bandwidth together, choose a part with higher GBW or split the gain across two or more stages, each with a modest gain.

Performance well below the corner

The closed-loop −3 dB frequency is not where the amplifier stops working well; there the gain is already down about 30% and the output lags by 45°. What matters below it is the loop gain, approximately GBW divided by the product of frequency and noise gain, because loop gain is what suppresses distortion, gain error and output impedance. It falls as frequency rises: at one-tenth of the closed-loop bandwidth it is only about 10, so feedback reduces distortion only about tenfold and the phase shift is already near 6°. Low-distortion and precision circuits therefore choose GBW with large margin.

Where the rule breaks down

The constant-GBW rule applies to dominant-pole, voltage-feedback op-amps in small-signal operation. Decompensated op-amps trade stability for speed and are specified only above a minimum closed-loop gain. Current-feedback amplifiers do not follow the rule: their bandwidth is set mainly by the feedback resistor and stays nearly constant over a range of gains. Large signals are limited by slew rate rather than GBW, so a circuit can meet its small-signal bandwidth yet distort at full amplitude. Additional poles near the unity-gain frequency also reduce phase margin, causing peaking.

Answer: C. About 100 kHz

An internally compensated op-amp's open-loop gain falls at −20 dB/decade, so gain × bandwidth is roughly constant. With a 1 MHz GBW, a gain of 10 gives about 1 MHz ÷ 10 = 100 kHz. Strictly, divide by the non-inverting (noise) gain, so an inverting gain of −10 divides by 11, about 91 kHz. To get both high gain and wide bandwidth, choose a higher-GBW op-amp or split the gain across stages.

FAQ

Is gain-bandwidth product the same as unity-gain bandwidth?

For a dominant-pole compensated op-amp they are nearly equal, since the open-loop gain falls at −20 dB/decade all the way to unity. They differ when other poles lie close to the unity-gain frequency.

How do you calculate bandwidth from GBW?

Divide GBW by the noise gain, 1 + Rf/Rg. A 1 MHz op-amp in a non-inverting gain of 10 gives about 100 kHz; an inverting gain of −10 gives about 91 kHz.

What GBW do I need for my amplifier?

At minimum, the noise gain times the highest signal frequency, but for low distortion and small phase error choose several to many times more, and check slew rate separately for large signals.
