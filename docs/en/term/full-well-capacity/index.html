What is Full-well capacity?

The maximum number of electrons one image-sensor pixel can hold. The larger it is, the brighter the scene can be before highlights clip, widening dynamic range.

Japanese name: 飽和電子数(フルウェル容量)

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

What sets the full-well capacity

During exposure, photoelectrons collect in the potential well of each pixel's photodiode, in modern CMOS sensors usually a pinned photodiode. Its capacity depends on the photodiode's area and depth, its doping profile and the voltage swing the well can accept. The readout chain can also impose a limit: charge is transferred to a floating diffusion and converted to voltage, so if that node or the output range saturates first, it sets the effective full well. Typical values range from a few thousand electrons in small smartphone pixels to tens of thousands, or about 100,000, in large-pixel cameras.

Link to dynamic range and SNR

Dynamic range is the ratio of the largest signal, the full well, to the noise floor, set mainly by read noise in electrons. A 6000-electron full well with 2 electrons of read noise gives 3000:1, about 70 dB. Full well also caps image quality in bright areas: photon shot noise equals the square root of the signal, so the best possible signal-to-noise ratio is the square root of the full well, for example 100:1 (40 dB) at 10,000 electrons.

Pixel size and conversion gain trade-offs

Shrinking pixels reduces both the light collected and the charge a pixel can hold, so small pixels clip highlights sooner and show more noise in dim scenes. Binning combines neighboring pixels to recover sensitivity when resolution is less important. Conversion gain, in microvolts per electron, poses another trade-off: high gain lowers effective read noise but lets the floating diffusion saturate at fewer electrons. Dual-conversion-gain pixels switch extra capacitance in for bright scenes, and some high-dynamic-range designs store charge that overflows the photodiode in an added capacitor.

Beyond full well, and measuring it

When light exceeds the full well, excess charge spills into neighboring pixels and bright areas spread, which is called blooming; in CCDs the charge tends to spread along the transfer (vertical) direction and can form vertical streaks. CMOS pixels provide an overflow path, often through the reset transistor, to drain excess charge. Full well is commonly measured with the photon transfer curve: in electrons the shot-noise variance equals the mean, so plotting variance against mean gives conversion gain from the slope, and the point where variance stops rising and collapses marks saturation.

Answer: B. The light received and charge stored per pixel decrease, so sensitivity and dynamic range tend to fall

Smaller pixels allow more pixels on the same sensor, but light per pixel falls in proportion to area and the full-well capacity shrinks too. Noise becomes more visible in dim scenes and highlights clip more easily in high-contrast ones. Smartphones therefore keep high pixel counts but combine pixels by binning, switching modes according to scene brightness.

What is the maximum number of electrons that one pixel of an image sensor can store called?

  1. AFull-well capacity (saturation charge)
  2. BQuantum efficiency
  3. CDark current
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Full-well capacity (saturation charge)

Full-well capacity is the maximum number of electrons a photodiode can hold; beyond it, highlights clip. It ranges from a few thousand in small smartphone pixels to tens of thousands or about 100,000 in large DSLR pixels. Dividing full well by read noise (in electrons) gives dynamic range—6000 ÷ 2 = 3000, about 70 dB. Smaller pixels hold fewer electrons, tending to reduce dynamic range.

FAQ

What is full-well capacity in an image sensor?

It is the maximum number of photoelectrons one pixel can store before it saturates and highlights clip. Typical values range from a few thousand electrons in small pixels to around 100,000 in large ones.

How does full-well capacity affect dynamic range?

Dynamic range is roughly full-well capacity divided by read noise. For example, 6000 electrons with 2 electrons of read noise gives 3000:1, or about 70 dB.

Why do smaller pixels have lower full-well capacity?

A smaller photodiode has less area and volume in which to store charge, so it saturates at fewer electrons. It also collects less light, which reduces sensitivity.
