What is HEPA filter?

A high-efficiency air filter that captures at least 99.97% of 0.3 µm particles. Filters that capture even finer particles are ULPA filters.

What a HEPA filter is

A HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air) filter is a high-performance air filter that captures at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 µm in size. Filter media made of fine glass fibers or similar materials in nonwoven form are pleated and fitted into a frame, giving a large filtering area with little pressure drop. In semiconductor cleanrooms, HEPA filters are built into units such as fan filter units (FFUs) lined up across the ceiling, which blow filtered clean air downward. They are also used in hospital operating rooms and pharmaceutical plants. Performance definitions and test methods are set by standards such as JIS and American and European standards, which differ in their details.

How particles are captured

A HEPA filter does not just sieve out particles larger than the gaps between fibers; it captures particles by making them stick to the fibers. Large particles cannot follow the bending airflow and hit fibers by inertia, particles carried by the airflow close past a fiber are intercepted when they touch it, and very small particles collide with fibers through diffusion driven by Brownian motion. Mid-sized particles, for which none of these mechanisms works well, penetrate most easily; this most penetrating particle size (MPPS) is generally around 0.1 to 0.3 µm. Performance is defined at 0.3 µm because it is close to this hardest-to-capture size.

Versus ULPA filters

An ULPA (ultra-low penetration air) filter has an even higher capture efficiency than HEPA, rated for example at 99.9995% or more for 0.15 µm particles. Semiconductor manufacturing, which handles very fine patterns, often uses ULPA filters in areas that require especially high cleanliness. However, the higher the efficiency, the greater the pressure drop for a given airflow, which raises fan power, and the higher the price. European standards and others divide HEPA and ULPA into several classes according to efficiency at the MPPS, so when comparing filters, check which standard the figures come from.

Limits and maintenance

HEPA and ULPA filters capture particles; gaseous airborne molecular contamination (AMC) such as ammonia and organics passes straight through. Cleanrooms therefore add chemical filters using activated carbon or similar media. Media made of borosilicate glass fibers can also release boron, which may cause unintended doping of wafers, so low-boron media are used as well. After installation, filters are scanned with a particle counter to check for leaks through the media and the frame, and the rise in pressure drop is monitored so the filter can be replaced once it clogs.

Answer: A. Capturing at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 µm in size

A HEPA filter is defined (in JIS and elsewhere) as capturing at least 99.97% of particles around 0.3 µm, close to the hardest size to filter. Larger particles are caught by inertia and smaller ones by Brownian diffusion, so intermediate sizes penetrate most easily. Higher-performance ULPA filters are rated at 99.9995% or better at 0.15 µm. FAQ

What is a HEPA filter?

It is a high-performance air filter that captures at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 µm in size. Built from pleated media of fine glass fibers or similar materials, it is used in cleanrooms, operating rooms, pharmaceutical plants and elsewhere to remove airborne particles.

What is the difference between HEPA and ULPA filters?

ULPA filters have an even higher capture efficiency than HEPA, for example capturing at least 99.9995% of 0.15 µm particles. Semiconductor manufacturing uses ULPA in areas that need a cleaner environment, at the cost of higher pressure drop and price.

Do particles smaller than 0.3 µm get through?

Most do not. Particles well below 0.3 µm readily collide with fibers through diffusion driven by Brownian motion and are actually captured well. The sizes that get through most easily are intermediate, around 0.1 to 0.3 µm, and HEPA performance is defined for particles near that range.
