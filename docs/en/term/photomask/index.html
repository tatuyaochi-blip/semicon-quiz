What is Photomask (reticle)?

The master pattern used for exposure, typically a light-blocking pattern on a quartz substrate (reflective for EUV). For reduction scanners it is made four times larger than the pattern on the wafer.

Japanese name: フォトマスク(レチクル)

Photomask (reticle) appears in 7 practice questions across 3 topics, including Lithography, Yield and quality control, Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets).

Field: Lithography

How a photomask is made

Mask making starts from a mask blank: a low-defect, very flat quartz plate 6 inches square and about 6 mm thick, coated with a chromium or MoSi absorber film and resist. An electron-beam writer exposes the pattern directly into the resist. Single-beam writers can take over ten hours for a complex mask, so advanced masks with heavy optical proximity correction (OPC) or curvilinear shapes use multi-beam writers with hundreds of thousands of beamlets. After development and etching, the mask is measured, inspected for defects, repaired where possible and fitted with a pellicle.

4x reduction and the reticle field

Scanners project the mask through reduction optics, so mask features are four times larger than on the wafer, which makes them easier to write and makes small mask errors shrink on the wafer. One exposure covers a field of about 26 mm by 33 mm, roughly 858 mm², on the wafer. This reticle limit caps the size of a single die, which is why very large processors approach it and why larger systems use chiplets. Silicon interposers bigger than one field are made by stitching wiring across adjacent exposures. High-NA EUV fields are half the standard size.

Binary, phase-shift and EUV masks

A binary mask simply blocks light with chromium. An attenuated phase-shift mask uses a partially transmitting MoSi film that also shifts the light phase by 180 degrees, sharpening the image at feature edges. Because EUV light at 13.5 nm is absorbed by almost every material, EUV masks are reflective: a multilayer stack of molybdenum and silicon acts as a mirror, and a patterned absorber on top defines dark regions. EUV blanks must be nearly free of defects, since a buried flaw in the multilayer cannot be removed later.

Defects and pellicles

Any defect on a mask prints in every field of every wafer exposed with it, appearing on wafer maps as a failure at the same position in each shot. A pellicle, a transparent membrane under 1 µm thick stretched on a frame about 6 mm above the patterned surface, keeps particles away from the focal plane so they are blurred and do not print. EUV pellicles must be only tens of nanometers thick, made of materials such as polysilicon or carbon nanotubes, to transmit around 90 percent of the light and survive heating.

Answer: C. To keep particles from landing directly on the mask, so that particles on the pellicle are out of focus and unlikely to print

A particle on a mask prints as a defect in every field of every wafer exposed with it. A pellicle is a transparent film under 1 µm thick stretched on a frame about 6 mm above the mask; particles landing on it are far out of focus and do not print. For EUV, ultrathin films tens of nm thick—polysilicon or carbon nanotubes—transmitting about 90% of EUV light and withstanding high temperatures have been developed.

FAQ

What is the difference between a photomask and a reticle?

Historically a photomask carried the pattern for an entire wafer at 1:1, while a reticle carried one or a few dies for a stepper that repeats it across the wafer. Today the terms are used almost interchangeably for the master plate used in scanners.

Why are photomask patterns four times larger than on the wafer?

Scanners use 4x reduction projection optics. Larger features are easier to write and inspect on the mask, and errors in mask dimensions are reduced by the same factor when printed.

What is a pellicle on a photomask?

It is a thin transparent membrane mounted a few millimeters above the mask pattern. Particles that land on it are out of focus during exposure and therefore do not print on the wafer.
