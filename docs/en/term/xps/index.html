What is XPS (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy)?

An analysis method that determines the elements in the top few nanometers of a surface and their chemical bonding states from the energies of photoelectrons emitted under X-ray irradiation.

Japanese name: XPS(X線光電子分光法)

Field: Metrology and analysis

Principle of XPS

XPS (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy), also called ESCA, is an analysis method that irradiates a sample with X-rays in ultra-high vacuum and measures the kinetic energy of the photoelectrons ejected by the photoelectric effect. Subtracting the photoelectron's kinetic energy and the instrument's work function from the X-ray energy gives the energy with which the electron was bound to its atom (the binding energy). Because binding energies take nearly fixed values for each element and orbital, peak positions in the spectrum identify the elements, and peak areas give an approximate composition. The X-ray source is typically, for example, aluminum Kα radiation (1486.6 eV).

Why chemical bonding states can be identified

Even for the same element, the binding energy of core electrons shifts slightly when the way the atom bonds to its neighbors changes. This is called the chemical shift. For example, the Si 2p peak appears at about 99 eV for elemental Si and about 103 eV for SiO2, so separating the peaks reveals the degree of oxidation and the intermediate oxidation states at the Si/SiO2 interface. Being able to distinguish oxides from metals, or nitrides from oxynitrides, is the major difference from the many analysis methods that identify only which elements are present.

Probing the top few nanometers

X-rays penetrate deep into the sample, but photoelectrons readily scatter and lose energy inside the solid, so only those generated within a few nanometers of the surface escape unchanged. XPS is therefore suited to evaluating native oxides, gate dielectrics, surfaces after cleaning or plasma treatment, and thin films and interfaces. Changing the photoelectron detection angle emphasizes information from shallower depths (angle-resolved XPS), while deeper regions are examined by depth profiling, which repeats measurements while sputtering the surface away gradually with argon or other ions. Note, however, that ion sputtering can alter the chemical state.

Sensitivity and comparison with other methods

XPS can detect almost every element except hydrogen and helium, but its detection limit is generally around 0.1–1 at%, so it is unsuited to trace impurity analysis. Impurity depth profiles at high sensitivity are measured with SIMS, and trace metal contamination on wafer surfaces with total-reflection X-ray fluorescence (TXRF) or VPD combined with ICP-MS. Auger electron spectroscopy (AES), which also measures electrons, can focus its electron beam finely and suits analysis of tiny areas, while chemical-state information is generally easier to obtain with XPS. On insulators the surface charges up and shifts the peaks, so measurements are made while neutralizing the charge, for example with low-energy electrons.

FAQ

What does XPS tell you?

It reveals which elements are present in the top few nanometers of a sample, their approximate composition and the chemical bonding state of each element. For example, it can tell whether silicon is elemental or oxidized from the shift in peak position (the chemical shift).

What is the difference between XPS and SIMS?

XPS ejects photoelectrons with X-rays and examines the elements and chemical bonding states at the surface. SIMS mass-analyzes secondary ions while sputtering the sample with ions, measuring impurity depth profiles with far higher sensitivity, but it does not reveal chemical bonding states. They are chosen according to the purpose.

How do you examine deeper regions with XPS?

By depth profiling: measurements are repeated while sputtering the surface away gradually with argon or other ions. For only the top few nanometers, angle-resolved XPS, which changes the detection angle, can examine depth differences without sputtering. Because ion sputtering can change the chemical state, the results must be interpreted with care.
