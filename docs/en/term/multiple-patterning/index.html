What is Multiple patterning?

Forming patterns with a pitch too fine for a single exposure by splitting them across several exposures or process steps. Splitting into two is double patterning.

Japanese name: マルチパターニング

Multiple patterning appears in 4 practice questions across 2 topics, including Lithography, Deposition (CVD and PVD).

Field: Lithography

Why one exposure is not enough

Resolution follows R = k1·λ/NA. Argon fluoride (ArF) immersion lithography combines a 193 nm wavelength with NA 1.35, and with k1 near its practical limit of about 0.27 the finest half-pitch is roughly 39 nm, a pitch of about 80 nm. Layers in advanced logic and memory need tighter pitches than that. Multiple patterning reaches them by building the final pattern from several exposures or from process steps that multiply a relaxed pattern, so no single exposure has to resolve the final pitch.

LELE: splitting the layout

In litho-etch-litho-etch (LELE), the layout is decomposed into two masks, often described as coloring, so that features closer than the single-exposure limit land on different masks; each is printed and etched in turn. Three masks (LELELE) extend the idea. Two catches dominate. Overlay error between exposures directly changes the spacing between neighboring features, demanding very tight alignment. And some layouts, such as three mutually close features, cannot be split into two colors, so design rules must forbid them.

SADP and SAQP: spacers set the pitch

Self-aligned double patterning (SADP) prints mandrel lines at a relaxed pitch, coats them with a conformal film, usually by atomic layer deposition (ALD), and etches it back to leave spacers on both sidewalls. Removing the mandrels leaves spacer lines at half the original pitch, with widths set by film thickness, giving excellent uniformity and no overlay between halves. Repeating the process gives self-aligned quadruple patterning (SAQP). The result is a regular grating, so additional cut or block masks remove unwanted segments, which pushes designs toward unidirectional lines.

Costs and the EUV alternative

Every extra mask, deposition and etch adds cost, cycle time and opportunities for defects, and each step contributes variation: in spacer schemes, for example, mandrel width errors make alternate spaces unequal, known as pitch walking. These costs drove adoption of extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography at 13.5 nm, which returns many layers to a single exposure. At the very tightest pitches, EUV itself can again be combined with multiple patterning.

Answer: B. Patterns with a pitch too fine to resolve in one exposure are formed in multiple exposure and processing steps

Double patterning forms pitches too fine for a single exposure either by splitting the layout into two interleaved patterns exposed and etched separately (LELE) or by doubling lines with sidewall spacers (SADP, SAQP). It pushed ArF immersion beyond its roughly 80 nm pitch limit into the 20 nm-plus range, but at a steep increase in steps, masks and cost, driving the adoption of EUV.

FAQ

What is the difference between LELE and SADP?

LELE splits the layout into two masks that are exposed and etched separately, so overlay between them matters. SADP uses sidewall spacers on a single printed pattern to halve the pitch in a self-aligned way, with line width set by film thickness.

Why is multiple patterning expensive?

Each layer needs extra masks, lithography passes, depositions, etches and inspections, which raise cost and cycle time and add sources of variation and defects.

Does EUV eliminate multiple patterning?

EUV allows many layers to be printed in a single exposure, greatly reducing the need for multiple patterning. At the finest pitches, however, EUV can still be combined with double patterning.
