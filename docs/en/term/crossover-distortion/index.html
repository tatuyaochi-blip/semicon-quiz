What is Crossover distortion?

Distortion in a class-B push-pull amplifier near 0 V, where neither transistor conducts during the handover between positive and negative half-cycles. Class-AB biasing with a small idle current reduces it.

Japanese name: クロスオーバーひずみ

Crossover distortion appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Bipolar transistors, Analog and electronic circuits.

Field: Bipolar transistors

Where the dead zone comes from

A class-B output stage pairs an NPN emitter follower for positive half-cycles with a PNP emitter follower for negative ones, with both bases driven by the same signal. A transistor conducts only when its base-emitter voltage reaches about 0.6 to 0.7 V, so for inputs between roughly −0.6 V and +0.6 V neither device conducts and the output stays near zero. The waveform is flattened at every zero crossing, creating a kink that produces higher-order harmonics, which are particularly audible.

Why it is worst at low signal levels

The dead zone is a fixed voltage band, so it represents a large fraction of a small signal but a tiny fraction of a large one. Unlike clipping, which grows with amplitude, crossover distortion is therefore most noticeable at low volume and in quiet passages, and percentage distortion figures measured at full power can hide it. It is also hard to remove completely with negative feedback, because the kink is sharp and loop gain falls at higher frequencies.

Class-AB biasing

The standard fix is to bias the two bases about two base-emitter drops apart, roughly 1.2 V, using two diodes or a VBE multiplier, so a small quiescent current flows through both transistors at idle. Each device then stays slightly on through the crossover region, and the handover becomes smooth. Small emitter resistors and thermal coupling of the bias element to the output transistors' heatsink keep the idle current stable as temperature rises, preventing thermal runaway.

Efficiency vs. distortion across amplifier classes

Class A conducts continuously, giving low distortion but a theoretical maximum efficiency of only 25% with a resistive load (50% with transformer coupling) and heat even without a signal. Class B idles at almost no current and reaches π/4, about 78.5%, but suffers crossover distortion. Class AB sits between them, behaving like class A for small signals and like class B for large ones, which is why most linear audio output stages use it. Class D switching amplifiers achieve higher efficiency still.

Answer: C. Crossover distortion

In a class-B push-pull stage, near zero input neither transistor reaches its ~0.6 V VBE, so there is a dead zone where the waveform distorts at the handover—crossover distortion, most noticeable at low volume. Biasing the bases about 1.2 V apart with diodes or a VBE multiplier lets a small idle current flow (class AB) and reduces it. Most audio amplifier output stages are class AB.

Answer: C. High power efficiency (theoretical maximum about 78.5%), but prone to crossover distortion

Class A always draws a large bias current, giving low distortion but a theoretical maximum efficiency of only 25% with a resistive load (50% transformer-coupled), and it heats up even without a signal. Class B draws almost no current at idle, reaching about 78.5% (π/4), but suffers crossover distortion at the handover. In practice, class AB with a small bias current and the even more efficient class D are used.

FAQ

How do you eliminate crossover distortion?

Bias the output transistors into class AB with diodes or a VBE multiplier so a small idle current flows, and place the output stage inside a negative-feedback loop to reduce the remaining error.

Does a class AB amplifier have crossover distortion?

Much less than class B, because both transistors conduct around zero crossing; some residual distortion remains if the bias current is too low or drifts with temperature.

Why is crossover distortion more noticeable at low volume?

The dead zone of about ±0.6 V is fixed, so it is a much larger share of a small signal than of a large one.
