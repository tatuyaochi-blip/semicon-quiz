What is FIT rate?

A unit of failure rate: 1 FIT is one failure per billion (10⁹) device-hours. It is used for parts with very low failure rates, such as semiconductors.

Japanese name: FIT

Field: Reliability

Definition of FIT

FIT (failures in time) is a unit of failure rate: 1 FIT is one failure per 10⁹ (one billion) device-hours. Device-hours are the number of parts multiplied by the hours of use. Expressed as a failure rate per hour, 1 FIT is 1×10⁻⁹ /h. Semiconductor failure rates are very low and become awkwardly small numbers in other units, so FIT is widely used. For example, using one million parts rated at 100 FIT for 10,000 hours is expected to produce 10⁶ × 10⁴ × 100 / 10⁹ = 1000 failures.

Relation to MTTF

In the random-failure period, where the failure rate λ is constant, the mean time to failure (MTTF) is the reciprocal of the failure rate, 1/λ. Since 100 FIT is 1×10⁻⁷ /h, the MTTF is 10⁷ hours, about 1140 years. This does not mean a single part lasts 1140 years; it means failures occur at this average rate when many parts are used. With a constant failure rate, the probability of surviving to time t is R(t) = exp(−λt), so by the MTTF more than 60% have failed. FIT also expresses the failure rate in the random-failure period, not when wear-out begins (the lifetime).

Estimating FIT

At failure rates this low, testing under actual use conditions produces almost no failures. Accelerated tests at raised temperature or voltage are therefore run on many samples, and an acceleration factor converts the result to device-hours under use conditions. Even with zero failures, the chi-square distribution gives an upper bound on the failure rate at a chosen confidence level. For example, testing 1000 units for 1000 hours with an acceleration factor of 80 and seeing no failures shows a failure rate of about 12 FIT or less at 60% confidence. Some FIT values, such as for soft errors caused by cosmic-ray neutrons, are estimated from accelerated tests using intense neutron beams.

Use at system level and related metrics

For a system of parts in series whose failure rates can be treated as constant, the FITs of the parts can be added to estimate the overall failure rate. With many parts, the total number of failures is not negligible even if each FIT is small. For example, using 10,000 parts at 100 FIT is expected to produce about 9 failures in one year (about 8,760 hours). In automotive functional safety (ISO 26262), the target guideline for PMHF, the metric for random hardware failures, is less than 10 FIT for ASIL D. FIT is a different metric from DPPM, which expresses the fraction of defective parts at shipment; FIT expresses the failure rate per unit time in use.

FAQ

How large a failure rate is 1 FIT?

One failure per 10⁹ device-hours, or 1×10⁻⁹ per hour. For example, using one million parts at 1 FIT for 1000 hours is expected to produce about one failure.

How are FIT and MTTF related?

With a constant failure rate, MTTF is the reciprocal of the failure rate. Since 1 FIT is 10⁻⁹ /h, the MTTF in hours is 10⁹ divided by the FIT value; at 100 FIT the MTTF is 10⁷ hours (about 1140 years). This is an average for the random-failure period, however, and does not represent a part's lifetime.

What is the difference between FIT and DPPM?

DPPM is the number of defective units per million shipped and expresses quality at the time of shipment. FIT is a failure rate expressing how many failures occur per 10⁹ device-hours in use, and expresses reliability.
