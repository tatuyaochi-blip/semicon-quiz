What is Sputtering?

A type of PVD in which ions in a plasma bombard a target and the ejected atoms are deposited on the wafer.

Japanese name: スパッタリング

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

How sputtering works

A target plate made of the film material sits at a negative potential of several hundred volts in a low-pressure argon plasma. Argon ions accelerate into the target and knock surface atoms loose by momentum transfer, and the ejected atoms travel to the wafer and condense into a film. No chemical reaction is needed, so sputtering is a form of physical vapor deposition (PVD). The sputter yield, atoms ejected per ion, is roughly 0.5 to 3 for argon at a few hundred eV and rises with ion energy, ion mass and oblique incidence. Deposition rate rises roughly in proportion to target power, so targets are water-cooled.

Magnetron and RF sputtering

In magnetron sputtering, magnets behind the target create a field crossing the electric field near its surface, trapping electrons in spiralling paths that ionize more argon. The dense plasma sustains high rates at about 0.1 to 1 Pa and reduces electron bombardment of the wafer. Erosion concentrates where the field is strongest, so magnets are rotated to use the target evenly. Conductive targets can use DC power, while insulating targets need RF power so that positive charge does not build up on their surface. Adding a reactive gas such as nitrogen deposits compounds like TiN from a metal target.

Sputtering vs. evaporation and CVD

Compared with vacuum evaporation, sputtering preserves alloy composition, because atoms are knocked out physically rather than boiled off at component-dependent rates, and sputtered atoms arrive with several to tens of eV instead of about 0.1 eV, giving denser films with better adhesion. Like evaporation, though, it is line-of-sight: bottom coverage in deep holes can fall below 10 percent, whereas CVD and ALD coat features conformally. Collimators and later ionized PVD, which ionizes sputtered atoms and pulls them vertically with substrate bias, improve bottom coverage. Thornton's structure zone model relates film microstructure to substrate temperature and gas pressure.

Where you will meet it

Aluminum wiring was deposited by sputtering aluminum alloy targets with about 0.5 percent copper to resist electromigration. In copper interconnects, sputtering deposits Ta or TaN barrier layers and the thin copper seed layer on which electroplating builds the wire, using copper targets of around 6N purity. TiN, titanium and various electrode metals are also sputtered. Targets need high purity and uniform grain structure because impurities and grain variations show up directly in the film.

Answer: A. PVD (physical vapor deposition)

Sputtering accelerates ions from an argon or similar plasma at several hundred volts into a target; atoms knocked out by momentum transfer deposit on the wafer. Since no chemical reaction is involved, it is classified as PVD (physical vapor deposition). It deposits metal wiring, barrier metals (Ta, TiN), Cu seed layers and electrodes. CVD relies on chemical reactions of precursor gases, and thermal oxidation oxidizes the silicon itself.

  2. BTo slow down deposition
  3. CTo change the color of the film

Answer: A. To improve film coverage at the bottom of deep holes and trenches (bottom coverage)

In conventional sputtering atoms arrive from many angles and seldom reach the bottoms of deep holes. Ionized PVD (iPVD) ionizes the sputtered atoms in a dense plasma and draws them vertically onto the wafer with a substrate bias, coating the bottoms of high-aspect-ratio holes; bottom film can even be resputtered onto the sidewalls. It forms barrier and seed layers for copper interconnects.

FAQ

What is the difference between sputtering and evaporation?

Evaporation heats the source until atoms vaporize, while sputtering ejects atoms by ion bombardment. Sputtering keeps alloy compositions closer to the target and gives denser, better-adhering films, while evaporation is simpler and gives very pure films.

Why is argon used in sputtering?

Argon is chemically inert, so it does not react with the film, and its ions are heavy enough to sputter most metals efficiently. It is also inexpensive and easy to ionize in a stable plasma.

Why does sputtering have poor step coverage?

Sputtered atoms travel in nearly straight lines and barely migrate after landing, so sidewalls and bottoms shadowed from the opening receive little film. Ionized PVD or CVD and ALD are used when deep features must be coated.
