What is Transmission gate?

An analog switch made of an nMOS and a pMOS connected in parallel. The nMOS passes 0 well and the pMOS passes 1 well, so both values pass without voltage loss.

Japanese name: トランスミッションゲート

Transmission gate appears in 2 practice questions across 1 topic, including MOSFET.

Field: MOSFET

Why a single transistor falls short

An nMOS switch with its gate at VDD passes a 0 fully, but when passing a 1 its source rises until the gate-source voltage drops to the threshold voltage, stopping near VDD − Vth, and the body effect lowers this level further. A pMOS switch is the mirror image: it passes a strong 1 but a weak 0 that stops near |Vth| above ground. A degraded high level can leave the next stage's pMOS partly on, wasting static current, so single-transistor switches are used with care.

How the complementary pair works

A transmission gate places an nMOS and a pMOS in parallel and drives their gates with complementary control signals, C and its inverse. When the gate is on, the nMOS handles signals near ground and the pMOS handles signals near VDD, so the combined switch passes the full range from rail to rail. The on-resistance of the pair also varies less across the signal range than either device alone. Because current can flow either way, the switch is bidirectional.

Where transmission gates are used

In digital logic, two transmission gates make a compact 2-to-1 multiplexer, and they appear in XOR gates, D latches and master-slave flip-flops, often with fewer transistors than a static CMOS equivalent. In analog and mixed-signal circuits they act as analog switches for routing signals, in sample-and-hold circuits and in switched-capacitor filters; the classic 4066 quad bilateral switch is built on this principle. A transmission gate needs an inverter, or a complementary signal, to generate its second control phase.

Limitations

A transmission gate is a passive switch with no gain, so it cannot restore a weak signal or drive a heavy load. Each switch adds series resistance and capacitance, and the delay of a chain grows roughly with the square of the number of stages, so buffers are inserted every few gates. Signals must stay within the supply rails, or the source and drain junctions become forward-biased. When the switch turns off, channel charge injection and clock feedthrough disturb the voltage on high-impedance nodes.

Answer: A. It can pass both 0 and 1 without voltage loss

A lone nMOS passes a 1 only up to VDD − Vth, and a lone pMOS passes a 0 only down to |Vth|. Placing them in parallel with complementary gate signals lets each cover the other's weakness, so both 0 and 1 pass without loss. Since signals can flow both ways, transmission gates are used in multiplexers, latches and analog switches (such as the 4066). They provide no drive, though, so long chains slow down and need buffers.

FAQ

What is the difference between a transmission gate and a pass transistor?

A pass transistor is a single nMOS or pMOS and loses a threshold voltage on one logic level; a transmission gate combines both types in parallel and passes 0 and 1 without that loss.

How many transistors does a transmission gate use?

Two, one nMOS and one pMOS, plus an inverter (two more transistors) if the complementary control signal is not already available.

Can a transmission gate pass analog signals?

Yes. It is bidirectional and passes voltages anywhere between the supply rails, which is why CMOS analog switches and multiplexers are built from transmission gates.
