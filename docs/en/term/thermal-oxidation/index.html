What is Thermal oxidation?

A method of forming an SiO2 film on silicon by heating it in oxygen or water vapor. There are dry and wet oxidation.

Japanese name: 熱酸化

Thermal oxidation appears in 16 practice questions across 2 topics, including Oxidation and diffusion, Deposition (CVD and PVD).

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

Dry versus wet oxidation

Dry oxidation reacts silicon with oxygen gas, Si + O2 → SiO2, while wet oxidation uses steam, Si + 2H2O → SiO2 + 2H2. Water dissolves in SiO2 several hundred times more than O2, so wet oxidation grows oxide several to ten times faster: about 70 nm dry versus 400 nm wet after one hour at 1000 °C. Wet oxide retains some hydrogen and is slightly less dense, so it suits thick isolation and masking layers. Dry oxide grows slowly with precise thickness control and a low density of interface states, which made it the classic gate oxide.

The Deal-Grove model

The Deal-Grove model treats oxidation as oxidant moving from the gas to the oxide surface, diffusing through the oxide and reacting with silicon at the Si/SiO2 interface. Balancing these fluxes gives x² + Ax = B(t + τ). While the oxide is thin, the interface reaction limits growth and thickness rises linearly with time at rate B/A. Once the oxide is thicker than roughly A/2, diffusion limits growth and thickness follows the square root of time, so doubling it takes about four times as long. Higher temperature or oxidant pressure speeds both regimes.

Silicon consumption and stress

Oxide grows by consuming the substrate: forming a film of thickness x uses about 0.44x of silicon, so 44 percent of the oxide lies below the original surface and 56 percent above. The roughly 2.2 times volume expansion leaves the oxide in compressive stress. Above about 950 °C the amorphous SiO2 can flow viscously and relax this stress; at lower temperatures stress retards oxidation at convex corners and leaves them sharp. Because new oxide always forms at a freshly created interface, thermal oxide has a cleaner interface than deposited oxide.

Practical process details

Oxidation runs at about 800 to 1,200 °C, usually in vertical furnaces holding around 100 wafers. The (111) surface oxidizes faster than (100), by about 1.5 times for thin oxides. Adding a few percent HCl getters sodium and metal contamination and suppresses oxidation-induced stacking faults, which grow from silicon interstitials injected during oxidation. Heavily doped polysilicon oxidizes faster and gives poorer oxide. Thermal SiO2 has a relative permittivity of about 3.9, the reference for equivalent oxide thickness (EOT). For thin, orientation-independent films, ISSG radical oxidation is used.

  3. CThickness is constant regardless of time

Answer: A. Thickness is roughly proportional to the square root of time (parabolic law)

The Deal-Grove model gives x² + Ax = B(t + τ). For thin oxides the interface reaction limits growth and thickness rises nearly linearly with time (x ≈ (B/A)t); for thick oxides oxidant diffusion through the film limits it and thickness follows the square root of time (x ≈ √(Bt)), so doubling thickness takes about four times as long. Thick oxides are therefore sped up with high temperature, steam and high pressure.

FAQ

How much silicon is consumed during thermal oxidation?

About 0.44 times the final oxide thickness. Growing 100 nm of SiO2 consumes roughly 44 nm of silicon, and the surface rises by about 56 nm.

Why is wet oxidation faster than dry oxidation?

Water molecules dissolve in SiO2 at a concentration several hundred times higher than oxygen, so far more oxidant reaches the Si/SiO2 interface. This makes wet oxidation several to ten times faster at the same temperature.

What is the difference between thermal oxide and CVD oxide?

Thermal oxide is grown by converting the silicon substrate itself, giving a dense film and a high-quality interface. CVD oxide is deposited from precursor gases on top of any surface without consuming silicon, which allows lower temperatures and thick films on non-silicon layers but gives a poorer interface.
