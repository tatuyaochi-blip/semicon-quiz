What is Specialty gases?

High-purity gases with toxic or flammable properties used in semiconductor manufacturing, such as silane, arsine and phosphine. They are supplied under strict control.

Japanese name: 特殊材料ガス

Field: Process materials (gases and chemicals)

What counts as a specialty gas

Fabs use two broad classes of gas. Bulk gases such as nitrogen, oxygen, argon and hydrogen are consumed in large volumes and are piped from on-site plants or bulk tanks. Specialty gases are used in smaller amounts for specific process steps and usually arrive in cylinders. They include deposition sources such as silane, dichlorosilane, ammonia and tungsten hexafluoride; dopant gases such as arsine, phosphine and diborane, often diluted in hydrogen or nitrogen; etch gases such as fluorocarbons, chlorine, hydrogen bromide and sulfur hexafluoride; and chamber-cleaning gases such as nitrogen trifluoride (NF3).

Why they are dangerous

Many specialty gases combine several hazards. Silane is pyrophoric: it can ignite spontaneously on contact with air. Arsine and phosphine are highly toxic at very low concentrations. Chlorine, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen bromide are corrosive, especially with moisture, and attack both people and gas lines. Others are flammable, oxidizing or asphyxiating, and several fluorinated gases such as NF3, SF6 and perfluorocarbons are potent greenhouse gases. Each gas therefore has its own handling rules, detectors and emergency response.

Purity and delivery

Purity is written as a count of nines: 6N means 99.9999%, leaving at most 1 ppm of total impurities, and 5N5 means 99.9995%. Moisture and oxygen are especially harmful because they corrode lines and degrade films. Cylinders sit in continuously exhausted gas cabinets with leak detectors that trigger alarms and automatic shutoff valves. Gas reaches tools through clean, electropolished stainless-steel lines, often double-walled for toxic gases, and lines are purged before and after every cylinder change by trained staff following written procedures.

After the process: abatement

Only part of the gas reacts in the chamber, so exhaust carries unreacted specialty gases and hazardous byproducts. Point-of-use abatement units beside each tool treat it by combustion, thermal or plasma decomposition, adsorption or water scrubbing before it joins the fab's central exhaust. Treating pyrophoric gases early also prevents duct fires, and removing byproducts that turn into solids, such as silica powder from silane, prevents clogging downstream.

Answer: B. House them in exhausted cylinder cabinets and monitor for leaks with gas detectors

Specialty gas cylinders are kept in continuously exhausted cylinder cabinets, with gas detectors monitoring for leaks. When a leak is detected, shutoff valves close automatically and alarms sound. Cylinder changes are done by trained personnel following procedures, with line purging before and after. Cylinders are never kept by workers' desks or used without proper piping.

FAQ

What is the difference between bulk gases and specialty gases in a fab?

Bulk gases such as nitrogen, oxygen, argon and hydrogen are used in large volumes and piped in, while specialty gases such as silane, arsine and NF3 are used in small amounts for specific steps, usually from cylinders, and are often toxic, flammable or corrosive.

What does 6N purity mean for a gas?

6N means six nines, 99.9999% purity, so total impurities are 1 ppm or less. A value such as 99.9995% is written 5N5.

Why is silane dangerous?

Silane is pyrophoric, meaning it can ignite spontaneously when it meets air, so leaks can start fires without any ignition source. It is handled in exhausted cabinets with leak detection and automatic shutoff.
