What is Radical?

A neutral atom or molecule with an unpaired electron that is highly reactive chemically. Plasmas contain far more radicals than ions, and they drive chemical etching and deposition.

Japanese name: ラジカル

Radical appears in 7 practice questions across 2 topics, including Etching, Oxidation and diffusion.

Field: Etching

How plasmas produce radicals

In a processing plasma, energetic electrons collide with feed-gas molecules. Some collisions ionize a molecule, but more of them simply break it apart into neutral fragments, because dissociation requires less energy than ionization; CF4 yields CF3 and F, and O2 yields atomic O. These fragments, with unpaired electrons, are radicals. Being uncharged, they are not pulled to the walls by the plasma sheath and survive longer than ions, so their density typically exceeds the ion density by two to three orders of magnitude or more.

Radicals vs. ions in etching

Radicals arrive at the wafer from all directions and react spontaneously with suitable surfaces; fluorine radicals, for instance, convert silicon into volatile SiF4. On its own this chemistry is isotropic and highly selective. Ions are fewer, but the sheath accelerates them vertically, so they strike horizontal surfaces with tens to hundreds of electronvolts, enhancing reactions there and clearing passivation from trench bottoms. Anisotropic etching relies on this division of labour: radicals supply most of the chemistry, ions decide where it proceeds fastest, and sidewall passivation protects the vertical walls.

Radical-only processes

Some steps deliberately exclude ions. In a downstream or remote plasma, the plasma is generated away from the wafer so ions recombine before arriving and only long-lived radicals reach the surface. This gives damage-free resist ashing and isotropic cleans. Radical oxidation uses oxygen radicals to grow good-quality oxide at about 400–600 °C with little dependence on crystal orientation, which makes it suitable for FinFET fins and 3D NAND structures and for steps with tight thermal budgets.

Controlling the radical flux

Radical density depends on gas chemistry, pressure, RF power and how quickly radicals are consumed. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) sources let engineers set plasma density, and with it radical and ion flux, separately from ion energy, and pulsed plasmas shift the mix of radical species. Consumption leads to loading effects: when a wafer exposes more etchable area, radicals are depleted and the etch rate falls. In deep, narrow features radicals must diffuse down the hole, so etch rate often decreases with aspect ratio.

Answer: A. It forms high-quality oxide even at relatively low temperatures, with little difference in rate between crystal orientations

Oxygen radicals generated by plasma or similar means are highly reactive, forming oxide comparable to thermal oxide at relatively low temperatures of about 400–600 °C. Compared with conventional oxidation, growth depends less on crystal orientation and slows less at corners, and radicals can even oxidize nitride. Radical oxidation is used for 3D structures such as FinFET fins and 3D NAND and in steps with tight thermal budgets.

FAQ

What is the difference between a radical and an ion in a plasma?

A radical is an electrically neutral atom or molecule with an unpaired electron, so it is chemically reactive but not steered by electric fields. An ion carries charge and is accelerated by the sheath toward the wafer, giving directional bombardment.

Why are there more radicals than ions in an etching plasma?

Breaking a molecule apart takes less electron energy than ionizing it, so dissociation happens more often. Radicals also live longer because they are not drawn to the walls, so they accumulate to much higher densities than ions.

Do radicals cause isotropic etching?

Etching by radicals alone is isotropic because neutral radicals arrive from all directions. Directionality comes from ion bombardment combined with sidewall passivation.
