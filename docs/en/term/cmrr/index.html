What is CMRR (common-mode rejection ratio)?

The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain in a differential amplifier. The larger it is, the better the circuit rejects noise that appears equally on both inputs.

Japanese name: CMRR(同相信号除去比)

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

Definition in numbers

A differential amplifier should respond only to the difference between its inputs. The common-mode rejection ratio is the ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain, CMRR = |Ad/Acm|, usually given in decibels as 20·log10 of that ratio. Op-amps typically offer 80–120 dB at low frequency. A useful way to read it: with 100 dB, a ratio of 100,000, a 1 V shift in the common-mode voltage looks like only about 10 µV of differential input error. Higher CMRR means better immunity to noise picked up equally on both wires, such as interference on long cables or ground differences.

What limits it inside the amplifier

In a differential pair, the tail current source splits its current between the two transistors according to the input difference; if both inputs move together, the split ideally stays the same. Real circuits fall short because the tail source has finite output impedance, so its current changes slightly with common-mode voltage, and because the two transistors and their loads are never perfectly matched, so that change is converted into a differential output. At higher frequencies, parasitic capacitance at the tail node lowers its impedance, which is why CMRR drops as frequency rises.

Resistor matching in difference amplifiers

A single op-amp with four resistors makes a simple difference amplifier, but its CMRR is usually limited by resistor matching rather than by the op-amp. In the worst case it is about (1 + G)/(4ε), where G is the differential gain and ε the resistor tolerance. With 1% resistors and unity gain, that is only about 50, or 34 dB. The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier fixes this: two input op-amps provide high input impedance and gain, raising the differential signal before a laser-trimmed or on-chip-matched difference stage, so CMRR above 100 dB is achievable.

CMRR vs. PSRR and common-mode range

Two related specifications are often confused with CMRR. The power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) describes how well the amplifier ignores changes in its supply voltage, not in its input common-mode voltage. The input common-mode voltage range is the span over which the inputs can sit while the amplifier still works properly at all; CMRR is only meaningful inside that range. Exceeding it, for example driving a non-rail-to-rail input near the supply, causes gross errors that no CMRR figure predicts. Datasheets give each of these specs separately, usually with graphs versus frequency.

Answer: B. It is good at rejecting noise that appears equally on both inputs (common-mode signals)

A differential amplifier ideally amplifies the difference between its inputs while rejecting components common to both (common-mode signals). CMRR is the ratio of differential to common-mode gain, typically 80–120 dB for op-amps. Higher CMRR means better immunity to common-mode noise such as pickup on long cables or supply fluctuations. CMRR falls at higher frequencies and is degraded by mismatched external resistors, so instrumentation amplifiers are used when precision matters.

FAQ

What is a good CMRR value?

General-purpose op-amps typically specify 80–120 dB at DC, and instrumentation amplifiers can exceed 100 dB. What matters is the value at the frequency of the interference you need to reject, since CMRR falls with frequency.

How do you convert CMRR from dB to a ratio?

Ratio = 10^(dB/20). For example, 80 dB is 10,000 and 100 dB is 100,000, meaning a common-mode change is attenuated by that factor relative to a differential signal.

Why is my difference amplifier's CMRR so low?

In a single op-amp difference amplifier, mismatch among the four resistors usually dominates; 1% resistors can limit CMRR to around 34 dB at unity gain. Use tightly matched resistor networks or an instrumentation amplifier.
