What is Ingot?

A cylindrical single-crystal block grown by the CZ method or similar, before it is cut into wafers. The ends are removed and the outside is ground to a uniform diameter before slicing.

Japanese name: インゴット

Field: Silicon wafers

How an ingot is grown

In the Czochralski (CZ) method, high-purity polysilicon is melted in a quartz crucible just above silicon's melting point of about 1414 °C. A seed crystal is dipped into the melt and slowly pulled upward while seed and crucible rotate in opposite directions, which evens out the temperature around the axis and yields a cylindrical crystal. A thin neck first lets dislocations grow out, then the diameter widens through the shoulder, or crown, into the constant-diameter body. A camera tracks the bright meniscus ring at the crystal–melt boundary, and pull rate and heater power are adjusted to hold diameter constant.

Doping and segregation along the ingot

Dopants are added to the melt: boron for p-type, phosphorus, arsenic or antimony for n-type. Most dopants have a segregation coefficient k below 1, meaning the growing crystal accepts less impurity than the melt holds, so the rejected dopant builds up in the shrinking melt and the later-grown part of the ingot is more heavily doped and lower in resistivity. Phosphorus has k of about 0.35, arsenic about 0.3 and antimony about 0.023, while boron's value of about 0.8 keeps p-type ingots fairly uniform. Only sections within the resistivity specification become product.

Crown, tail and defect control

Only the constant-diameter body is used; the crown and the tail are cut off. The tail is deliberately tapered into a cone before the crystal leaves the melt, because abrupt separation of a full-diameter crystal causes thermal shock that creates slip dislocations, which can propagate back into the body by roughly one diameter. Pull rate also affects crystal quality, because the ratio of growth rate to temperature gradient (V/G) decides whether vacancy- or interstitial-type point defects dominate, so diameter control must not compromise it. Oxygen dissolved from the quartz crucible is incorporated as well and is measured along the ingot.

From ingot to wafer

The ingot is cropped, ground to an exact diameter and given an orientation notch or, on older sizes, a flat. A multi-wire saw with hundreds of parallel wires about 0.1 mm thick then slices it into hundreds of wafers just under 1 mm thick in one run of several hours. The wafers are edge-beveled, lapped or ground for flatness, etched to remove the sawing damage layer and polished to a mirror finish before cleaning and inspection. Float-zone (FZ) growth, which uses no crucible, produces ingots with very low oxygen, favored for high-resistivity and some power devices.

  2. BMirror polishing → slicing → etching → lapping
  3. CEtching → mirror polishing → slicing → lapping

Answer: A. Slicing → lapping (grinding) → etching → mirror polishing

The ingot is ground to a uniform diameter and notched, then sliced to just under 1 mm thick with a multi-wire saw. After edge beveling, lapping or grinding sets thickness and flatness, etching removes the tens-of-µm damage layer, and double-side plus final polishing produce a nearly atomically smooth mirror surface. Wafers are then cleaned, inspected and shipped in clean shipping containers (FOSBs).

Which equipment is commonly used to slice a silicon ingot into many wafers at once?

  1. ADicing blade
  2. BMulti-wire saw
  3. CCMP tool
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Multi-wire saw

A multi-wire saw strings hundreds of parallel wires about 0.1 mm in diameter, running at high speed with abrasive slurry or fixed diamond, slicing an ingot into hundreds of wafers at once. The kerf is only about 0.1 mm-plus, reducing material waste, and cutting an ingot takes several hours. Dicing blades, by contrast, cut finished wafers into chips.

FAQ

Why are silicon wafers round?

The CZ crystal is pulled while rotating, so it grows as a cylinder and slices into round wafers. Round substrates also suit spin coating, polishing and other rotation-based processes.

How is a silicon ingot cut into wafers?

After grinding to diameter, the ingot is sliced with a multi-wire saw that cuts hundreds of wafers simultaneously using fine wires carrying abrasive slurry or fixed diamond. Lapping, etching and polishing then remove the saw damage.

Why does resistivity change from the top to the bottom of an ingot?

Most dopants segregate into the melt as the crystal grows, so the melt becomes richer in dopant over time and the bottom of the ingot ends up more heavily doped. Boron, with a segregation coefficient near 1, shows this effect least.
