What is SPFM (single-point fault metric)?

An ISO 26262 hardware metric expressing how small the share of single-point faults (which alone violate a safety goal) and residual faults is among safety-related faults. Typical targets are at least 90% for ASIL B, 97% for ASIL C and 99% for ASIL D.

Japanese name: SPFM(単一点故障指標)

The formula

SPFM equals one minus the summed failure rate of single-point and residual faults divided by the total failure rate of the safety-related hardware elements. Failure rates are expressed in FIT, failures per 10⁹ device-hours. If the safety-related hardware totals 1000 FIT and 5 FIT of it is single-point or residual, SPFM is 1 − 5/1000 = 99.5%, meeting the ASIL D guideline of at least 99%. Because it is a ratio, SPFM measures architectural robustness and does not by itself show whether the absolute risk is acceptable; that is the role of PMHF.

Single-point, residual and latent faults

A single-point fault occurs in an element with no safety mechanism and by itself violates the safety goal, such as an unprotected register in a steering controller's datapath. A residual fault is the portion of faults in a protected element that its safety mechanism fails to detect or control. Multiple-point faults violate a goal only in combination with another fault; the ones that stay hidden, latent faults, are assessed by the separate latent fault metric (LFM), with guideline targets of 60%, 80% and 90% for ASIL B, C and D.

How SPFM is computed: FMEDA

In a failure modes, effects and diagnostic analysis (FMEDA), each block, such as SRAM, CPU, clock generation and power supply, gets a failure rate from handbooks or test data. That rate is split by failure mode, the safe fraction that cannot violate the goal is removed, and the diagnostic coverage (DC) of the relevant safety mechanism is applied. The residual rate is roughly the failure rate times the unsafe fraction times one minus DC. Summing over all blocks gives SPFM, LFM and PMHF, and any shortfall points to the blocks needing better protection.

How designers raise it

The lever is diagnostic coverage on the blocks that dominate the residual rate. Typical mechanisms include error-correcting codes (ECC) on memories and buses, dual-core lockstep for CPUs, watchdog timers, voltage and clock monitors, and built-in self-tests. ISO 26262 broadly classes coverage as low, medium and high at about 60, 90 and 99%. Since an unprotected block with even a small failure rate can consume the budget, ASIL D designs aim to leave almost nothing without a mechanism.

Answer: C. 99% or higher

SPFM indicates how few of the safety-related faults are single-point faults, which by themselves lead to violation of a safety goal, or residual faults, which remain undetected by safety mechanisms; it is calculated as 1 minus the proportion of their combined failure rate. Target guidelines are 90% or higher for ASIL B, 97% or higher for ASIL C and 99% or higher for ASIL D. The latent fault metric (LFM) guidelines are 60% or higher for ASIL B, 80% or higher for ASIL C and 90% or higher for ASIL D, so 90% and 60% are easily confused with LFM or ASIL B values.

The total failure rate of safety-related hardware is 1000 FIT, of which single-point faults and residual faults (faults that remain undetected by safety mechanisms) total 5 FIT. What is the single-point fault metric (SPFM)?

  1. A99.5%
  2. B95%
  3. C99.95%
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 99.5%

SPFM is calculated as 1 minus the "sum of the single-point and residual fault failure rates" divided by the "total safety-related failure rate." In this example, 1 − 5 / 1000 = 1 − 0.005 = 0.995, or 99.5%. This meets the ASIL D guideline of 99% or higher. 95% is the value obtained by overestimating the proportion by an order of magnitude, and 99.95% by underestimating it by an order of magnitude. To raise SPFM, add safety mechanisms with high diagnostic coverage to reduce faults that remain undetected.

FAQ

What SPFM is required for ASIL D?

The ISO 26262 guideline is at least 99% for ASIL D, compared with 97% for ASIL C and 90% for ASIL B. ASIL A has no SPFM target.

What is the difference between SPFM and LFM?

SPFM covers faults that violate a safety goal on their own, directly or because a safety mechanism missed them. LFM covers multiple-point faults that remain hidden and could combine with a later fault.

How do I calculate SPFM from FIT rates?

Add the single-point and residual failure rates, divide by the total failure rate of safety-related hardware, and subtract the result from one. For 5 FIT out of 1000 FIT, SPFM is 99.5%.
