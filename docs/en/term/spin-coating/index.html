What is Spin coating?

Dispensing a liquid at the center of the wafer and spinning it at high speed so centrifugal force spreads a thin, uniform film. It is used for photoresist and spin-on dielectrics.

Japanese name: スピンコート

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

Stages of a spin coat

Liquid is dispensed at the center of the wafer, either while it is stationary or slowly turning. The wafer then accelerates and the liquid spreads outward to cover the surface. At the final speed, typically thousands of revolutions per minute, centrifugal force flings excess liquid off the edge and the film thins rapidly. As it thins, solvent evaporation takes over, the viscosity rises, and the film sets. A bake afterward, called a soft bake or post-apply bake for photoresist, drives out remaining solvent before the next step.

Controlling thickness and uniformity

Final thickness depends on the liquid's viscosity and solids content and on spin speed, scaling roughly as the inverse square root of speed, so engineers use a measured spin curve to choose speed for a target. Solvent evaporation rate, set by exhaust flow, temperature and humidity, also matters. Within-wafer variation of a few nanometers is achievable. For photoresist, thickness control is especially important because light reflecting inside the film causes linewidth to oscillate with thickness, a behavior known as the swing curve.

Edge bead and backside rinse

Surface tension leaves a thicker rim of material, the edge bead, around the wafer perimeter, and some liquid can wrap around to the back side. Left in place, this material can flake off as particles during handling, contaminate chucks and scanner stages, and upset focus near the edge. Coater tracks therefore spray solvent onto the outer few millimeters while spinning, called edge bead removal (EBR), and rinse the backside. Optical edge exposure of resist is an alternative way to clear the rim.

Uses and limitations

Spin coating applies photoresist, bottom anti-reflective coatings, spin-on carbon hard masks, spin-on glass and polyimide passivation. It is fast and gives excellent uniformity on flat round wafers, but most of the dispensed liquid is thrown off, so material efficiency is low. Over topography the film partially planarizes, ending up thicker in trenches and thinner over raised features. Square or very large substrates and deep structures are better served by alternatives such as slit coating or spray coating.

Left in place, it flakes off as particles during handling, contaminates chucks and scanner stages, and disturbs focus at the periphery, so solvent is applied to the outer few mm to remove it (edge bead removal, EBR). A backside rinse also removes resist that has wrapped onto the back of the wafer.

FAQ

How does spin speed affect film thickness?

Higher speed gives a thinner film, with thickness falling roughly in proportion to one over the square root of spin speed for a given liquid. Viscosity and solids content set the overall thickness range.

What is edge bead removal?

It is a step in which solvent is applied to the outer few millimeters of a spinning wafer to remove the thick rim of resist left by spin coating, preventing particles and contamination of tools.

Why is spin coating wasteful?

Most of the dispensed liquid is flung off the wafer during spinning, so only a small fraction ends up in the film. Dispense volume and recipe are optimized to reduce waste of expensive resists.
