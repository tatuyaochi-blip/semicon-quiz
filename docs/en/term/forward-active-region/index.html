What is Forward-active region?

The state with the base-emitter junction forward-biased and the base-collector junction reverse-biased. A collector current proportional to the base current flows, and it is used for amplification.

Japanese name: 能動領域

Field: Bipolar transistors

Bias conditions of the active region

The active region (forward-active region) is the operating state of a bipolar transistor in which the base-emitter junction is forward-biased and the base-collector junction is reverse-biased. In an NPN transistor, most of the electrons injected from the emitter into the base cross the thin base without recombining and are swept into the collector by the strong field of the base-collector junction. For a silicon NPN the rule of thumb is a VBE of about 0.6–0.7 V with the collector at a higher potential than the base (VCE > VBE). With both junctions forward-biased the transistor is in saturation; with both reverse-biased it is in cutoff.

What sets the collector current

In the active region the collector current IC depends exponentially on the base-emitter voltage, IC ≈ IS·exp(VBE/VT), where VT is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at room temperature. It follows that IC rises about tenfold for every 60 mV or so of VBE. The base current IB is roughly IC/hFE, giving IC = hFE × IB. Raising the collector voltage barely changes IC, so the output characteristics show nearly horizontal lines, one per IB, and their slight upward slope is the Early effect.

Why it is used for amplification

In the active region a small change in input voltage produces a large change in current. The small-signal transconductance is gm = IC/VT, about 38 mS at room temperature for IC = 1 mA. With a load resistor on the collector the voltage gain is about gm × the load resistance, and the maximum gain of the transistor alone is gm·ro = VA/VT, where VA is the Early voltage. Amplifiers place the operating point near the middle of the active region so that signal swings do not push the transistor into saturation or cutoff. Current mirrors, which copy a current, also rely on active-region operation.

How it maps to MOSFET regions

The naming against MOSFETs is confusing. The bipolar saturation region, with both junctions forward-biased and VCE down to about 0.1–0.3 V, is the switch-on state and plays a role closer to the MOSFET's linear (triode) region. Conversely, the bipolar active region corresponds to the MOSFET's 'saturation region', where current barely depends on the output voltage; both are used for amplification. There is also a reverse-active region with the roles of emitter and collector swapped, but because the structure is asymmetric the current gain is small and it is normally not used.

FAQ

What is the difference between the active, saturation and cutoff regions?

They are distinguished by how the two junctions are biased. Base-emitter forward and base-collector reverse is the active region (amplification); both forward is saturation (switch on); both reverse is cutoff (switch off).

Which MOSFET region corresponds to the bipolar active region?

The MOSFET saturation region. In both, output current depends little on the output-side voltage and is set by the input (VBE or VGS), so both are used for amplification. Note that bipolar 'saturation', despite the same name, means the switch-on state.

Is the active region the same as the active area?

No. The active region is an operating state (bias condition) of a bipolar transistor. The active area in layout and process terms is the part of the silicon, surrounded by isolation, where transistors are built.
