What is Silicon photonics?

Technology for integrating optical circuits such as waveguides, modulators and photodetectors on silicon. It uses the materials and processes of CMOS fabs, enabling volume production on 200 mm or 300 mm wafers. Because silicon emits light poorly, lasers are added using compound semiconductors.

Japanese name: シリコンフォトニクス

Field: Silicon photonics

What gets built on the chip

Silicon photonics starts from an SOI (silicon-on-insulator) wafer whose top silicon layer, commonly 220 nm thick, is patterned into waveguides, with a 2–3 µm buried oxide as the lower cladding. Silicon is transparent at the 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm telecom bands, so it guides light with modest loss. On the same wafer go modulators based on carrier dispersion in pn junctions, germanium photodetectors grown by selective epitaxy, and passive filters such as ring resonators, arrayed waveguide gratings (AWGs) and Mach-Zehnder interferometers that combine or separate wavelengths.

Why CMOS fabs suit it

Every main step reuses CMOS tooling: lithography and dry etching define waveguides, ion implantation forms modulator junctions, selective epitaxy grows germanium, and metal interconnects wire it up. That allows precise, reproducible volume production on 200 mm and 300 mm wafers. Feature sizes are a few hundred nanometres, so DUV lithography is sufficient and leading-edge logic nodes are not needed. What matters more is nanometre-level control of width and thickness, because small dimensional errors shift the wavelengths at which resonant devices operate.

The light-source problem

Silicon is an indirect-gap semiconductor: the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum sit at different crystal momenta, so radiative recombination needs a phonon and is far less likely than non-radiative recombination. Efficient lasers therefore come from III-V compound semiconductors such as InP, either bonded onto the silicon wafer (heterogeneous integration) or attached as separate chips. Because III-V materials raise contamination concerns, they are usually processed apart from the CMOS line. Detection uses germanium because silicon barely absorbs beyond about 1.1 µm; discrete receivers often use InGaAs instead.

Where it is used

The main application is optical transceivers for data-center and AI networks, where wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) puts separate signals on several wavelengths in one fiber, for example four 100 Gb/s lanes for 400 Gb/s. Integration also enables co-packaged optics, which place optical engines beside a switch chip. Silicon nitride waveguides, with an index near 2.0, are often added to the same platform for lower loss and lower temperature sensitivity, though silicon nitride cannot form carrier-based modulators because it is an insulator.

In silicon photonics, why are compound semiconductors combined with silicon for the laser light source?

  1. ABecause silicon does not transmit light at all
  2. BBecause compound semiconductors are always cheaper
  3. CBecause silicon has an indirect band gap and cannot emit light efficiently

Answer: C. Because silicon has an indirect band gap and cannot emit light efficiently

Silicon photonics builds waveguides, modulators and germanium photodetectors precisely and in volume in CMOS fabs, but silicon's indirect gap rules out an efficient light source. InP-based lasers are therefore bonded onto silicon wafers (heterogeneous integration) or attached as separate chips. Driven by optical interconnects for AI data centers, co-packaged optics and related technologies are advancing rapidly.

What is the main role of the buried oxide (BOX) layer in the SOI wafers used in silicon photonics?

  1. AIt acts as the absorption layer of a photodetector beneath the waveguide, absorbing telecom-wavelength light and converting it into current
  2. BIt acts as a light-emitting layer that generates light in the top silicon layer
  3. CIt serves as the lower cladding of the waveguide, preventing light from leaking into the silicon substrate
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. It serves as the lower cladding of the waveguide, preventing light from leaking into the silicon substrate

An SOI (Silicon on Insulator) wafer consists of a silicon substrate topped with a buried oxide (BOX) and then a thin single-crystal silicon layer. Waveguides are formed by patterning the top silicon layer (220 nm thick is common), and the low-index BOX serves as the lower cladding. If the BOX is too thin, light extending outside the waveguide (the evanescent field) leaks into the high-index silicon substrate, so a thick BOX of about 2–3 µm is commonly used. Oxide has a large band gap and neither absorbs nor emits light.

FAQ

Why can't silicon be used to make lasers?

Silicon has an indirect band gap, so an electron and hole can only recombine radiatively with the help of a phonon, which is rare. Silicon photonics therefore uses III-V lasers such as InP, bonded to the chip or supplied from outside.

What is used as the photodetector in silicon photonics?

Germanium grown by selective epitaxy on the silicon, because germanium absorbs at 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm while silicon is transparent there. Standalone receivers often use InGaAs.

Does silicon photonics need advanced process nodes?

No. Waveguides are a few hundred nanometres wide, so mature DUV lithography is enough. The demanding part is tight control of dimensions and thickness rather than minimum feature size.
