What is Waveguide?

A structure that confines and guides light by surrounding a high-index core with a lower-index cladding. Silicon photonics commonly uses silicon about 0.5 µm × 0.22 µm in cross section surrounded by oxide, allowing bend radii of just a few µm.

Japanese name: 導波路

Field: Silicon photonics

How a waveguide confines light

Light launched along a high-index core is held there by total internal reflection at the boundary with the lower-index cladding; the guided pattern of the field is called a mode. A small part of the field, the evanescent tail, always extends into the cladding. Confinement grows with index contrast: silicon (about 3.48 at 1.55 µm) against oxide (about 1.45) differs by roughly 2, whereas the core and cladding of optical fiber differ by less than 1%. That is why a silicon strip only about 0.5 µm × 0.22 µm can guide light around bends of a few µm radius.

Why silicon works at telecom wavelengths

A photon at 1.55 µm carries about 1.24 / 1.55 ≈ 0.8 eV, less than silicon's band gap of about 1.12 eV, so it cannot excite band-to-band transitions and silicon is nearly transparent. Silicon only absorbs strongly below about 1.1 µm, which is also why a separate material such as germanium is needed for detectors. On an SOI (silicon-on-insulator) wafer the waveguide is patterned in the thin top silicon layer, commonly 220 nm, and the buried oxide (BOX), typically 2–3 µm thick, acts as the lower cladding so the evanescent field does not leak into the silicon substrate.

Strip vs. rib waveguides

A strip waveguide is etched all the way through the silicon layer. Its large lateral contrast gives tight confinement and the smallest bends. A rib waveguide is etched only partway, leaving thin slabs on both sides. Confinement is weaker, so sharp bends leak light into the slab, but the slabs can be doped to carry current from electrodes placed well away from the optical mode to a pn junction in the core. Metal right next to the waveguide would absorb light, so modulators and other electrically driven devices are usually built on rib geometry.

Loss budgets in dB

The same high contrast that allows tight bends also makes the mode sensitive to sidewall roughness, which scatters light. Typical strip-waveguide loss is about 1–3 dB/cm depending on the process, with rib or optimized processes lower, against roughly 0.2 dB/km for fiber. Losses are tracked in decibels because they add along a path: transmittance T = 10^(−L/10). Two decibels per centimeter over 5 mm gives 1 dB, or about 79% of the power remaining; 3 dB is roughly half and 10 dB one-tenth. Fiber-to-chip coupling through a grating or edge coupler is usually a larger item in the budget than the waveguide itself.

Answer: C. About 0.8 eV. It is smaller than silicon's band gap, so it propagates with almost no absorption

1.24 ÷ 1.55 ≈ 0.80 eV, which is smaller than silicon's band gap of about 1.12 eV, so it cannot lift valence-band electrons into the conduction band and no band-to-band absorption occurs. Silicon is therefore nearly transparent at telecom wavelengths and can serve as a waveguide material. Conversely, silicon alone cannot make a photodetector for this wavelength. Silicon absorbs light with wavelengths shorter than about 1.1 µm (energies above 1.12 eV). About 1.9 eV is the result of multiplying 1.24 by 1.55, and corresponds to the energy of red light at a wavelength of about 650 nm.

  1. AThe large index contrast lets light leak out easily, so the waveguide can only be bent at radii larger than an optical fiber's, making it unsuitable for integration
  2. BThe large index contrast confines light tightly, so it can be bent at small radii of a few µm and devices can be packed densely
  3. CThe larger the index contrast, the less sensitive it is to sidewall roughness, so propagation loss becomes as low as an optical fiber's
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The large index contrast confines light tightly, so it can be bent at small radii of a few µm and devices can be packed densely

Light is confined by total internal reflection at the boundary between the high-index core and the low-index surroundings (cladding), and the larger the index contrast, the tighter the confinement. The difference between silicon and oxide is about 2, orders of magnitude larger than the core–cladding difference in optical fiber (less than 1%), allowing thin waveguides with cross-sections of about 0.5 µm × 0.22 µm and bend radii down to a few µm. On the other hand, the larger the index contrast, the more sidewall roughness scatters light, so propagation loss—though condition-dependent—is about 1–3 dB/cm for a typical strip waveguide (lower for rib waveguides or low-loss processes), far greater than fiber's (about 0.2 dB/km).

FAQ

Why is silicon transparent at 1550 nm?

A 1550 nm photon has about 0.8 eV of energy, below silicon's 1.12 eV band gap, so it cannot lift electrons across the gap and is not absorbed by band-to-band transitions. Silicon only absorbs strongly at wavelengths shorter than about 1.1 µm.

What is the difference between a grating coupler and an edge coupler?

A grating coupler diffracts light up to a fiber held above the chip, so it can sit anywhere and be tested on the wafer, but its bandwidth is only a few tens of nm and it is polarization sensitive. An edge coupler butts a fiber against a polished facet, usually through an inverse taper that enlarges the mode, giving lower, broadband loss at the cost of facet processing.

Why are silicon waveguides polarization dependent?

A flat waveguide about 0.5 µm wide and 0.22 µm thick confines TE (horizontal) and TM (vertical) polarizations very differently, so their effective indices and losses differ. Standard single-mode fiber does not hold polarization, so receivers use polarization splitter-rotators or two-dimensional grating couplers.
