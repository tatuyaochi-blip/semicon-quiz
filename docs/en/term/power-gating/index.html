What is Power gating?

A low-power technique that switches off the supply to unused blocks, eliminating nearly all their leakage. State saving and wake-up time must be considered.

Japanese name: パワーゲーティング

Power gating appears in 6 practice questions across 3 topics, including Power Management, MOSFET, IC design flow and EDA.

Field: Power Management

How power gating is implemented

Power switches, also called sleep transistors, are placed between a block and its supply: header switches are PMOS devices between the main supply and the block's local virtual supply rail, and footer switches are NMOS devices on the ground side. They use high-threshold transistors so they leak little when off, and they are distributed in large numbers across the block to keep resistance low. When the block is off, isolation cells clamp its outputs to defined logic levels so floating signals do not disturb the blocks that remain powered.

Saving state

Switching off the supply erases flip-flop and memory contents. Designs either accept losing state and reinitialize, save important state to memory in an always-on domain before shutdown, or use retention flip-flops whose small extra latch stays powered from an always-on supply while the main flip-flop is off. SRAMs may instead use a retention mode with lowered voltage that keeps data but cuts most leakage. The power controller that sequences isolation, state saving and switch control must itself sit in an always-on domain.

Costs and the break-even point

Switches add series resistance, so the block sees some voltage drop and runs slightly slower, and they take area. Turning on causes inrush current as the block's capacitance charges, so switches are enabled gradually, typically weak ones first and then the strong ones, to avoid sagging the shared supply. Waking up therefore takes far longer than resuming from clock gating. Each off-on cycle also costs energy, so shutting down only pays off when the idle period exceeds a break-even time over which the saved leakage energy outweighs that overhead.

Power gating vs. clock gating and DVFS

Clock gating stops the clock so a block stops switching, removing dynamic power and resuming within a cycle, but leakage continues because the supply is still connected. Power gating removes leakage too, at the price of state handling and wake-up latency. DVFS lowers voltage and frequency for blocks that keep working. Real chips combine all three: clock gating for idle periods of a few cycles, power gating for idle CPU cores, GPUs and accelerators in deep sleep states, and DVFS for running blocks.

Answer: B. Clock gating cuts dynamic power and resumes instantly; power gating also cuts leakage but takes time to resume

Clock gating only stops the clock, so state is retained and operation resumes on the next clock, but leakage continues. Power gating cuts power and so also eliminates leakage, but needs time to save and restore state and for the supply to stabilize. They are combined: clock gating for idle periods of a few cycles, power gating for long ones.

FAQ

What is the difference between power gating and clock gating?

Clock gating stops the clock to cut dynamic power and resumes almost instantly, but leakage continues. Power gating disconnects the supply, removing leakage as well, but loses state unless it is saved and takes longer to wake.

What is an isolation cell in power gating?

It is a gate on the outputs of a switchable domain that forces them to a fixed logic value while the domain is off, so undefined floating signals do not reach powered logic.

Why is power gating not used for short idle periods?

Each shutdown and wake-up spends energy on recharging the block and saving and restoring state, and it takes time. If the idle period is shorter than the break-even time, the leakage saved does not repay that overhead.
