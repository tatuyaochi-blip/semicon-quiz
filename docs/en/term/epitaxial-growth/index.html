What is Epitaxial growth?

A technique for growing a single-crystal thin film on a single-crystal substrate with the same crystal orientation as the substrate.

Japanese name: エピタキシャル成長

Epitaxial growth appears in 19 practice questions across 4 topics, including Epitaxial growth, Process materials (gases and chemicals), Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA).

Field: Epitaxial growth

How epitaxy copies the substrate crystal

In epitaxial growth, atoms arriving at a clean single-crystal surface settle onto lattice sites that continue the substrate's arrangement, extending the crystal upward. The surface must be clean: a native oxide about 1 nm thick or carbon contamination seeds defects such as stacking faults, so silicon wafers are typically baked in hydrogen near 1100 °C to evaporate the oxide as SiO. Growing the same material as the substrate is homoepitaxy; growing a different one, such as SiGe on silicon or GaN on sapphire or SiC, is heteroepitaxy, which must manage lattice and thermal-expansion mismatch.

Silicon epitaxy chemistry and growth regimes

Silicon epitaxy by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) uses trichlorosilane, dichlorosilane or silane diluted in a large hydrogen flow, as in SiHCl3 + H2 → Si + 3HCl. Hydrogen carries the precursor, keeps the surface reduced and removes chlorine as HCl; chlorinated chemistries suppress nucleation on oxide, enabling selective growth on exposed silicon only. At lower temperatures the surface reaction limits growth, so the rate follows an Arrhenius dependence and temperature uniformity sets thickness uniformity. At higher temperatures gas transport limits growth and flow uniformity dominates. On 300 mm wafers, lamp-heated single-wafer reactors hold variation to about ±1–2%.

Doping during growth

Adding phosphine, arsine or diborane to the gas stream dopes the layer in situ, avoiding implant damage and activation anneals. Because doping changes layer by layer, epitaxy can create transitions within a few nanometers, buried heavily doped layers and even profiles that are lighter at the surface, which thermal diffusion cannot do. A competing effect is autodoping: dopant evaporating from a heavily doped substrate, including its back side and edges, is reincorporated through the gas phase. Sealing the backside with oxide or polysilicon, purging before growth and growing at reduced pressure, which speeds gas diffusion, suppress it.

Where epitaxy is used

Epitaxial layers have fewer defects than Czochralski (CZ) wafers and doping independent of the substrate, so they form lightly doped drift layers in power devices and substrates for CMOS image sensors. In FinFETs, raised source/drain crystals of boron-doped SiGe (pMOS) and phosphorus-doped silicon (nMOS), reaching around 10^21 cm^-3, lower resistance and strain the channel, and must grow at about 600–700 °C or lower to respect the thermal budget. SiC epitaxy uses substrates about 4° off-axis so step-flow growth preserves the 4H polytype, and LEDs and lasers depend on epitaxial multilayer stacks.

Answer: C. To reduce parasitic resistance and apply strain to the channel

Fins are only a few nm wide, so source/drain resistance would be high and contact area small. Epitaxially growing diamond-shaped raised crystals enlarges the cross-section, lowering resistance and easing contact formation. pMOS uses Ge-rich SiGe and nMOS uses heavily phosphorus-doped SiP; their lattice mismatch also strains the channel to boost mobility.

Answer: A. Growing a single-crystal thin film on a single-crystal substrate with the same crystal orientation as the substrate

In epitaxial growth, atoms stack up following the substrate's crystal arrangement, forming a single-crystal film with the same orientation. Doping and even material can be chosen independently of the substrate, and the layer has fewer defects than CZ wafers, so epitaxy is used for lightly doped drift layers in power devices, CMOS image sensor substrates, SiGe source/drains in transistors, and the multilayer stacks of LEDs and lasers.

FAQ

What is the difference between homoepitaxy and heteroepitaxy?

Homoepitaxy grows a layer of the same material as the substrate, such as silicon on silicon. Heteroepitaxy grows a different material, such as SiGe on silicon or GaN on sapphire, and must cope with mismatched lattice constants and thermal expansion.

What is selective epitaxial growth?

Selective epitaxy deposits crystal only on exposed semiconductor surfaces and not on surrounding oxide or nitride. Chlorine-containing chemistries help by suppressing nucleation on the dielectric, and the technique is used for raised source/drain regions.

Why use epitaxial wafers instead of ordinary polished wafers?

An epitaxial layer has fewer crystal defects than bulk CZ silicon and its doping can be chosen independently of the substrate, for example a lightly doped layer on a heavily doped wafer for power devices.
