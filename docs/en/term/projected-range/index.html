What is Projected range?

The average depth at which implanted ions come to rest. It is deeper with higher acceleration energy and with lighter ions.

Japanese name: 投影飛程

Projected range appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Ion implantation.

Field: Ion implantation

What projected range is

Implanted ions zigzag through silicon, repeatedly colliding with nuclei and electrons, until they lose their energy and stop. The length of the path an ion actually travels is its range (total range) R, and the average of that path projected onto the incident direction is the projected range Rp. Because the path bends, Rp is shorter than R. Stopping depths vary from ion to ion, and their standard deviation is called ΔRp (the straggle). If the depth profile is approximated as a Gaussian, Rp gives the depth of the peak and ΔRp a measure of its width.

Dependence on energy and ion species

Rp is set mainly by acceleration energy and grows deeper as energy rises. As a guide, boron implanted into silicon reaches about 30–40 nm at 10 keV and about 0.3 µm at 100 keV. At the same energy lighter ions go deeper: at 50 keV, phosphorus (mass 31) reaches about 60 nm and arsenic (mass 75) about 30 nm, because heavier ions lose more energy per collision and stop shallower. Tables of Rp and ΔRp for each ion-substrate combination, calculated with theory (LSS theory) or Monte Carlo simulation, are available.

How ions stop and the shape of the profile

Ions lose energy through collisions with nuclei (nuclear stopping) and interactions with electrons (electronic stopping). While fast, they lose energy mainly by electronic stopping and slow down while traveling nearly straight; once slow, nuclear stopping dominates, deflecting them sharply and knocking atoms out of place. Crystal damage therefore peaks a little shallower than Rp, at about 0.7–0.8 Rp. Real profiles are often asymmetric, and channeling, in which ions travel deep along crystal rows, stretches a tail on the deep side, so precise calculations use Pearson distributions and the like.

Estimating junction depth and mask thickness

Rp and ΔRp allow process design values to be estimated. For a Gaussian, the peak concentration Np is roughly dose ÷ (2.5 × ΔRp), and the junction depth, where the concentration equals the substrate concentration NB, is Rp + ΔRp × √(2 ln(Np/NB)). If Np is 10,000 times NB, for example, the junction lies about 4.3 ΔRp deeper than Rp. A mask must be thick enough that ions cannot punch through: Rp in the mask material plus several standard deviations. There is also lateral spread comparable to ΔRp, so dopant reaches under mask edges.

Answer: B. Gaussian distribution; the peak depth is called the projected range (Rp)

Implanted profiles approximate a Gaussian whose peak depth is the projected range Rp and whose width is the straggle ΔRp; peak concentration is roughly dose ÷ (2.5 × ΔRp). Rp and ΔRp depend on energy and the ion and substrate species and are tabulated. Real profiles are often asymmetric, with channeling tails extending deeper, so more elaborate distribution functions and simulations are used.

FAQ

What is the difference between projected range and range?

The range (total range) is the length of the zigzag path an ion actually travels, while the projected range is the average of that path projected onto the incident direction. Because the path bends, the projected range is shorter than the range. Implant depth is normally discussed in terms of the projected range Rp.

What is ΔRp?

The standard deviation of the depths at which implanted ions stop, also called the straggle. It sets the width of the Gaussian approximation, and the peak concentration is roughly dose ÷ (2.5 × ΔRp).

How do you make the projected range deeper?

Mainly by raising the acceleration energy. If the implanter's voltage is limited, doubly or triply charged ions gain two or three times the energy at the same voltage. At the same energy, lighter ions go deeper. Conversely, shallower implants combine lower energies, molecular ions and channeling suppression.
