What is Lattice mismatch?

A difference in the atomic spacing (lattice constant) between a grown film and its substrate. A large mismatch causes strain and defects such as dislocations.

Japanese name: 格子不整合

Lattice mismatch appears in 3 practice questions across 3 topics, including Epitaxial growth, Compound semiconductors, Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA).

Field: Epitaxial growth

How mismatch is quantified

Lattice mismatch is usually written as a fraction or percentage, f = (a_film − a_substrate) / a_substrate, where a is the lattice constant of each material. A positive value means the film's natural spacing is larger than the substrate's, so a coherently grown film is squeezed in-plane (compressive strain); a negative value means it is stretched (tensile strain). Germanium on silicon is about 4% mismatched, GaN on SiC about 3.5%, while pairs such as AlAs on GaAs are nearly matched.

Critical thickness and misfit dislocations

A thin mismatched film can still grow as a single crystal by elastically distorting to adopt the substrate's in-plane spacing; this is called pseudomorphic or coherent growth. The stored strain energy grows with thickness, and beyond a critical thickness it becomes favorable to form misfit dislocations at the interface, which relax the strain. The larger the mismatch, the thinner the critical thickness. Some dislocations do not stay at the interface but thread up through the film as threading dislocations, which act as leakage paths and nonradiative recombination centers, reducing light output and device lifetime.

Living with large mismatch

When a device needs a film on a poorly matched substrate, growers trap or filter the defects instead of trying to avoid them entirely. Compositionally graded buffer layers change the lattice constant step by step so dislocations are spread out and confined to the buffer. Strained superlattices bend threading dislocations sideways so they do not reach the active layer. Selective-area growth through windows in a mask limits the area over which defects can propagate. Choosing a closer-matched substrate, such as SiC instead of sapphire for GaN, also helps. Lattice mismatch should not be confused with thermal-expansion mismatch, which creates strain during cooling after growth.

Mismatch as a design tool

Controlled mismatch below the critical thickness is deliberately exploited as strain engineering. In advanced CMOS, the source and drain regions are regrown epitaxially with materials of a different lattice constant: Ge-rich SiGe for pMOS, whose larger lattice compresses the channel, and heavily phosphorus-doped silicon (SiP) for nMOS, which tends toward tensile strain. The resulting channel strain raises carrier mobility. In FinFETs the raised source/drain epitaxy also enlarges the contact area and lowers parasitic resistance. Strained quantum wells in semiconductor lasers use the same idea to tailor band structure.

Answer: A. Crystal defects such as misfit dislocations form

Growing a material with a different lattice constant forces the film to compress or stretch to match the substrate, storing strain; beyond the critical thickness, misfit dislocations form at the interface to relieve it. Some thread up through the film, causing leakage, reduced light output and shorter lifetime. Remedies include graded buffer layers, superlattices that bend dislocations and selective-area growth.

FAQ

How do you calculate lattice mismatch percentage?

Subtract the substrate lattice constant from the film lattice constant, divide by the substrate lattice constant and multiply by 100. For example, germanium (about 5.66 Å) on silicon (about 5.43 Å) gives roughly 4.2%.

Is lattice mismatch always bad?

Not necessarily. A small, controlled mismatch in a film thinner than the critical thickness produces useful strain, which is used to boost transistor mobility; it becomes harmful once the film relaxes by forming dislocations.

What is the difference between lattice mismatch and thermal mismatch?

Lattice mismatch is a difference in atomic spacing that strains the film during growth, while thermal mismatch is a difference in thermal expansion coefficients that adds strain as the wafer cools from growth temperature, sometimes causing bowing or cracking.
