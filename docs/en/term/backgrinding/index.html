What is Backgrinding?

Grinding the back of a wafer before dicing to thin it for packaging. Chips for stacking are thinned to a few tens of micrometers.

Japanese name: バックグラインド

Field: Packaging and assembly

How the process runs

Wafers arrive from the fab at roughly 725–775 µm thick, depending on diameter, because they must survive front-side processing. Before dicing, a protective backgrind tape is laminated over the circuit side and the wafer is held face-down on a vacuum chuck. A rotating diamond grinding wheel first removes most of the material in a coarse grind, then a finer-grit wheel brings it to the target thickness with a smoother surface. Thickness is measured during grinding, and afterwards the wafer is transferred to dicing tape on a frame before the backgrind tape is removed.

Removing grinding damage

Grinding leaves a thin layer of microcracks and crystal damage on the back surface. This layer weakens the die, acts as a starting point for fractures and contributes to wafer warpage, which becomes severe as the wafer gets thinner. It is therefore removed by a stress-relief step such as polishing, dry polishing or wet or plasma etching. Note the contrast with CMP, which planarizes front-side films during wafer processing, and with etch-back, which uniformly removes a deposited film; backgrinding removes bulk silicon from the back.

Typical thicknesses and why they matter

Thin packages for mobile devices often use dies of around 100 µm or less, and dies for stacked memory may be thinned to tens of micrometers so many layers fit in one package height. Thinning also shortens the heat path from the active layer, and in vertical power devices it reduces the substrate's contribution to on-resistance. In through-silicon via (TSV) processing, backgrinding exposes the via tips from the back. Very thin wafers are fragile, so they are often bonded temporarily to a rigid carrier during processing.

Dicing before grinding

Dicing an already thinned wafer risks breakage and backside chipping. In dicing before grinding (DBG), grooves slightly deeper than the final die thickness are cut into the front first; when backgrinding reaches the grooves, the dies separate cleanly while still held by tape. A variant replaces the grooves with a laser-modified layer formed inside the silicon, which splits during grinding. These approaches are favored for dies tens of micrometers thick used in stacked memory.

Answer: A. Back grinding

In backgrinding, the wafer face is protected with tape while a rotating grinding wheel thins the back from about 750 µm to the target thickness. For stacked memory it may go down to tens of micrometers. The damaged layer left by grinding can initiate cracks, so it is removed by polishing or etching. Answer: A. Dicing before grinding (DBG)

In DBG (dicing before grinding), grooves slightly deeper than the final thickness are cut into the wafer front first; backgrinding then separates the chips when it reaches the grooves. This reduces breakage and backside chipping compared with dicing an already thinned wafer, so it is used for chips tens of micrometers thick for stacked memory. A variant forms a laser-modified layer instead of grooves.

FAQ

Why are wafers thinned before packaging?

Thinner dies allow thinner packages and more dies per stack, improve heat removal and, in vertical power devices, lower on-resistance. Full-thickness wafers are only needed for mechanical strength during fab processing.

What is DBG (dicing before grinding)?

A method that cuts partial-depth grooves from the front first and then backgrinds until the grooves are reached, separating the dies. It reduces chipping and breakage for very thin dies.

Is backgrinding the same as CMP?

No. Backgrinding mechanically removes bulk silicon from the wafer back to thin it, while CMP planarizes films on the front side during wafer fabrication.
