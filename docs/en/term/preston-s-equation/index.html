What is Preston's equation?

The empirical rule that the CMP removal rate is proportional to the product of polishing pressure and the relative speed between pad and wafer.

Japanese name: プレストンの式

Preston's equation appears in 3 practice questions across 2 topics, including CMP and cleaning, Metrology and analysis.

Field: CMP and cleaning

The equation and its coefficient

Preston's equation states that removal rate equals Kp × P × V, where P is the pressure pressing the wafer against the pad and V is the relative velocity between them. It was introduced as an empirical rule for polishing glass in 1927 and carried over to chemical mechanical planarization (CMP). The Preston coefficient Kp lumps together everything else, including slurry chemistry and abrasive, pad material and conditioning, temperature and the film being polished, so it must be measured for each process. Doubling pressure or speed roughly doubles removal.

Why CMP planarizes

On a patterned wafer, raised features contact the pad first and carry a larger share of the applied load, so their local pressure is higher than in recessed areas. By Preston's equation they are removed faster, and the step height shrinks as polishing continues. Because the pad is not perfectly rigid, it also bends into wide recessed areas, which causes dishing of large metal pads and erosion of dense arrays. Layout rules on pattern density and dummy fill exist largely to keep local pressure, and therefore removal, uniform.

Where it breaks down

The linear law is an approximation. Many processes show little removal below a threshold pressure, deviations at high speed when the slurry film thickens between pad and wafer, and temperature effects as chemical reaction rates rise with heating from friction. Slurries designed for selectivity, such as ceria with additives used for shallow trench isolation, are strongly non-Prestonian because additive layers suppress removal at low pressure. Engineers still use the equation as the starting model and fit corrections from experiments.

Using it to control uniformity

If the platen and the wafer carrier rotate in the same direction at nearly the same speed, the relative velocity becomes nearly uniform across the wafer, removing one source of non-uniformity. Pressure is then the main knob. Modern heads divide their membrane into several concentric zones, roughly five to ten, each with independently set air pressure. Incoming thickness profiles are fed forward and in-situ measurements adjust zone pressures in real time, bringing post-CMP thickness variation down to a few nanometers.

Answer: C. The product of polishing pressure and the relative velocity between pad and wafer

Preston's equation, removal rate = k × pressure × relative velocity, is an empirical law introduced for glass polishing in 1927. Doubling pressure or rotation speed roughly doubles removal rate, making it the basis of process design, with k depending on slurry, pad and film. Higher local pressure on raised areas makes them polish faster—the reason CMP planarizes.

FAQ

What is the Preston coefficient?

It is the empirical constant Kp in removal rate = Kp × P × V. It captures the effects of slurry, pad, conditioning and film material and is determined experimentally for each process.

Does doubling pressure double the CMP removal rate?

Approximately, within the range where Preston's equation holds. Real processes deviate at very low pressure, very high speed and with selectivity-tuned slurries.

Why do CMP platen and head rotate at similar speeds?

Matching rotation speed and direction makes the relative pad-to-wafer velocity nearly the same at every point on the wafer, which by Preston's equation helps make removal uniform.
