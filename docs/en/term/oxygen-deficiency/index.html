What is Oxygen deficiency?

A condition in which the oxygen concentration in air falls below 18%. It tends to occur where large amounts of nitrogen are used or in confined spaces, and can cause unconsciousness within a few breaths.

Why low oxygen gives no warning

Normal air contains about 20.9% oxygen. Nitrogen, argon and helium are not toxic; they harm by displacing oxygen, so they are called simple asphyxiants. The urge to breathe is driven mainly by rising carbon dioxide in the blood, not falling oxygen, and in an inert-gas atmosphere you keep exhaling CO2 normally. A person therefore feels no breathlessness or alarm, and nitrogen has no color or odor. Judgment and coordination fail before the victim realizes anything is wrong, which is why oxygen deficiency is so often fatal.

How concentration relates to effects

Oxygen below 18% is treated as hazardous in many regulations; some workplace rules set the alarm threshold higher, around 19.5%. Approximate effects worsen steeply as concentration falls: near 16%, faster breathing, headache and reduced concentration; near 12%, dizziness and poor judgment; around 8–10%, fainting; and at about 6% or lower, collapse within seconds after one or two breaths, followed by death within minutes. Very low-oxygen air rapidly pulls oxygen out of the blood in the lungs, which explains the speed.

Where it happens in a fab

Semiconductor fabs use nitrogen in enormous volumes for purging tools and lines, filling FOUPs and protecting vacuum pumps, often piped from an on-site air separation plant. Hazards arise where gas can accumulate: inside tool enclosures during maintenance, in pits, tanks and subfloor spaces, and near liquid-nitrogen dewars, since one liter of liquid nitrogen expands to roughly 700 liters of gas. Cold nitrogen vapor and argon, which is heavier than air, collect in low areas, while a leak into a small confined space can deplete oxygen quickly.

Preventing incidents

Fixed oxygen monitors with alarms in rooms where inert gases are used, and portable meters before entry, are the first line of defense. Confined spaces need measured oxygen levels, continuous ventilation, isolation and lockout of gas supply lines, a work permit and an attendant stationed outside. A recurring pattern in fatal accidents is a would-be rescuer entering without breathing apparatus and also collapsing, so rescue procedures must specify self-contained breathing apparatus and calling for help rather than rushing in.

Large fabs place air separation plants on or next to the site and pipe it in. A leak into a confined space can drop oxygen below 18%, risking oxygen deficiency. Answer: A. Oxygen deficiency

Nitrogen is colorless, odorless and nontoxic, but a leak that lowers oxygen concentration causes oxygen deficiency without warning. Below 18% oxygen is considered hazardous, and a single breath of air very low in oxygen can cause loss of consciousness. Before entering confined spaces or tools using nitrogen, measure oxygen, ventilate thoroughly and post an attendant. Electric shock and noise are not hazards specific to nitrogen.

FAQ

Why is nitrogen dangerous if it is not toxic?

Nitrogen displaces oxygen, and because the body senses carbon dioxide rather than low oxygen, a person breathing it gets no warning before losing consciousness.

What oxygen level is considered dangerous?

Below 18% is widely treated as oxygen-deficient and hazardous, versus about 20.9% in normal air. At around 6% or less, collapse can occur after only one or two breaths.

Can you smell or notice a nitrogen leak?

No. Nitrogen is colorless and odorless, and breathing it causes no sense of suffocation, so oxygen monitors are needed to detect the hazard.
