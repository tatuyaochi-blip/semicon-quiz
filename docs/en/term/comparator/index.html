What is Comparator?

A circuit that compares two input voltages and outputs the result as a binary high or low. It is also a basic building block of ADCs.

Japanese name: コンパレータ

Comparator appears in 7 practice questions across 3 topics, including A/D and D/A conversion, Analog and electronic circuits, Image sensors and optical devices.

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

Comparator vs. op-amp

Both have a differential input and very high gain, but they are optimized for opposite jobs. An op-amp is designed for stable linear operation with negative feedback and recovers slowly once its output saturates. A comparator runs open loop, spends almost all its time saturated at one rail or the other, and is built to switch quickly and recover cleanly, often with an open-drain or logic-level output that drives digital inputs directly. Using an op-amp as a comparator works for slow signals but usually gives long and unpredictable propagation delay.

Hysteresis and the Schmitt trigger

When a noisy or slowly varying input hovers near the threshold, a plain comparator can toggle many times as noise crosses back and forth, a problem called chattering. Adding positive feedback creates two thresholds: a higher one for a rising input and a lower one for a falling input. The gap between them, the hysteresis, must exceed the noise amplitude. This arrangement is a Schmitt trigger, also available as logic gates such as the 74HC14, and is used for switch inputs, zero-crossing detection and RC relaxation oscillators.

Specs that matter

Input offset voltage sets how far apart the inputs must be before the decision is reliable, and in an ADC it appears directly as offset error. Propagation delay, often specified for a given input overdrive, sets maximum speed; small overdrive gives longer delay. Input common-mode range, supply current and output type complete the picture. Inside ADCs, comparators are usually clocked: a preamplifier followed by a regenerative latch of cross-coupled inverters that amplifies a tiny difference to full logic levels. If the input difference is extremely small, the latch can take too long to resolve, which is metastability.

Comparators inside ADCs

Every analog-to-digital converter ends in comparator decisions, and the architectures differ in how many they use. A flash ADC needs 2^N − 1 comparators, seven for 3 bits and 255 for 8 bits, which makes it fast but power-hungry. A successive-approximation (SAR) ADC reuses one comparator N times, comparing the input with an internal DAC from the most significant bit down. Pipelined ADCs use a few comparators per stage with redundancy for digital correction, and column-parallel image-sensor ADCs pair a comparator with a counter against a shared ramp.

Answer: B. Compares two input voltages and outputs the result as a binary value (high or low)

A comparator decides which of two input voltages is larger and outputs a binary high or low. Unlike an op-amp, it is designed to run without negative feedback and recover quickly from saturation, often with open-drain outputs that connect directly to logic. It is used in ADC decisions, over/undervoltage monitoring and zero-crossing detection. Hysteresis is often added to prevent output chatter from noise.

FAQ

Can I use an op-amp as a comparator?

For slow, non-critical signals it often works, but op-amps recover slowly from saturation and may not produce clean logic levels, so a dedicated comparator is better when speed or clean edges matter.

Why add hysteresis to a comparator?

Hysteresis gives separate rising and falling thresholds so noise near the switching point cannot make the output toggle repeatedly.

How many comparators does an N-bit flash ADC need?

2^N − 1, so a 3-bit flash ADC needs 7 and an 8-bit one needs 255.
