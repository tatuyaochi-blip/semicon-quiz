What is Native oxide?

A thin oxide about 1 nm thick that forms naturally when silicon is exposed to air or water. Because it raises contact resistance and disturbs epitaxial growth, it is removed right before such steps.

Japanese name: 自然酸化膜

Field: CMP and cleaning

Native oxide vs. thermal oxide

Native oxide is not grown on purpose. When bare silicon meets air or water, oxygen and moisture react with the surface, and the film grows quickly at first, then nearly stops at around 1 nm because further growth requires oxidants to diffuse through the oxide. The result is thin, often non-stoichiometric and can trap water and carbon. Thermal oxide, by contrast, is grown deliberately at about 800–1,200 °C to a controlled thickness, forming a dense film with a high-quality interface. That excellent thermal oxide is one reason silicon displaced germanium, which lacks a comparably good oxide.

Why it gets in the way

Even 1 nm of insulator matters at an interface that must conduct or grow crystal. Under a contact or silicide it raises contact resistance and can block the metal-silicon reaction. Before epitaxy, residual oxide or carbon prevents proper single-crystal growth and seeds defects such as stacking faults. It also degrades the uniformity of very thin gate dielectrics and the quality of direct bonding. Because a cleaned surface starts reoxidizing immediately, fabs limit the queue time between the clean and the next step, or link the two in vacuum.

How it is removed

The standard wet method is dilute hydrofluoric acid (DHF), typically 0.5–1% for tens of seconds, which dissolves SiO2 and leaves a hydrogen-terminated, hydrophobic surface; water beading off the wafer confirms removal. In RCA-style cleaning it complements SC-1, which removes particles and organics, and SC-2, which removes metals. Dry cleans using HF vapor or NF3/NH3-based remote plasma avoid liquids, so tall, narrow structures cannot collapse, and can be clustered with deposition chambers. Before silicon epitaxy, a hydrogen bake near 1,100 °C converts the oxide to volatile SiO, with lower-temperature alternatives where thermal budget is tight.

Native oxides as a tool

Native oxide is not always a nuisance. In vapor phase decomposition (VPD), HF vapor dissolves the native oxide and a scanned droplet collects surface metals for ICP-MS or TXRF analysis, reaching sensitivities around 10⁸–10⁹ atoms/cm². On aluminium bond pads, a dense native oxide a few nanometres thick protects the metal from corrosion, although probe needles and bonding must break through it. Other semiconductors are less fortunate: GaAs and germanium lack a good native oxide, which complicates their surface passivation and gate dielectrics.

Answer: B. Dry cleaning using vapor-phase gases or radicals

Tall, narrow patterns can be pulled over by surface tension as cleaning liquids dry. Vapor-phase (dry) cleans using HF vapor or radicals from NF3/NH3 plasmas (as in SiCoNi processing) involve no liquid, eliminating collapse risk, and can be vacuum-linked to deposition chambers so wafers proceed without reoxidizing. They are used, for example, to remove oxide at the bottoms of contacts.

FAQ

How thick is native oxide on silicon?

Typically about 1 nm. It forms quickly in air or water and then almost stops growing at room temperature.

How do you remove native oxide from silicon?

Dip the wafer in dilute HF (about 0.5–1%) for tens of seconds, or use HF vapor or a remote-plasma dry clean. Before epitaxy, a high-temperature hydrogen bake can also remove it.

What is the difference between native oxide and thermal oxide?

Native oxide forms uncontrolled at room temperature, stops at about 1 nm and is of poor quality. Thermal oxide is grown deliberately at about 800–1,200 °C to a chosen thickness and has a dense film and a good interface.
