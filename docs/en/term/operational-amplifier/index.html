What is Operational amplifier (op-amp)?

An amplifier with differential inputs and very high gain. Used with negative feedback in amplifiers, filters and more.

What is inside an op-amp

A typical operational amplifier (op-amp) has three parts. The input stage is a differential pair: a tail current source splits its current between two transistors according to the difference of the input voltages, so signals common to both inputs largely cancel, which gives a high common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR). A current-mirror active load converts that stage's output to single-ended form with high gain, a second gain stage adds more, and an output stage supplies load current. A compensation capacitor across the second stage, magnified by the Miller effect, sets the frequency response. Open-loop gain is typically 10^5 to 10^6.

Negative feedback and the virtual short

Op-amps are almost always used with negative feedback. The ideal model assumes infinite input impedance, zero output impedance and infinite open-loop gain; with feedback the output settles where the voltage between the inputs is essentially zero, the virtual short. Gain then depends only on components: an inverting amplifier gives −Rf/Ri with input impedance Ri, and a non-inverting amplifier gives 1 + R2/R1 with very high input impedance. With no R2, the voltage follower has a gain of 1 and serves as a buffer. A capacitor in the feedback path makes an integrator; a feedback resistor alone converts current to voltage in a transimpedance amplifier.

Bandwidth and slew rate

An internally compensated op-amp's gain falls at 20 dB per decade, so gain times bandwidth stays roughly constant. With a gain-bandwidth product (GBW) of 1 MHz, a non-inverting gain of 10 leaves about 100 kHz of bandwidth; strictly, divide by the noise gain, 1 + Rf/Ri. Large signals meet a separate limit, the slew rate, the fastest the output can change. A sine of peak amplitude Vp distorts above SR/(2πVp): 0.5 V/µs and 10 V give only about 8 kHz.

Real-world errors and the comparator

Mismatch in the input transistors produces an input offset voltage, a few millivolts in general-purpose parts and tens of microvolts or less in precision or chopper-stabilized types, which appears at the output multiplied by the noise gain. Bipolar inputs also draw bias current, so a resistor equal to Ri in parallel with Rf is placed at the non-inverting input to cancel its effect. A comparator looks similar but is designed to run without feedback, switching fully and recovering quickly from saturation, often with an open-drain output and added hysteresis to prevent noisy chatter.

Try it: questions about Operational amplifier (op-amp)

Which combination correctly describes an ideal op-amp?

  1. AInput impedance: infinite; output impedance: 0
  2. BInput impedance: 0; output impedance: infinite
  3. CInput impedance: 0; output impedance: 0
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Input impedance: infinite; output impedance: 0

An ideal op-amp is assumed to have infinite input impedance (no current into the inputs), zero output impedance (output voltage independent of load) and infinite open-loop gain. From these assumptions, a negative-feedback circuit keeps both inputs at the same voltage—the "virtual short"—so circuit gain depends only on resistor ratios. Real op-amps have open-loop gains of about 10⁵–10⁶, which fall with frequency.

In an inverting amplifier using an ideal op-amp, with input resistor Ri = 10 kΩ and feedback resistor Rf = 100 kΩ, what is the voltage gain?

  1. A+11
  2. B−0.1
  3. C−10
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. −10

An inverting amplifier's gain is −Rf/Ri, here −100 kΩ/10 kΩ = −10. Because the inverting input is a virtual ground, the input current Vin/Ri flows entirely through Rf, giving an output of −Rf × Vin/Ri. The input impedance equals Ri (10 kΩ), which loads the signal source. +11 is the gain of a non-inverting amplifier with the same resistors (1 + Rf/Ri).

FAQ

What is the virtual short in an op-amp?

With negative feedback and very high open-loop gain, an op-amp drives its output so that the two input voltages are nearly equal, even though they are not connected. This holds only while the output is not saturated and feedback is negative.

Can an op-amp be used as a comparator?

It can work at low speed, but op-amps are optimized for feedback operation and may recover slowly from saturation or have output levels unsuited to logic. Dedicated comparators switch faster, often have open-drain outputs, and are designed to run open-loop.

What is gain-bandwidth product?

Gain-bandwidth product is the frequency at which an op-amp's open-loop gain falls to 1, and for a compensated op-amp the closed-loop gain times bandwidth is roughly equal to it. A 10 MHz GBW part, for instance, gives about 100 kHz at a noise gain of 100.
