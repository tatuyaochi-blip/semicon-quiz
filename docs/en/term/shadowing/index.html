What is Shadowing?

When implanting at a tilt, the effect of tall patterns such as resist casting shadows so that some areas receive no implant.

Japanese name: シャドウイング

Shadowing appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Ion implantation, Deposition (CVD and PVD).

Field: Ion implantation

Why implants are tilted at all

Silicon is a crystal, and along its major axes the atoms line up into open channels. An ion beam aimed straight down a channel can travel much deeper than the intended profile, an effect called channeling. Tilting the wafer by a few degrees, typically around 7°, and adding a twist angle makes the crystal look more random to the beam. Some implants are tilted on purpose for a different reason: halo or pocket implants use larger tilts so dopant lands under the gate edge to suppress short-channel effects. Every tilt, whatever its purpose, opens the door to shadowing.

The geometry of an implant shadow

A mask feature of height h, such as a resist line or a gate stack, blocks ions over a lateral distance of roughly h × tan(θ) on its far side, where θ is the tilt angle. With 500 nm of resist and a 7° tilt the unimplanted strip is about 60 nm wide; at the larger angles used for halos it grows quickly. Because the shadow falls on only one side, the source and drain of the same transistor receive different doping, so their characteristics no longer match and device behavior can depend on its orientation on the wafer.

Remedies used in practice

The standard fix is quad-mode implantation: the total dose is split into four parts with the wafer rotated by 90° between them, so every side of a feature is exposed from some direction and the result is symmetric on average. Engineers also co-design resist thickness, opening width and tilt angle so that the shadow never reaches the region that matters. Channeling can alternatively be suppressed with a thin screen oxide or a pre-amorphization implant, which allows smaller tilts and therefore shorter shadows in densely packed layouts.

Shadowing in PVD

The same word describes a deposition problem. In physical vapor deposition (PVD), such as sputtering and evaporation, atoms fly in nearly straight lines and hardly move after landing. The top surface and the trench mouth collect film, while sidewalls and bottoms outside the line of sight receive little; the overhang at the opening can even pinch off and leave a void. Collimators and ionized PVD make the flux more directional and help bottom coverage, but conformal coatings in deep features usually call for CVD or ALD, where gas-phase precursors reach every surface.

Answer: C. Shadowing

Tilting wafers for channeling control or halo implants leaves regions shadowed by tall resist or gates unimplanted—shadowing. The doped region becomes asymmetric, making source-side and drain-side characteristics differ. Remedies include rotating the wafer in 90° steps to implant from four directions (quad mode) and co-designing resist height with tilt angle.

Answer: B. Because atoms travel almost in straight lines, they have difficulty reaching areas not in line of sight from the opening

In PVD, atoms leaving the target or evaporation source travel in nearly straight lines and barely migrate after landing. Top surfaces and trench entrances therefore receive film readily, while sidewalls and bottoms not in line of sight from the opening receive little (shadowing). CVD and ALD, whose precursor gases penetrate deep into features and react at the surface, achieve much better step coverage.

FAQ

What causes shadowing in tilted ion implantation?

When the wafer is tilted, tall structures such as resist or gate stacks block the ion beam on one side, leaving a strip roughly the feature height times tan(tilt) unimplanted. The result is asymmetric doping between the two sides of a feature.

How is implant shadowing reduced?

The dose is usually split into four implants with 90° wafer rotations (quad mode), and resist height, opening width and tilt are chosen together. Using a screen oxide or pre-amorphization to control channeling also allows smaller tilt angles.

Is shadowing the same as channeling?

No. Channeling is ions penetrating too deep along open crystal directions, and tilting the wafer is one way to avoid it; shadowing is the side effect of that tilt, where parts of the wafer receive no ions at all.
