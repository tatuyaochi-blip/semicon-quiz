What is Wet oxidation?

Thermal oxidation in an atmosphere containing water vapor. It grows faster than dry oxidation and is used to make thick oxides.

Japanese name: ウェット酸化

Wet oxidation appears in 3 practice questions across 1 topic, including Oxidation and diffusion.

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

How wet oxidation works

In wet oxidation, silicon wafers are heated, typically between about 900 and 1100 °C, in an atmosphere containing water vapor, and the reaction Si + 2H2O → SiO2 + 2H2 converts the surface to silicon dioxide. The oxidant must diffuse through the oxide already present and react at the Si/SiO2 interface, so the interface moves into the silicon; roughly 0.44 nm of silicon is consumed for every nanometer of oxide grown. The steam is often generated by burning hydrogen and oxygen just upstream of the furnace tube, a variant called pyrogenic oxidation, which gives a clean, controllable water supply.

Why it is faster than dry oxidation

Water molecules dissolve in SiO2 several hundred times more readily than O2, so far more oxidant reaches the interface per unit time. The Deal–Grove model describes growth as a reaction-limited linear regime for thin films and a diffusion-limited parabolic regime for thick films, and the higher oxidant solubility boosts the diffusion-limited regime in particular. As a result wet oxidation grows oxide several to ten times faster than dry oxidation: at 1000 °C for one hour, roughly 400 nm wet versus about 70 nm dry. Rates rise steeply with temperature, from about 200 nm at 900 °C to about 700 nm at 1100 °C for one hour wet.

Wet oxide vs. dry oxide

Speed comes at a cost in film quality. Some of the hydrogen released by the reaction remains in the oxide, making wet oxide slightly less dense and giving it a higher density of interface states and traps than dry oxide. Dry oxidation in pure O2 is slow but produces a dense film with low interface-state density and very precise thickness control, which is why thin gate oxides were traditionally grown dry.

Where you will meet it

Wet oxidation is the standard way to grow thick thermal oxides, from a few hundred nanometers to a micrometer or more. Classic uses include field oxide in LOCOS (local oxidation of silicon) isolation, masking oxides that block dopant diffusion or implantation, and thick insulating layers in power devices and MEMS. Because growth slows as the film thickens, very thick layers still need long, hot runs, so engineers trade temperature against thermal budget, since high-temperature steps also redistribute dopants that are already in the wafer.

Answer: B. Wet oxidation grows oxide faster

Wet oxidation uses steam; H2O dissolves in SiO2 several hundred times more than O2, so it grows oxide several to ten times faster than dry oxidation—at 1000 °C for an hour, roughly 70 nm dry versus 400 nm wet. It suits thick isolation and masking oxides. Dry oxidation is slower but yields dense, low-interface-state films with precise thickness control, so it was used for thin gate oxides.

FAQ

Why is wet oxidation faster than dry oxidation?

Water vapor is far more soluble in silicon dioxide than oxygen, so much more oxidant diffuses through the growing film to the silicon interface. This mainly speeds up the diffusion-limited growth of thick oxides.

Is wet oxide used for MOSFET gate oxide?

Generally not for high-quality gate dielectrics. Dry oxidation gives denser oxide with fewer interface states and better thickness control, so wet oxidation is reserved for thick isolation and masking layers.

How much silicon is consumed during thermal oxidation?

About 0.44 times the final oxide thickness, so growing 1 µm of oxide consumes roughly 0.44 µm of silicon. This is true for both wet and dry oxidation.
