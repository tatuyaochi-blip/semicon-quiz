What is Intrinsic carrier density?

The density of thermally generated electrons and holes in an undoped semiconductor, written ni. For silicon it is about 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at room temperature and rises steeply with temperature. In equilibrium, n × p = ni².

Japanese name: 真性キャリア密度

Intrinsic carrier density appears in 6 practice questions across 3 topics, including Semiconductor physics, Oxidation and diffusion, PN junctions and diodes.

Field: Semiconductor physics

Where intrinsic carriers come from

Even in perfectly pure silicon, thermal energy occasionally lifts a valence electron across the band gap, leaving a hole behind, so electrons and holes appear in equal numbers. Their equilibrium density, the intrinsic carrier density ni, follows ni ∝ T^(3/2)·exp(−Eg/2kT). For silicon at room temperature ni is about 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³, quoted as 1.0–1.5×10¹⁰ depending on the source, so only about one atom in 10¹² contributes a carrier. Narrower gaps give far more: germanium is about 2×10¹³ cm⁻³, while GaAs is only about 2×10⁶ cm⁻³.

The law of mass action

In thermal equilibrium n × p = ni², whether or not the material is doped. Adding donors raises the electron density and gives holes more partners to recombine with, so holes fall in inverse proportion to the electron density. With 1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ fully ionized donors, n ≈ 1×10¹⁶ and p = (1×10¹⁰)² / 10¹⁶ = 1×10⁴ cm⁻³. These minority carriers are a trillion times scarcer than majority carriers, yet they set junction reverse current and bipolar transistor action. The law fails away from equilibrium: under illumination or forward bias, np exceeds ni².

Strong temperature dependence

Because ni contains exp(−Eg/2kT), it rises steeply with temperature, roughly doubling for every 10 °C near room temperature in silicon. Minority-carrier densities rise with it, so pn-junction reverse leakage climbs rapidly when hot. The same effect makes a silicon diode's forward voltage fall about 2 mV/°C at constant current, used for on-chip temperature sensing and cancelled in bandgap references. When ni approaches the doping level, the device loses its doping-controlled behavior, one reason wide-gap SiC and GaN suit high-temperature operation.

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic in processing

A region is extrinsic when its doping far exceeds ni, which at room temperature happens with only tiny amounts of dopant. At diffusion temperatures, however, ni itself rises to around 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ or more, so only heavily doped regions remain extrinsic. There, the shifted Fermi level increases the population of charged point defects and the internal field pushes ionized dopants along, making diffusion coefficients concentration dependent. Arsenic profiles consequently become box-like, with flat tops and steep fronts, instead of ideal erfc or Gaussian shapes.

What happens to the intrinsic carrier density when the temperature of an intrinsic semiconductor is raised?

  1. AIt increases
  2. BIt decreases
  3. CIt does not change
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. It increases

As temperature rises, valence electrons more easily cross the band gap with thermal energy, creating more electron-hole pairs. The intrinsic carrier density follows ni ∝ T^(3/2)·exp(−Eg/2kT), and in silicon near room temperature it roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise. This steep increase is why PN-junction reverse leakage grows at high temperature, and why wide-bandgap SiC and GaN handle high-temperature operation well.

In a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium, what is the product of electron density n and hole density p (n×p)? (ni: intrinsic carrier density)

  1. AIt is always 0
  2. BIt equals ni squared
  3. CIt equals the square of the impurity concentration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. It equals ni squared

In thermal equilibrium, n × p = ni² holds even with doping, as long as the semiconductor is non-degenerate (the law of mass action): adding electrons gives holes more partners to recombine with, so holes decrease in inverse proportion to the electron density. For example, adding 1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ donors to silicon (ni ≈ 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³) gives a hole density of 1×10⁴ cm⁻³. It does not hold out of equilibrium, such as under illumination or current injection, where np > ni².

FAQ

What is the intrinsic carrier concentration of silicon at 300 K?

About 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³, with published values ranging roughly from 1.0 to 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ depending on the band-gap and effective-mass data used.

Does n × p = ni² hold in a forward-biased diode?

No. The relation holds only in thermal equilibrium; with forward bias V, the product near the junction rises to about ni² × exp(qV/kT) because carriers are being injected.

Why does intrinsic carrier density increase with temperature?

More thermal energy lets more valence electrons jump the band gap, and the dependence is exponential in −Eg/2kT. Wider band gaps make the rise far smaller at a given temperature.
