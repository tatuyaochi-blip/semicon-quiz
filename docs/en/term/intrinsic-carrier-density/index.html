What is Intrinsic carrier density?

The density of thermally generated electrons and holes in an undoped semiconductor, written ni. For silicon it is about 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at room temperature and rises steeply with temperature. In equilibrium, n × p = ni².

Japanese name: 真性キャリア密度

Try it: questions about Intrinsic carrier density

What happens to the intrinsic carrier density when the temperature of an intrinsic semiconductor is raised?

  1. AIt increases
  2. BIt decreases
  3. CIt does not change
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. It increases

As temperature rises, valence electrons more easily cross the band gap with thermal energy, creating more electron-hole pairs. The intrinsic carrier density follows ni ∝ T^(3/2)·exp(−Eg/2kT), and in silicon near room temperature it roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise. This steep increase is why PN-junction reverse leakage grows at high temperature, and why wide-bandgap SiC and GaN handle high-temperature operation well.

In a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium, what is the product of electron density n and hole density p (n×p)? (ni: intrinsic carrier density)

  1. AIt is always 0
  2. BIt equals ni squared
  3. CIt equals the square of the impurity concentration
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. It equals ni squared

In thermal equilibrium, n × p = ni² holds even with doping (the law of mass action): adding electrons gives holes more partners to recombine with, so holes decrease correspondingly. For example, adding 1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ donors to silicon (ni ≈ 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³) gives a hole density of 1×10⁴ cm⁻³. It does not hold out of equilibrium, such as under illumination or current injection, where np > ni².
