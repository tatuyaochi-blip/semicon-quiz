What is FFU (fan filter unit)?

A unit combining a fan with a high-efficiency filter. Arrays of them in the cleanroom ceiling blow clean air downward.

Japanese name: FFU(ファンフィルタユニット)

Field: Cleanrooms and equipment

What is inside an FFU

A fan filter unit is a self-contained box that fits into the cleanroom ceiling grid. A motor-driven fan draws air from the plenum space above the ceiling and pushes it through a high-efficiency filter on the room side. HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air) filters capture at least 99.97% of particles at 0.3 µm, and ULPA (ultra-low penetration air) filters reach 99.999% or better for particles around 0.1–0.2 µm. Many units also allow fan speed to be set individually, often electronically from a central controller.

How the airflow works

In the cleanest areas, FFUs cover most of the ceiling and produce unidirectional downflow, typically around 0.3–0.5 m/s, sweeping particles shed by people and equipment downward before they can drift sideways onto wafers. Air leaves through a perforated raised floor, returns through side chases to the plenum and is recirculated through the filters many times per hour. Makeup air conditioning controls temperature and humidity, and a slight positive pressure keeps unfiltered air from leaking in through doors and gaps.

FFUs vs. ducted air systems

Older cleanrooms pushed filtered air from large central air handlers through extensive ductwork to ceiling filters. With FFUs, each unit generates its own pressure, so the plenum can run near neutral pressure, ductwork is minimal and airflow can be tuned unit by unit for layout changes or local cleanliness needs. The trade-offs are many motors to maintain, heat and vibration from the fans, and a substantial electricity bill because they run continuously; efficient motors and reducing flow where full cleanliness is not needed help.

FFUs in minienvironments

Modern fabs keep wafers in sealed FOUPs (front-opening unified pods) and expose them only inside small enclosures, so the room itself can be less clean. An EFEM (equipment front-end module), which holds the load ports, door opener and transfer robot in front of a process tool, has its own FFU on top to keep that small volume extremely clean. Where airborne molecular contamination matters, chemical filters can be added alongside the particle filter.

Answer: C. EFEM (equipment front-end module, including load ports and transfer robots)

An EFEM (equipment front end module) houses the load ports for FOUPs, the door-opening mechanism and the robot that transfers wafers into the tool, all in a space kept clean by an FFU. Wafers move between FOUP and tool without exposure to room air, making it the heart of the minienvironment. OHTs move FOUPs between tools, and ULPA filters clean the air.

Answer: A. FFU (fan filter unit)

An FFU combines a fan with a HEPA or ULPA filter in one unit; many are installed across a cleanroom ceiling to blow clean air downward. Airflow is adjustable per unit, accommodating layout changes and local cleanliness requirements, so FFUs have largely replaced systems that push air through large ducts. An EFEM is a tool front-end module and an OHT an overhead transport vehicle.

FAQ

What is the difference between HEPA and ULPA filters?

HEPA filters capture at least 99.97% of 0.3 µm particles, while ULPA filters reach 99.999% or better for particles around 0.1–0.2 µm. Semiconductor cleanrooms and minienvironments often use ULPA.

What air velocity do cleanroom FFUs provide?

Unidirectional-flow cleanrooms typically run downflow of about 0.3–0.5 m/s, adjusted per unit according to the cleanliness needed in each area.

Is an FFU the same as an EFEM?

No. An FFU is just a fan plus filter that supplies clean air; an EFEM is the front-end module of a process tool, containing load ports and a wafer-transfer robot, and it uses an FFU to keep its interior clean.
