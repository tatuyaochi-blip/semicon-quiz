What is Silane (SiH4)?

A gas compound of silicon and hydrogen used as the source for CVD of polysilicon, silicon oxide and silicon nitride. It ignites spontaneously in air, so it is handled under strict control.

Japanese name: シラン(SiH4)

Silane as a silicon source

Silane (SiH4, monosilane) decomposes on heating to deposit silicon: SiH4 → Si + 2H2. In LPCVD at about 600–650 °C it forms polysilicon for gates, resistors and MEMS layers; below about 580 °C the film is amorphous, and annealing that amorphous film near 600 °C grows larger grains than direct poly deposition. For epitaxy, silane works at roughly 600–900 °C, lower than dichlorosilane or trichlorosilane. Disilane (Si2H6) and trisilane decompose even more readily for low-temperature growth, and silane mixed with germane (GeH4) grows SiGe.

Oxide and nitride from silane

Silane also supplies silicon for dielectrics. In plasma-enhanced CVD (PECVD) at about 300–400 °C, silane with nitrous oxide (N2O) deposits silicon oxide or tunable-index oxynitride (SiON), and silane with ammonia deposits silicon nitride containing about 10–30% hydrogen, used as final passivation and as a copper cap. Higher-quality LPCVD nitride uses dichlorosilane and ammonia at 700–800 °C instead. Silane is also used to nucleate tungsten CVD before WF6 is reduced with hydrogen, and with a hydrocarbon such as propane for SiC epitaxy.

Why silane is dangerous

Silane is pyrophoric: it can ignite spontaneously on contact with air, burning to a white silica powder. The worse hazard is that a high-concentration leak may not ignite at once, allowing a gas cloud to accumulate and then ignite explosively. Its lower explosive limit is about 1.4% in air. Fabs therefore store cylinders in exhausted gas cabinets, deliver it through lines with leak detectors, flow-limiting devices and automatic emergency shutoff valves, and purge lines with inert gas before any maintenance.

Silane vs. chlorosilanes and TEOS

Chlorinated sources such as dichlorosilane and trichlorosilane need higher temperatures, but the HCl they release suppresses nucleation on oxide, favoring selective epitaxy, and trichlorosilane at about 1100–1150 °C grows thick layers at several µm per minute. Silane deposits at lower temperatures without chlorine, but grows less selectively and can form particles by gas-phase reaction. For oxide, liquid TEOS is a safer alternative that gives more conformal step coverage than silane-based oxide.

Answer: B. Amorphous silicon

Silicon films from thermal decomposition of silane are amorphous below about 580 °C and polycrystalline above it. Depositing amorphous silicon and then annealing near 600 °C for a long time grows crystals from few nuclei, yielding larger grains (and higher mobility) than direct poly deposition. Thin-film transistors in LCDs also use laser crystallization.

Answer: A. It is pyrophoric and may ignite if it leaks into air

Silane is pyrophoric: it ignites spontaneously on contact with air, and a high-concentration leak can accumulate before igniting explosively. It is therefore kept in exhausted cylinder cabinets and supplied through lines with leak detectors and emergency shutoff valves. It is not radioactive, nor is it a benign gas needing no special care. Silane is a common CVD precursor for polysilicon, SiO2 and SiN.

FAQ

Why is silane dangerous?

Silane is pyrophoric and can ignite spontaneously in air. A large leak may also build up before igniting, causing an explosion, so it is handled in exhausted cabinets with leak detection and automatic shutoff.

At what temperature does silane deposit polysilicon?

LPCVD polysilicon is typically deposited from silane at about 600–650 °C. Below roughly 580 °C the film comes out amorphous instead of polycrystalline.

What is the difference between silane and disilane?

Disilane (Si2H6) contains a Si–Si bond that breaks more easily than the bonds in monosilane (SiH4), so it decomposes and deposits silicon at lower temperatures, which helps keep dopant profiles sharp in low-temperature epitaxy.
