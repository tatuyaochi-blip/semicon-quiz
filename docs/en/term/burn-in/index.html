What is Burn-in?

A test that operates products under stress such as high temperature and high voltage to remove units prone to early failure before shipment.

Japanese name: バーンイン

Burn-in appears in 6 practice questions across 5 topics, including Testing and inspection, Image sensors and optical devices, Compound semiconductors.

Field: Testing and inspection

What burn-in screens out

Some chips pass every electrical test at time zero yet contain latent defects: a thin spot in a gate oxide, a marginal via, a particle bridging two lines, or contamination. Under normal use these weak parts fail early, producing the high, falling failure rate of the infant-mortality period at the start of the bathtub curve. Burn-in deliberately accelerates those failures in the factory so the weak units can be identified and removed, leaving a shipped population whose failure rate is already in the low, nearly constant random-failure period.

Typical burn-in conditions

Parts are loaded onto burn-in boards inside ovens and operated for hours to tens of hours at around 125 °C with supply voltage set above nominal. Temperature accelerates thermally activated failure mechanisms roughly following the Arrhenius relation, and elevated voltage stresses dielectrics, so a short burn-in emulates a much longer period of field use. In static burn-in the device is only biased, while dynamic burn-in toggles internal nodes with test patterns so that more of the circuit is stressed. Conditions are chosen so that good parts lose only a small fraction of their wear-out lifetime.

Where it fits in the test flow

A typical flow is wafer test, assembly, final test and shipment. Burn-in is usually inserted around final test, followed by a post-burn-in electrical test that catches the units that failed under stress. Wafer-level burn-in, done before dicing, is used when bare known-good dies are needed for multi-chip packages. Because burn-in costs oven time, boards and floor space, it is applied mainly to high-reliability products such as automotive and server parts, while other products may rely on shorter elevated-voltage stress tests and statistical reliability data.

Not to be confused with display burn-in

In displays, burn-in means something different: permanent image retention when a static image is shown for a long time. OLED pixels emit their own light and age with accumulated current, so pixels that stay bright, such as a status bar, dim relative to their neighbors and leave a ghost image. Inorganic micro-LED displays are promoted partly because they do not suffer this effect. Semiconductor burn-in is a deliberate screening test, while display burn-in is an unwanted aging artifact.

FAQ

Why is burn-in done at high temperature and voltage?

Most early-failure mechanisms, such as defective oxides and weak interconnects, are accelerated by heat and electric field. Stressing at around 125 °C and above-nominal voltage makes latent defects fail within hours instead of months in the field.

Does burn-in shorten the life of good chips?

It consumes a small part of their life, but the stress level and duration are chosen so that wear-out still begins well after the intended service life. The benefit of removing infant-mortality failures outweighs this small loss.

What is the bathtub curve?

It is a plot of failure rate versus time with three periods: a high, decreasing early-failure rate, a low and nearly constant random-failure rate, and a rising wear-out rate. Burn-in is used to get past the first period before shipment.
