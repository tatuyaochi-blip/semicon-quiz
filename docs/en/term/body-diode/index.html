What is Body diode?

A parasitic diode formed between source and drain by the structure of a power MOSFET. It can serve as the freewheeling diode in an inverter, but its reverse recovery needs attention.

Japanese name: ボディダイオード

Body diode appears in 3 practice questions across 2 topics, including Power semiconductors, MOSFET.

Field: Power semiconductors

Where the body diode comes from

A MOSFET is a four-terminal device: gate, source, drain and body. In a vertical n-channel power MOSFET, the source metal contacts both the n+ source and the p-type body, mainly so the body potential is fixed and the parasitic bipolar transistor formed by source, body and drift region cannot turn on. Shorting body to source leaves the junction between the p body and the n- drift region connected between source and drain: a diode with its anode at the source and cathode at the drain, which conducts when the drain is pulled below the source.

Freewheeling and synchronous rectification

In inverters and motor drives, inductive load current must keep flowing when a switch turns off. During the dead time, when both switches in a half-bridge are off to prevent shoot-through, that current flows through the body diode of the opposite MOSFET, so the body diode serves as the freewheeling diode without an extra part. To reduce conduction loss, the MOSFET channel is usually turned on during reverse conduction, called synchronous rectification, so current flows through the low-resistance channel instead of the diode's forward voltage drop.

The reverse recovery problem

The body diode is a bipolar PN junction, so while it conducts, minority carriers are stored in the drift region. When the opposite switch turns on and reverses the voltage, that stored charge must be swept out, producing a reverse current spike that adds switching loss, stresses the switch and causes ringing and electromagnetic interference. In silicon power MOSFETs reverse recovery is relatively slow, so fast-switching designs minimize diode conduction time or add an external fast diode. SiC MOSFET body diodes store far less charge and recover much faster.

Body diode degradation in SiC

SiC brings its own issue. Because of the wide band gap, a SiC body diode has a high forward voltage, typically around 3 V or more, and forward current through it can trigger bipolar degradation: the energy released by electron-hole recombination expands basal-plane dislocations in the crystal into stacking faults, planar defects that impede conduction and raise on-voltage over time. Remedies include epitaxial buffer layers that convert basal-plane dislocations into a harmless type during growth, and integrating Schottky diodes so the body diode does not carry current.

Answer: A. Basal plane dislocations in the crystal expand into stacking faults

Forward current through a SiC body diode can, via electron-hole recombination energy, expand basal-plane dislocations in the crystal into stacking faults, raising on-voltage (bipolar degradation); stacking faults are planar defects that impede conduction. Remedies include buffer layers that convert basal-plane dislocations into another dislocation type during epitaxy, and integrating Schottky diodes so the body diode is not relied on.

FAQ

Does every MOSFET have a body diode?

Any MOSFET has PN junctions between its body and source/drain, but in discrete power MOSFETs the body is tied to the source, so one of those junctions appears as a diode between source and drain. GaN HEMTs have no such PN junction and therefore no body diode, although they can still conduct in reverse through the channel.

Can the body diode replace an external freewheeling diode?

Often yes, especially with synchronous rectification and SiC MOSFETs. With slower silicon body diodes, fast-switching circuits may add an external fast or Schottky diode to limit reverse recovery losses.

Why does a body diode have reverse recovery?

It is a bipolar PN junction, so forward conduction stores minority carriers in the drift region, and that charge must be removed before the diode can block voltage, causing a brief reverse current.
