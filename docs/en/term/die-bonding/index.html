What is Die bonding?

Attaching and fixing a diced chip at its position on a lead frame or package substrate, using adhesive, solder or sintered material.

Japanese name: ダイボンディング

Die bonding appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Packaging and assembly.

Field: Packaging and assembly

Its place in back-end assembly

Die bonding, also called die attach, is the step in which chips (dies) separated by dicing are picked up one by one and attached and fixed at their positions on a lead frame or package substrate. In packages connected by wire bonding, the flow is die bonding, then wire bonding, then resin molding. Beyond holding the chip in place, the bond is the path for heat to flow down out of the chip and, in power devices, the path for current through the backside electrode, so the thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity and reliability of the bonding material shape product characteristics.

Types of bonding material

Bonding materials include conductive silver paste (epoxy resin filled with silver particles), insulating resin paste, sheet-form die attach film (DAF), solder and sintered silver. In general ICs, easy-to-handle resin pastes are widely used, while memories that stack many chips use DAF, which gives a uniform thickness and does not squeeze out. Power devices with high current and heat have used solder, which conducts heat and electricity well. SiC power modules that operate at high temperature sometimes use sintered silver, which bonds at around 250 °C yet yields silver with a high melting point of about 960 °C.

Key quality points

In die bonding, the accuracy of chip position and tilt, uniform bond-line thickness and few voids (gaps) are important. A void in the bond layer impedes heat flow at that spot, causing local temperature rise that can damage the chip or shorten its life. Voids are inspected with X-ray transmission images or a scanning acoustic microscope (SAT). In addition, because the chip and substrate have different coefficients of thermal expansion, repeated temperature changes stress the bond layer, causing cracks or delamination. In power-cycling tests of power modules, cracking of the solder layer is a typical failure along with wire lift-off.

How it differs from flip-chip and other bonding

In die bonding, the chip is basically mounted circuit side up with its backside bonded, and the electrical connections are made afterward by wire bonding. In flip-chip, by contrast, the chip faces circuit side down, and bumps provide mechanical attachment and electrical connection at the same time. Placing such a chip on the substrate is called flip-chip bonding and is distinguished from ordinary die bonding. For stacking thin chips such as HBM or connecting fine-pitch bumps, thermocompression bonding (TCB), which heats and presses chips one at a time, is used. All of these share the need to align and place each chip individually with high accuracy.

Answer: C. Die bonding

Die bonding fixes a singulated chip to a lead frame or substrate using adhesives such as silver paste, solder or die-attach film. Power devices with high current and heat use solder or sintered silver for thermal and electrical conductivity, while stacked memory uses film for uniform thickness. Wire bonding then makes the electrical connections. Dicing is the step that separates the chips.

FAQ

What is the difference between die bonding and wire bonding?

Die bonding attaches and fixes the chip to a lead frame or substrate, while wire bonding then connects the chip's electrodes to external terminals electrically with fine metal wires. In packages connected by wire bonding, die bonding comes first.

What is a die attach film (DAF)?

A sheet-form adhesive used for die bonding. If it is laminated to the back of the wafer before dicing, the chips are separated with an adhesive layer of exactly their own size already attached. Because it gives a uniform thickness and does not squeeze out like paste, it is used in applications such as memories with stacked chips.

Why is sintered silver used for power devices?

Because solder has a low melting point in the 200 °C range and tends to fatigue under repeated temperature cycling at high temperature. Sintered silver bonds at around 250 °C, the bonded silver has a high melting point of about 960 °C, and its thermal conductivity is better than solder's, making it suitable for SiC power modules operating at high temperature. High cost is its drawback.
