What is Critical thickness?

The maximum thickness to which a lattice-mismatched layer can be grown while staying strained without forming dislocations. Beyond it, misfit dislocations appear.

Japanese name: 臨界膜厚

Critical thickness appears in 2 practice questions across 1 topic, including Epitaxial growth.

Field: Epitaxial growth

Why a thickness limit exists

When a film with a different lattice constant grows on a substrate, its first layers stretch or compress in-plane to match the substrate atom spacing; this is coherent or pseudomorphic growth. The elastic strain energy stored per unit area grows in proportion to film thickness. A misfit dislocation at the interface costs energy to create but relieves some of that strain. Once the film is thick enough that relieving strain pays for the dislocation, relaxation becomes favorable, and that crossover is the critical thickness. Energy-balance models such as Matthews–Blakeslee describe this trade-off quantitatively.

Typical numbers

Critical thickness falls steeply as mismatch grows. Si and Ge differ in lattice constant by about 4%, so a SiGe alloy has a mismatch of roughly 4% times its Ge fraction. For about 20% Ge the critical thickness is on the order of tens of nanometers, while pure Ge on Si relaxes after only a few monolayers. Layers grown at low temperature can stay strained beyond the equilibrium value because dislocation formation is kinetically slow; such metastable films may relax later during high-temperature steps, so the thermal budget after growth matters.

What goes wrong beyond it

Exceeding the critical thickness creates misfit dislocations along the interface. Their ends cannot stop inside the crystal, so many continue as threading dislocations that run up through the film to the surface and through any device built on it. They act as leakage paths and recombination centers, cutting carrier lifetime, raising junction leakage, reducing light output in LEDs and lasers and shortening device lifetime. Relaxation also removes the strain itself, which is often the very property the layer was designed to provide.

Designing around the limit

Strained-silicon channels, SiGe source/drain stressors, SiGe and III–V quantum wells all depend on keeping each strained layer below its critical thickness, or on strain-compensating alternating layers. When a thick layer of a mismatched material is genuinely needed, the opposite strategy is used: a graded buffer raises the composition slowly so that relaxation happens gradually and misfit dislocations can glide long distances, leaving fewer threading segments in a relaxed virtual substrate. Superlattices that bend threading dislocations and selective-area growth on small regions further reduce the defect density reaching the active layer.

Answer: C. The maximum thickness that can be grown while keeping its strain without generating dislocations

A lattice-mismatched film initially grows strained to match the substrate (coherent growth). As thickness increases, strain energy accumulates, and beyond the critical thickness misfit dislocations form and relax it. Larger mismatch means a thinner critical thickness—roughly tens of nm for SiGe with 20% Ge. It is a key parameter for designing dislocation-free strained SiGe layers and quantum wells.

FAQ

What happens when a strained layer exceeds its critical thickness?

Misfit dislocations form at the interface to relieve the strain, and many extend as threading dislocations through the film. The layer loses its strain and gains defects that cause leakage and reduced lifetime.

What is the critical thickness of SiGe on silicon?

It depends strongly on Ge content; for around 20% Ge it is on the order of tens of nanometers, and it shrinks rapidly as the Ge fraction and therefore the mismatch increase.

Can a film be grown thicker than the critical thickness without dislocations?

Temporarily, yes: at low growth temperature dislocation formation is kinetically slow, giving a metastable strained film. Such films can relax if they later see high-temperature processing.
