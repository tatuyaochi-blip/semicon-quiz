What is Hot carrier injection (HCI)?

Degradation in which carriers accelerated by the strong field near the drain are injected into the gate insulator, shifting the threshold voltage and reducing drive current.

Japanese name: ホットキャリア注入(HCI)

Field: Reliability

How hot carriers arise

Near the drain of a MOSFET, much of the drain voltage drops across a narrow region, so the electric field along the channel becomes strong. Electrons flowing in the channel (holes in a pMOS) are accelerated by this field into hot carriers with energies far above the thermal energy of the crystal. Some hot carriers surmount the energy barrier between silicon and oxide and are injected into the gate dielectric, or break bonds at the interface and create interface states. They also generate new electron-hole pairs by impact ionization, and in an nMOS the resulting holes flow into the substrate as substrate current.

Degradation and its evaluation

When injected charge is trapped in the dielectric or interface states increase, the threshold voltage shifts and mobility falls, reducing drive current. Because the damage is localized near the drain, a characteristic sign is asymmetry when the device is measured with source and drain swapped. For evaluation, a drain voltage higher than in use is applied as stress, the time for the threshold voltage or drive current to change by a set amount is measured, and the lifetime under use conditions is extrapolated. Substrate current reflects the amount of impact ionization and has long served as an indicator of susceptibility to degradation.

Countermeasures such as the LDD structure

The basic countermeasure is to lower the peak field near the drain. The LDD (lightly doped drain) structure uses the gate sidewall spacer to form a shallow, lightly doped region on the channel side of the drain, easing the field. Lowering the supply voltage is also an effective countermeasure because it reduces the energy carriers gain. Oxynitride gate dielectrics, made by incorporating nitrogen into the gate oxide, also improve resistance to hot carriers. In today's transistors, shallow, heavily doped extensions that also suppress short-channel effects have become mainstream.

How it differs from NBTI and other degradation

HCI is degradation that progresses while current flows through the transistor and voltage is applied to the drain; in digital circuits it occurs mainly during switching transitions. Circuits that switch more often therefore degrade faster. NBTI, by contrast, progresses in a pMOS with negative gate voltage at high temperature even when no current flows. HCI is also distinct from TDDB, in which a sustained electric field across the dielectric eventually causes breakdown. In advanced LSI design, degradation from HCI and BTI is predicted with models from voltage, temperature and activity, and timing is checked to ensure it is still met with the degraded characteristics.

Answer: C. Aging-aware reliability design

Transistor characteristics degrade gradually in use, slowing circuits. Aging-aware reliability design predicts degradation from each transistor's voltage, temperature and activity using models, then runs circuit simulation and timing analysis with aged characteristics to ensure margins hold until end of life. Another approach monitors degradation in operation and gradually raises supply voltage to compensate for the slowdown.

FAQ

What are hot carriers?

Electrons or holes accelerated by a strong electric field until their energy is far above the thermal energy of the surrounding crystal. In a MOSFET they arise in the strong field near the drain and degrade the transistor by being injected into the gate dielectric and creating interface states.

What is the difference between HCI and NBTI?

HCI is degradation caused by carriers accelerated in the high field near the drain while current flows and voltage is applied to the drain. NBTI is degradation that progresses in a pMOS with negative gate voltage at high temperature, even without current. The conditions and locations of the degradation differ.

Why does the LDD structure suppress hot carriers?

A lightly doped region at the channel-side edge of the drain spreads the drain voltage over a wider region, lowering the peak electric field. Carriers gain less energy, so injection into the gate dielectric and the creation of interface states are reduced.
