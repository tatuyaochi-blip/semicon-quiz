What is Carry-save adder?

An adder that reduces three numbers to two, a sum and a carry, without propagating carries. Made of independent full adders per bit, its delay is constant, so multipliers use it to add many partial products.

Japanese name: 桁上げ保存加算器

Carry-save adder appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Computer Arithmetic.

Field: Computer Arithmetic

Reducing three numbers to two

A carry-save adder (CSA) is simply full adders placed independently, one per bit. Feeding the same bit position of three numbers A, B and C into each full adder, and collecting the sum bits into a number S and the carry bits, shifted one place left, into a number Cy, gives A + B + C = S + Cy. For example, with the 4-bit numbers 5 (0101), 6 (0110) and 7 (0111), S = 0100 (4) and Cy = 1110 (14), and 4 + 14 = 18 = 5 + 6 + 7. Because no carry passes to the neighboring bit, the delay is a constant one full adder regardless of width.

How it differs from a ripple-carry adder

In an ordinary ripple-carry adder, each bit's result waits for the carry from the bit below, so in the worst case a carry travels from the least to the most significant bit and delay is proportional to width. Even sped up with a carry-lookahead adder, delay still grows roughly with the logarithm of the width. A carry-save adder simply saves the carries and passes them to the next stage, so no matter how many numbers are added, the intermediate additions never wait for carry propagation. The price is a redundant result split into two numbers, sum and carry, which must finally be added once by an ordinary adder to give a single number.

Use in multipliers

An n-bit multiplication produces n partial products, and adding them one after another is slow. Instead, carry-save adders are arranged in a tree, reducing three numbers to two in parallel at each level. Each level cuts the count to about two-thirds, so the number of levels needed to reach two is roughly logarithmic in the width. This is the Wallace tree; the Dadda tree reduces hardware by using full adders more economically. 4:2 compressors, which reduce four numbers to two, are also common. A fast adder such as a carry-lookahead adder then sums the final two numbers to complete the product. Combining this with Booth's algorithm to reduce the number of partial products is also common.

Other uses and caveats

The carry-save form works for any process that keeps adding many numbers. A multiply-accumulate (MAC) unit may keep its accumulator in sum-and-carry form to avoid carry propagation every cycle. Large-integer Montgomery multiplication in cryptography, and division and square-root computation, also gain speed by holding intermediate values in redundant form. In redundant form, though, magnitude, sign and overflow cannot be determined immediately, and registers holding the value need roughly twice as many bits. So when such a decision is needed, the value is converted back into a single number with an ordinary adder.

Answer: C. It reduces three numbers to two without carry propagation

Adding partial products one at a time with ordinary adders means waiting each time for carries to ripple from low to high bits. A carry-save adder is just full adders placed independently per bit, reducing three numbers to two (the per-bit sums and the per-bit carries) without waiting for carry propagation, so its delay is a constant one full-adder level. Stages of these reduce the partial products to two, which are added once at the end by a carry-lookahead adder or similar. It cannot reduce them to one in a single step.

FAQ

What is a carry-save adder?

It is an adder that reduces three numbers to two, a sum and a carry, without propagating carries. Made simply of full adders per bit, its delay is constant, so multipliers use it to add many partial products.

What is the difference between a carry-save adder and a ripple-carry adder?

A ripple-carry adder adds two numbers to give one result, but carries pass from low to high bits in sequence, so delay is proportional to width. A carry-save adder only reduces three numbers to two and does not propagate carries, so its delay is constant regardless of width.

Why can't a carry-save adder finish an addition on its own?

Because its output is split into two numbers, a sum and a carry. To use the result as a single value, the two must finally be added by an ordinary adder that propagates carries. Even when many numbers are added, this final addition is needed only once.
