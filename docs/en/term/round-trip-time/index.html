What is Round-trip time (RTT)?

The time from sending data until the response comes back. It includes the time signals take to travel the distance plus processing and queuing in devices along the way. It is used to calculate TCP's retransmission timeout and to estimate how long it takes to establish a connection (number of round trips × RTT), and it can be measured with ping. Light travels at about 200,000 km/s in optical fiber, so 1,000 km of distance alone adds about 10 ms of round-trip time.

Japanese name: RTT(往復遅延時間)

Try it: questions about Round-trip time (RTT)

A client makes a new HTTPS connection (TLS 1.3 over TCP) to a web server with a round-trip time (RTT) of 100 ms. Roughly how long does it take until the response to the first request starts to arrive? Assume DNS resolution is already done, and ignore server processing time and data transfer time.

  1. AAbout 100 ms
  2. BAbout 300 ms
  3. CAbout 200 ms
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. About 300 ms

First, the TCP three-way handshake takes one round trip (the client can send its first TLS message together with the final ACK). Next, the TLS 1.3 handshake takes one round trip, and then the HTTP request and response take one more, for three round trips in total: 3 × 100 = 300 ms. With TLS 1.2 the key exchange takes two round trips, giving four round trips, or 400 ms. This waiting time depends on the number of RTTs, which is set by distance, so adding link bandwidth does not shorten it. QUIC, used by HTTP/3, combines the transport and cryptographic handshakes into one round trip and also offers a way to send data in the first flight when reconnecting (0-RTT).

A TCP connection has a fixed receive window of 65536 bytes and an RTT of 10 ms. The link bandwidth is ample and there is no loss. Which is closest to the upper limit on this connection's throughput?

  1. AAbout 52 Mbps
  2. BAbout 6.6 Mbps
  3. CAbout 524 Mbps
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. About 52 Mbps

Under window control, at most one window of data can be sent per round trip (RTT), the time until ACKs return. The throughput limit is therefore window ÷ RTT = 65536 bytes × 8 ÷ 0.01 s ≈ 52.4 Mbps (about 52 Mbps), and it goes no higher even on a 1 Gbps link. To fill a fast, distant link, the window must be at least the product of bandwidth and RTT (the bandwidth-delay product): for 1 Gbps and an RTT of 10 ms, 10^9 × 0.01 = 10^7 bits = 1.25 MB. The window field in the TCP header is 16 bits, for a maximum of 65535 bytes, but the window scale option can enlarge it.
