What is Voltage follower?

A unity-gain op-amp circuit with the output tied directly to the inverting input. With high input impedance and low output impedance, it is used as a buffer.

Japanese name: ボルテージフォロワ

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

How a voltage follower works

The op-amp output is wired straight to the inverting input and the signal drives the non-inverting input, so the whole output is fed back (feedback fraction 1). With open-loop gain A, the output is Vin × A/(1 + A), within about 1/A of the input, and in phase with it. It is the non-inverting amplifier with gain 1 + R2/R1 taken to its limit: R2 shorted and R1 removed. No resistors are needed, and the gain cannot drop below 1 in this family.

Why a gain of one is useful

The follower converts impedance. Its input draws almost no current, while its output can supply current with a low output impedance that the feedback lowers further. Consider a divider of two 10 kΩ resistors across 10 V: unloaded it gives 5 V, but a 10 kΩ load in parallel with the lower resistor pulls the node down to about 3.3 V. Placing a follower between divider and load keeps the node at 5 V. The same idea buffers high-impedance sensors, reference voltages and ADC inputs whose sampling capacitors would otherwise disturb the source.

Stability and input-range pitfalls

The follower has the deepest possible feedback, so the loop gain equals the full open-loop gain and the loop crosses unity at the op-amp's highest frequency, where phase margin is smallest. Some op-amps are only stable at higher closed-loop gains; check that the datasheet says unity-gain stable. A capacitive load adds phase lag and can cause ringing, which a small series resistor at the output usually cures. Because the input voltage is also the common-mode voltage, it must stay within the common-mode input range; a 0 V to (V+ − 1.5 V) part on 5 V cannot follow a 4 V signal.

Voltage follower vs. emitter follower

Discrete buffers such as the emitter follower (common-collector stage) and source follower also give near-unity gain with high input and low output impedance, but their output sits a base-emitter or gate-source voltage away from the input, the gain is slightly below 1, and linearity is limited. The op-amp follower removes that offset because the feedback loop forces the output to match the input, leaving only the op-amp's small input offset voltage as error.

Answer: A. Its voltage gain is 1, with high input impedance and low output impedance, so it is used as a buffer

A voltage follower is a non-inverting amplifier with the output tied directly to the inverting input (gain 1), so the output equals the input. Its very high input impedance and low output impedance make it a buffer that passes signals from high-impedance sensors or dividers to later stages or loads without altering them. As this is the deepest-feedback configuration, some op-amps lack phase margin and may oscillate, so check that the datasheet says "unity-gain stable."

FAQ

Why use a voltage follower if its gain is only 1?

It isolates a high-impedance source from a load. The source sees almost no load current, while the op-amp supplies whatever current the load needs, so the voltage is passed on unchanged.

Why does my op-amp voltage follower oscillate?

Unity gain is the most demanding condition for stability, so an op-amp that is not unity-gain stable, or a capacitive load on the output, can leave too little phase margin. Choose a unity-gain-stable part or add a small isolation resistor in series with the output.

What is the difference between a voltage follower and a non-inverting amplifier?

A voltage follower is a non-inverting amplifier with the feedback resistor replaced by a wire and the ground resistor removed, giving a gain of exactly 1. A non-inverting amplifier with resistors has gain 1 + R2/R1.
