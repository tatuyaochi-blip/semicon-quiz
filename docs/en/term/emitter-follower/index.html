What is Emitter follower?

A common-collector amplifier. Its voltage gain is about 1, but its high input impedance and low output impedance make it a buffer that drives the next stage without loading the signal.

Japanese name: エミッタフォロワ

Emitter follower appears in 3 practice questions across 2 topics, including Bipolar transistors, MOSFET.

Field: Bipolar transistors

How the circuit works

In an emitter follower, the signal drives the base, the output is taken from the emitter, and the collector goes to the supply, which is AC ground, so the collector is common to input and output. The emitter sits one base-emitter drop (VBE, about 0.7 V) below the base and tracks it. The small-signal gain is RE/(RE + re), where RE is the total emitter load and re = 1/gm, about 26 Ω at 1 mA. Gain is therefore slightly below 1 and non-inverting. The stage provides current gain of about hFE + 1 rather than voltage gain.

Impedance transformation

The base current is only about 1/(hFE + 1) of the emitter current, so the load seen from the input is magnified: the input resistance is roughly (hFE + 1) times the emitter load plus re. Looking back from the output, the source resistance is divided by (hFE + 1) and re is added. With hFE = 100, a 1 kΩ load presents about 100 kΩ at the input, and a 10 kΩ source appears as roughly 125 Ω at the output. This lets a high-impedance source drive a low-impedance load without its signal being pulled down.

Limits and practical forms

A follower biased by a resistor or current source to the negative supply can pull the output up strongly but can pull it down only with that bias current, so large swings into heavy loads clip on one side. Complementary push-pull followers, an NPN sourcing and a PNP sinking current, solve this and are the classic output stage of amplifiers and power supplies; they need a small bias to avoid crossover distortion. The VBE level shift also drifts by about −2 mV/°C. A Darlington pair, two followers in cascade, raises input impedance further.

Emitter follower vs. source follower

The MOSFET counterpart is the source follower, or common-drain stage. Its input is a gate, so input impedance is essentially infinite, and its output impedance is about 1/gm. Because a MOSFET has lower gm than a bipolar transistor at the same current, its output impedance is higher, and its gain gmRs/(1 + gmRs) is further reduced by the body effect. Its level shift, VGS, is also larger than VBE and varies more between devices. The bipolar follower therefore drives loads better, while the source follower draws no input current.

Answer: C. Its voltage gain is about 1 and its output impedance is low, so it is used as a buffer

In an emitter follower the emitter tracks the base voltage minus VBE (about 0.7 V), so the voltage gain is about 1 and in phase. Meanwhile the input impedance is roughly (hFE+1) times the load, and the output impedance is low—about the source resistance ÷ hFE plus 1/gm. It is therefore used as a buffer that lets a high-impedance source drive a low-impedance load, and in power-supply output stages.

FAQ

Why use an amplifier with a voltage gain of 1?

Because it transforms impedance: it presents a high input impedance to the source and a low output impedance to the load, so a weak source can drive a heavy load without losing signal.

Is an emitter follower the same as a common-collector amplifier?

Yes. The two names describe the same circuit; the collector is at AC ground and common to input and output, and the emitter voltage follows the base.

What is the output impedance of an emitter follower?

Approximately re + Rs/(hFE + 1), where re = 1/gm (about 26 Ω at 1 mA) and Rs is the source resistance driving the base.
