What is Vacuum evaporation?

Forming a film by heating a material in vacuum until it evaporates and condenses on the substrate. Electron-beam heating handles high-melting-point materials, but step coverage is poor.

Japanese name: 真空蒸着

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

How evaporation works

The source material is heated in a chamber pumped to about 1e-4 Pa or better until its vapor pressure becomes significant and atoms leave its surface. At that pressure the mean free path of the atoms is much longer than the distance to the substrate, so they travel in straight lines without collisions and condense where they land. They arrive with only thermal energy, around 0.1 eV. Deposition rate and thickness are commonly monitored with a quartz crystal microbalance, whose resonant frequency falls as film accumulates on it.

Resistive versus electron-beam heating

Resistive evaporation passes current through a boat or filament of a refractory metal such as tungsten or molybdenum holding the source; it is simple but limited to materials that evaporate below the boat's temperature, and the boat can contaminate the film. Electron-beam evaporation instead focuses electrons accelerated through several kilovolts onto the source in a water-cooled crucible, melting only a small spot. It can evaporate refractory metals and oxides with little crucible contamination, though the electron impact also generates X-rays that can create charge traps in sensitive gate dielectrics.

Line of sight and lift-off

Because atoms fly straight from the source, surfaces facing away from it receive little material, so step coverage over topography is poor and sidewalls stay thin. Rotating planetary substrate holders improve uniformity across wafers but cannot make the coating conformal. Lift-off turns this weakness into a patterning method: resist is patterned with an undercut profile, metal is evaporated over everything, and the film breaks cleanly at the resist edges, so dissolving the resist removes unwanted metal. This is widely used for electrodes on compound semiconductors, where etching the metal would be difficult.

Evaporation versus sputtering

Sputtering ejects atoms from a target by ion bombardment, and they arrive with several to tens of eV, giving denser films with better adhesion. In evaporation, components of an alloy with different vapor pressures leave at different rates, so film composition drifts, whereas a sputtered film closely matches the target once its surface stabilizes. Sputtering also covers large areas more uniformly, so it dominates silicon IC metallization. Evaporation remains common for lift-off metal stacks, research, and thermal evaporation of organic layers in OLED displays.

Answer: C. Material is heated in vacuum to evaporate it and deposit it on the substrate

Vacuum evaporation heats material by resistance or electron beam until it evaporates, and the atoms travel through vacuum (about 10⁻⁴ Pa or better) to coat the substrate. The equipment is relatively simple and films are pure, but line-of-sight travel gives poor step coverage and alloy compositions drift, so sputtering dominates IC wiring. Evaporation is used for lift-off electrode patterning on compound semiconductors and for depositing OLED emissive layers.

Answer: A. It can evaporate even high-melting-point materials

Electron-beam evaporation focuses an electron beam accelerated through several kV onto the source material, vaporizing refractory metals such as tungsten and molybdenum and oxides that resistive heating cannot, with little crucible contamination. Atoms travel in straight lines, giving poor step coverage—which is exploited in lift-off, where film deposited over patterned resist is removed along with the resist to define electrodes.

FAQ

What is the difference between evaporation and sputtering?

Evaporation boils material off a heated source and the atoms arrive gently by line of sight; sputtering knocks atoms off a target with ions and they arrive with more energy. Sputtering gives better adhesion and alloy composition control, while evaporation suits lift-off and high-purity films.

Why does evaporation give poor step coverage?

In high vacuum the evaporated atoms travel in straight lines without scattering, so features shadow one another and vertical sidewalls receive very little material.

What is e-beam evaporation used for?

It is used to deposit high-melting-point metals and oxides with low contamination, and it is common for lift-off patterning of contacts on compound semiconductors and for optical coatings.
