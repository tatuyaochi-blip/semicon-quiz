What is ALE (atomic layer etching)?

Etching by alternating surface modification and removal so that each cycle removes about one atomic layer. It gives extremely precise control of etch depth.

Japanese name: ALE(原子層エッチング)

Field: Etching

The two-step cycle

Atomic layer etching (ALE) divides etching into separate, self-limiting half-steps. In a typical plasma ALE of silicon, chlorine is first introduced and adsorbs on the surface until it saturates, converting only the top layer into a weakly bonded chlorinated layer. After a purge, low-energy argon ions are applied with energy high enough to remove the modified layer but below the threshold for sputtering the unmodified silicon. Another purge follows and the cycle repeats. Each cycle removes a fixed amount, typically about 0.1–0.5 nm.

Why self-limitation matters

Because each step stops by itself, the etched depth is set by counting cycles rather than by timing a continuous etch. Removal per cycle is nearly the same in dense and isolated features, surfaces stay smooth, and the low ion energy causes little lattice damage. This makes ALE suited to steps that must stop within a few nanometers or etch films only a few atomic layers thick. The drawback is speed: gas switching and purges make each cycle slow, so ALE is usually reserved for the final, critical part of an etch.

Directional and thermal ALE

Plasma ALE, which uses ions in the removal step, is directional and can etch the bottom of a feature without attacking its sidewalls. Thermal ALE instead uses purely chemical reactions, for example fluorinating a metal oxide surface and then removing the fluoride with a ligand-exchange precursor, so it etches isotropically. Thermal ALE is useful where a precise, uniform lateral recess is needed inside three-dimensional structures that ions cannot reach directly.

ALE vs. ALD

ALE is the etching counterpart of atomic layer deposition (ALD), which builds films by alternating self-limiting precursor exposures. Both trade speed for atomic-scale control and uniformity. As transistor structures move to gate-all-around (GAA) nanosheets with layers only a few nanometers thick, ALE is entering use in gate-region processing and other steps where overetch must be minimal, and it is also studied for low-damage recess etching in compound-semiconductor devices.

Answer: B. It alternates surface modification and removal, controlling the etched amount at the atomic-layer level

ALE repeats self-limiting steps: first modify only the top surface layer, e.g., by chlorine adsorption, then remove just that layer with low-energy ions or similar. With a fixed removal of about 0.1–0.5 nm per cycle, it offers atomic-scale control, low surface damage and little dependence on pattern density. The etching counterpart of ALD, it is entering use in GAA and gate-region processing.

FAQ

What is the difference between ALE and ALD?

Both rely on alternating self-limiting surface reactions, but ALD adds material a fraction of a nanometer per cycle while ALE removes it. They are often described as mirror-image processes.

How much material does one ALE cycle remove?

Typically about 0.1–0.5 nm per cycle, roughly one atomic layer or less, depending on the material and chemistry. Total depth is set by the number of cycles.

Why is atomic layer etching not used for every etch step?

Each cycle needs separate modification, purge and removal steps, so the net etch rate is far lower than continuous plasma etching. It is used where precision and low damage justify the lower throughput.
