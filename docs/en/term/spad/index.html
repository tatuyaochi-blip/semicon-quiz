What is SPAD (single-photon avalanche diode)?

A photodetector biased above breakdown so that even a single photon triggers a large avalanche pulse. It is used for ranging, such as in LiDAR.

Japanese name: SPAD(単一光子アバランシェダイオード)

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Geiger-mode operation

A single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) is reverse-biased above its breakdown voltage by an excess bias, typically a few volts. In this Geiger mode a single photogenerated carrier can trigger a self-sustaining avalanche that produces a large, easily detected current pulse. The pulse looks the same whether one photon or several arrived, so the device counts events rather than measuring intensity. Because the avalanche would otherwise continue, a quench circuit, either a series resistor (passive) or an active sensing circuit, lowers the bias to stop it and then restores the bias.

Key figures of merit

Photon detection probability is the chance that an arriving photon produces a pulse. Dark count rate counts avalanches triggered by thermally generated or tunneling carriers with no light at all. Afterpulsing happens when carriers trapped during one avalanche are released after recharge and trigger a false pulse. Dead time, while the diode is being quenched and recharged, limits the maximum count rate. Timing jitter, often tens to hundreds of picoseconds, sets ranging precision. Raising the excess bias improves detection probability but also increases dark counts and afterpulsing.

SPAD vs. APD vs. PIN photodiode

A PIN photodiode has no internal gain: each absorbed photon yields at most one electron-hole pair, and an external amplifier must lift the signal above noise. An avalanche photodiode (APD) operates just below breakdown in linear mode, multiplying the photocurrent tens to hundreds of times while keeping the output proportional to the light. A SPAD crosses breakdown and gives up proportionality for effectively unlimited gain on each detection, which is what makes single-photon counting and precise photon timing possible.

Where you will meet it

Direct time-of-flight LiDAR and smartphone ranging sensors fire a short laser pulse and time the returning photons with a time-to-digital converter; every nanosecond of round trip corresponds to 15 cm of distance. Because individual detections are noisy, many pulses are accumulated into a histogram, a technique known as time-correlated single-photon counting. The same method measures fluorescence lifetimes, and SPADs also appear in quantum optics. Silicon SPADs can be built in CMOS processes as large arrays, often stacked on logic chips.

Answer: B. It uses avalanche multiplication to amplify the photocurrent internally and can detect very weak light

An APD is a photodiode biased near breakdown so that light-generated carriers are multiplied tens to hundreds of times by avalanche multiplication. This internal gain lets it detect weak light that would otherwise be buried in the following amplifier's noise. APDs are used in optical-communication receivers and LiDAR, and in Geiger mode (SPAD), biased above breakdown, even a single photon produces a large pulse, used in ranging and quantum optics.

Answer: A. SPAD (single-photon avalanche diode)

A SPAD operates above breakdown (Geiger mode), so even one photon-generated electron triggers an avalanche producing a large current pulse. The pulse size does not depend on photon number, making it a photon-counting device. A quench circuit stops the avalanche to prepare for the next photon. With picosecond-level timing precision, SPADs are used in LiDAR, smartphone ranging sensors and fluorescence-lifetime measurement.

FAQ

What is the difference between an APD and a SPAD?

An APD is biased below breakdown and gives an output proportional to light intensity with moderate gain. A SPAD is biased above breakdown, so each detected photon produces a full-size pulse that is counted digitally.

What is dark count rate in a SPAD?

It is the rate of avalanche pulses that occur without any light, caused by thermally generated or tunneling carriers. It sets the noise floor and rises with temperature and excess bias.

Why does a SPAD need a quenching circuit?

Above breakdown the avalanche sustains itself, so the bias must be pulled down to stop it and then restored before the next photon can be detected.
