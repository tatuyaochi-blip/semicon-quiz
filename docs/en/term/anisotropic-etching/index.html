What is Anisotropic etching?

Etching that proceeds only in a particular direction (mainly vertically). Needed to pattern fine features.

Japanese name: 異方性エッチング

Anisotropic etching appears in 4 practice questions across 1 topic, including Etching.

Field: Etching

Measuring anisotropy

Anisotropy compares lateral and vertical etch rates and is often expressed as A = 1 − (lateral rate / vertical rate): A = 1 is perfectly vertical, A = 0 is isotropic. In practice engineers quote the taper angle, the sidewall angle from the surface, with values near 90° meaning highly anisotropic. Anisotropic etching transfers the mask dimension into the film with almost no undercut, enabling features tens of nanometers wide and several micrometers deep. An isotropic etch would widen each opening by about the etch depth on each side.

How plasmas make etching directional

An RF-driven wafer electrode charges to a negative DC self-bias, tens to hundreds of volts, because mobile electrons reach it more easily than ions. Positive ions accelerate across the sheath nearly perpendicular to the wafer, especially at low pressure where collisions are rare. Ion bombardment strongly enhances chemical etching where it lands, so feature bottoms etch much faster than sidewalls. Polymer or oxide-like passivation on the sidewalls blocks lateral attack while ions clear it from the bottom. Deep silicon etching often alternates an SF6 etch step with a C4F8 passivation step, the Bosch process, leaving small scallops on the walls.

Profile control and defects

Too much passivation narrows the bottom and tapers the profile; too little lets radicals and scattered ions bow the sidewalls outward. Ions reflecting off sloped walls or deflected by charging can cause microtrenching at the bottom corners. Etch rate also tends to fall as aspect ratio increases. Taper is chosen per application: features to be filled later often benefit from about 85° for void-free filling, while gates and fins are made nearly vertical to keep top and bottom dimensions equal.

Anisotropic wet etching is different

The term also describes orientation-dependent wet etching of single-crystal silicon. Alkaline solutions such as KOH or TMAH etch the (111) planes far more slowly than (100) planes, so on a (100) wafer they form V-grooves and pits with sidewalls at about 54.7° to the surface. This crystallographic anisotropy is widely used in MEMS for cavities and membranes, but it is not the ion-driven vertical anisotropy used for patterning fine circuit features.

Answer: A. Etching proceeds mainly vertically and hardly at all laterally

In anisotropic etching, vertically accelerated ion bombardment and sidewall-protecting passivation make etching proceed preferentially downward, with almost no lateral undercut beneath the mask. Mask patterns are thus transferred without dimensional change, enabling fine vertical features such as tens of nm wide and several µm deep. Etching that proceeds equally in all directions is isotropic.

FAQ

What is the difference between anisotropic and isotropic etching?

Anisotropic etching proceeds mainly in one direction, usually straight down, so the pattern keeps the mask's width. Isotropic etching proceeds equally in all directions and undercuts the mask, as most wet etches do.

Can wet etching be anisotropic?

Yes, in a crystallographic sense: KOH or TMAH etch silicon much more slowly along (111) planes, producing sloped facets at about 54.7° on (100) wafers. For vertical sidewalls in fine patterns, plasma dry etching is required.

What is the Bosch process?

It is a deep reactive ion etching method for silicon that alternates short SF6 etch steps with C4F8 passivation steps. It can etch very deep vertical trenches and through-silicon vias, leaving characteristic scalloped sidewalls.
