What is Arrhenius model?

A model in which reaction or degradation rates vary with temperature as exp(−Ea/kT). It is used to estimate lifetime at operating temperature from accelerated tests at higher temperatures.

Japanese name: アレニウスモデル

Arrhenius model appears in 3 practice questions across 2 topics, including Reliability, SRAM and flash memory.

Field: Reliability

The equation and the acceleration factor

The Arrhenius model states that the rate of a thermally activated process is proportional to exp(−Ea/kT), where Ea is the activation energy, k is Boltzmann's constant (about 8.62e-5 eV/K) and T is absolute temperature. If lifetime is inversely proportional to the degradation rate, the acceleration factor between a stress temperature and a use temperature is AF = exp[(Ea/k)(1/Tuse − 1/Tstress)], with both temperatures in kelvin. For example, with Ea = 0.7 eV, stressing at 125 °C instead of using at 55 °C accelerates aging by roughly 78 times.

Activation energy decides everything

Because Ea sits in the exponent, the acceleration factor is very sensitive to it, and each failure mechanism has its own value; figures around 0.5-1 eV are commonly used. A mistaken Ea can change a lifetime estimate by an order of magnitude or more. The stress temperature must also be low enough that the same mechanism dominates as in use; if a new mechanism appears only at high temperature, extrapolation becomes invalid. Many models combine the Arrhenius term with other stresses, for example current density in Black's equation for electromigration.

The 10 °C rule of thumb

A familiar shortcut says reaction rates roughly double for every 10 °C rise, so lowering operating temperature by 10 °C roughly doubles lifetime. Near room temperature this corresponds to an activation energy of about 0.55 eV. For mechanisms with larger Ea the temperature sensitivity is greater, and at higher temperatures the same Ea gives a smaller factor per 10 °C, so the rule is only a rough guide. It still explains clearly why cooling electronics extends their life.

Where it is applied

High-temperature operating life (HTOL) tests run devices under bias at elevated temperature, often 125 °C, for on the order of 1000 hours, and the acceleration factor converts that to equivalent years at use conditions. Data-retention bakes for flash memory use the same idea, because stored charge leaks out faster when hot. Where voltage or humidity also accelerates failure, the Arrhenius term is combined with additional factors, as in Eyring-type or humidity models, to estimate field lifetime.

Try it: questions about Arrhenius model

Under which condition does the data retention of flash memory tend to degrade?

  1. AWhen stored at low temperature
  2. BWhen stored in a dark place
  3. CWhen stored at high temperature
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. When stored at high temperature

Stored electrons gain thermal energy and escape through the dielectric more easily at high temperature, so retention shortens when stored hot. This is exploited in accelerated tests that bake devices to estimate long-term retention quickly (the Arrhenius model). Oxide worn by many program/erase cycles worsens retention further, so leaving a heavily used SSD unpowered for long periods is best avoided.

In accelerated tests that raise temperature to shorten life test time, which model is often used to express the relationship between temperature and lifetime?

  1. AMoore's law
  2. BArrhenius model
  3. COhm's law
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Arrhenius model

The Arrhenius model holds that degradation rate is proportional to exp(−Ea/kT), where Ea is activation energy, k Boltzmann's constant and T absolute temperature, so lifetime shortens exponentially as temperature rises. It is used to estimate lifetime at use temperature from high-temperature tests. Ea differs by failure mechanism, with values around 0.5–1 eV commonly used. Moore's law and Ohm's law have nothing to do with lifetime.

FAQ

How do you calculate the Arrhenius acceleration factor?

Use AF = exp[(Ea/k)(1/Tuse − 1/Tstress)] with temperatures in kelvin and k = 8.62e-5 eV/K. For Ea = 0.7 eV, 125 °C stress versus 55 °C use gives about 78.

What activation energy should be used in reliability tests?

It depends on the failure mechanism and should come from data or established values for that mechanism; numbers around 0.5-1 eV are common. Using a generic value without justification can badly misestimate lifetime.

Does lifetime double for every 10 °C decrease in temperature?

Roughly, for an activation energy near 0.55 eV around room temperature. Mechanisms with higher activation energy are more temperature-sensitive, so the rule is only an approximation.
