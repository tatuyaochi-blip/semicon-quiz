What is ISO 14644-1?

The international standard that defines cleanroom cleanliness classes by the number of airborne particles. The smaller the number, the cleaner.

Japanese name: ISO 14644-1

How the classes are defined

ISO 14644-1 assigns a class number N from 1 to 9 based on the maximum allowed concentration of airborne particles per cubic meter. For particles of 0.1 µm and larger, Class N allows at most 10^N per m³, so each step down in N cuts the allowed count tenfold. Limits for other particle sizes D (in µm) follow the formula 10^N × (0.1/D)^2.08, which is why the allowed count drops quickly for larger particles. Class 5, for example, permits 100,000 particles of 0.1 µm or larger but only 3,520 of 0.5 µm or larger per m³.

Mapping to the old Federal Standard

Many engineers still use the classes of the withdrawn US Federal Standard 209E, which counted particles of 0.5 µm or larger per cubic foot. Since one cubic foot is about 28.3 liters, Federal Class 100 (100 particles per ft³) corresponds to roughly 3,500 per m³, almost exactly ISO Class 5. The rest of the scale lines up the same way: Federal Class 10 is about ISO 4, Class 1,000 about ISO 6 and Class 10,000 about ISO 7.

Measurement and occupancy states

Classification is done with optical particle counters, which detect particles by the light they scatter, sampling air at a number of locations that grows with the floor area of the room. The class applies to a stated occupancy state: as-built (empty room with services running), at-rest (equipment installed but no people) or operational (normal work in progress). People and moving equipment are major particle sources, so a room certified at rest may perform worse in operation. The standard covers only airborne particle concentration, not mass; temperature, humidity, lighting and chemical contamination are controlled separately and are not part of the class.

Cleanliness in a semiconductor fab

A particle comparable in size to a pattern feature can kill a die, so the air that wafers actually touch must be extremely clean, around ISO Class 1 to 3 in advanced fabs. Rather than making a whole building that clean, modern fabs keep wafers inside sealed carriers and tool minienvironments with filtered downflow, while the surrounding bays can be held at a less stringent class. Cleanliness is maintained with HEPA or ULPA (high-efficiency and ultra-low-penetration air) filters in the ceiling, unidirectional airflow that sweeps particles downward, gowning rules and slightly positive room pressure.

Answer: A. ISO Class 5

Former US Federal Standard Class 100 allows no more than 100 particles of 0.5 µm or larger per cubic foot (about 28.3 L), or roughly 3,500 per m³. ISO 14644-1 Class 5 allows 3,520 per m³ at 0.5 µm, making them nearly equivalent. Each step down in ISO class divides the allowed count by 10, so Class 1 is extremely strict and Class 9 is like ordinary room air.

Answer: C. Each step down in the class number N cuts the allowed particle count by about a factor of 10

ISO 14644-1 sets the limits for each class and particle size in a table; the values follow the formula 10^N × (0.1/D)^2.08 (D in µm), so ISO Class N allows at most 10^N particles of 0.1 µm or larger per m³. Class 5 thus allows 100000 and Class 4 10000; lower numbers are cleaner. Classes are based on particle count concentration, not mass. Advanced fabs require about Class 1–3 in the spaces wafers touch.

FAQ

Is ISO Class 1 cleaner than ISO Class 9?

Yes. Lower class numbers are cleaner; Class 1 allows at most 10 particles of 0.1 µm or larger per cubic meter, while Class 9 is roughly ordinary room air.

What is ISO Class 5 equivalent to?

ISO Class 5 is essentially the old US Federal Standard Class 100, allowing about 3,520 particles of 0.5 µm or larger per cubic meter, which is about 100 per cubic foot.

How many particles are allowed in an ISO Class 7 cleanroom?

About 352,000 particles of 0.5 µm or larger per cubic meter, corresponding to the old Federal Standard Class 10,000.
