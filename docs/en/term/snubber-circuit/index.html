What is Snubber circuit?

A network such as a resistor and capacitor placed across a power device to suppress the voltage spikes (surges) and ringing that occur during switching.

Japanese name: スナバ回路

Snubber circuit appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Power semiconductors, Power Electronics Circuits.

Field: Power semiconductors

Why surges and ringing occur

When a power device turns off, the current flowing through the parasitic inductance L of the wiring and components drops suddenly, and a voltage V = L·di/dt adds to the supply voltage, producing a voltage spike (surge) across the device. This inductance also resonates with the device's output capacitance and other capacitances, making voltage and current oscillate (ringing). A surge exceeding the device's voltage rating can destroy it, and ringing causes electromagnetic interference (EMI). Fast-switching devices such as SiC and GaN have larger di/dt and dv/dt, so countermeasures matter more.

Types of snubbers

The most basic is the RC snubber, a resistor and capacitor in series placed across the device: the capacitor absorbs energy and the resistor damps the oscillation. An RCD snubber adds a diode in parallel with the resistor, so during a surge the capacitor charges quickly through the diode and the stored energy is discharged slowly through the resistor. Power modules often use a C snubber, a small capacitor mounted directly across the supply terminals. Flyback converters commonly use an RCD clamp to limit the surge caused by the transformer's leakage inductance. Relay and switch contacts use RC snubbers to suppress arcing.

Rules of thumb for choosing values

First measure the ringing frequency f0. Then add a known capacitance Cadd across the device; if the frequency falls to f1, find the parasitic capacitance Cp from (f0/f1)² = 1 + Cadd/Cp, and the parasitic inductance L from f0 = 1/(2π√(L·Cp)). A damping resistor of about the characteristic impedance √(L/Cp) is a good starting point, and a capacitor of a few times Cp damps the oscillation well. In an RC snubber, charging and discharging the capacitor every switching cycle turns energy into heat in the resistor, so the loss is roughly C·V²·f, where f is the switching frequency. Values are chosen by balancing loss against suppression.

Design cautions

Because a snubber adds loss, the first step is to reduce the parasitic inductance itself, for example with short, wide wiring or laminated busbars. Place the snubber as close as possible to the device or supply terminals, using low-inductance film or ceramic capacitors and low-inductance resistors. Check the resistor's heating and the capacitor's ripple-current rating. Raising the gate resistance to slow switching also reduces the surge, but it increases switching loss, so it is weighed against a snubber.

Answer: B. To suppress voltage spikes (surges) and ringing that occur during switching

In fast switching, parasitic wiring inductance resonates with device capacitance, causing voltage overshoot (surge) and oscillation (ringing). Snubbers—RC networks or combinations with diodes—absorb this energy, protecting the device from overvoltage and reducing noise. The absorbed energy is dissipated as heat in the resistor, so component values are chosen to balance protection against loss.

FAQ

What is a snubber circuit?

It is a circuit placed across a power device or switch contacts to suppress the voltage spikes (surges) and oscillations (ringing) caused by parasitic inductance and similar effects during switching. The RC snubber, combining a resistor and capacitor, is the most basic form.

What is the difference between an RC and an RCD snubber?

An RC snubber is a resistor and capacitor in series; it is simple and good at damping ringing. An RCD snubber adds a diode across the resistor, so during a surge the capacitor charges quickly through the diode and discharges slowly through the resistor. This gives it a stronger clamping action on the peak surge voltage.

How much power does a snubber dissipate?

In an RC snubber, the energy stored in the capacitor is turned into heat in the resistor as it charges and discharges every switching cycle, so the loss is roughly C·V²·f. For example, 100 pF at 400 V and 100 kHz gives 100×10⁻¹² × 400² × 10⁵ = 1.6 W. A larger capacitance damps oscillation better but also increases the loss.
