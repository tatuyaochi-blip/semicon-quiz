What is Pad conditioning?

Abrading the surface of a CMP polishing pad with a diamond tool to restore its texture and slurry-holding ability, stabilizing the removal rate. Also called dressing.

Japanese name: パッドコンディショニング

Field: CMP and cleaning

Why conditioning is needed

CMP polishing pads are often made of foamed polyurethane with fine pores. The fine surface roughness (asperities) and the pores hold the slurry, distribute abrasive particles between pad and wafer, and carry away polishing debris. As polishing continues, however, the asperities are flattened (glazing) and debris and agglomerated particles clog the pores, so the pad can no longer hold the slurry. As a result, the removal rate falls and varies from wafer to wafer. Pad conditioning is the operation of abrading the pad surface slightly to regenerate its roughness and stabilize the removal rate.

Dressing with a diamond disk

Conditioning uses a disk with diamond grit fixed to a metal plate (a conditioner or dresser). The rotating disk is pressed against the pad and swept back and forth along the pad's radius to abrade the surface evenly. There are in-situ conditioning, done at the same time as wafer polishing, and ex-situ conditioning, done between polishes; conditioning during polishing is now the mainstream. The downforce, rotation speed, and sweep range and speed are adjusted to control the pad's surface roughness and how much is removed.

Effects on polishing results

Conditioning conditions affect not only the removal rate but also within-wafer uniformity and defects. If the amount removed varies along the pad radius, the pad thickness and groove depth vary by location, causing different removal rates at the wafer center and edge. If diamond grains fall out or chip, fragments left on the pad can scratch wafers. Disks are therefore engineered in diamond size, shape, arrangement and bonding strength, and the pad is also cleaned to keep debris from remaining on it.

Balancing against pad life

Aggressive conditioning stabilizes the removal rate but wears the pad faster and shortens its life. The pad surface has grooves cut into it to channel slurry, and as the pad wears and the grooves become shallower the slurry flow changes, so pads are replaced after a set amount of use. The diamonds on the disk also wear with use and cut less effectively, so disks are replaced periodically as well. Both pads and disks are consumables, and each replacement requires stopping the tool and running a break-in, so conditioning conditions are set by balancing polishing stability against consumable cost and tool uptime.

The fine asperities and pores on the polishing pad surface hold the slurry and drive polishing. Pad conditioning lightly abrades the pad surface with a diamond-grit disk to regenerate flattened asperities. More aggressive conditioning regenerates the texture well and tends to stabilize the removal rate, but it also wears the pad away faster. FAQ

Are pad conditioning and dressing the same?

They refer to the same operation: abrading the surface of a CMP polishing pad with a diamond tool to restore its texture and slurry-holding ability. The tool is called a conditioner or a dresser.

What happens without conditioning?

The pad's surface roughness is flattened and the pores clog with debris, so the pad can no longer hold slurry and the removal rate keeps falling. If the removal rate varies from wafer to wafer, controlling film thickness becomes difficult.

What is the difference between in-situ and ex-situ conditioning?

In-situ conditioning abrades the pad while wafers are being polished; it makes it easier to keep the removal rate constant and is now the mainstream. Ex-situ conditioning abrades the pad between wafer polishes, so the pad condition gradually changes during each polish.
