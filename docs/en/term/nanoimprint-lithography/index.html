What is Nanoimprint lithography?

Transferring patterns by pressing a mold with fine features into resist on the wafer. It can make fine patterns without expensive optics.

Japanese name: ナノインプリントリソグラフィ

Nanoimprint lithography appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Lithography.

Field: Lithography

Copying a pattern with a mold

Nanoimprint lithography (NIL) copies a pattern by pressing a template (mold) bearing a relief pattern into resist on the wafer. In UV nanoimprint, the mainstream approach for semiconductors, a template of quartz or another UV-transparent material is pressed into liquid resist; once the resist has filled the gaps, UV light cures it and the template is separated. In schemes that dispense resist by inkjet, the amount can be varied from place to place to match the pattern density. Because the template's shape is copied directly rather than forming an optical image, there is no resolution limit from diffraction.

Advantages and applications

Without large reduction-projection optics like those of a scanner, or a high-power light source as in EUV, tool cost and power consumption can be kept down. Because the template's shape is copied as is, features in the 10 nm range can be transferred, and three-dimensional shapes such as stepped profiles can be formed in a single step. In semiconductors, work has proceeded toward practical use in memories such as 3D NAND, and the technique is also researched and used for fine structures in optical elements and for biochips. Canon has developed nanoimprint tools for semiconductors and, as of the early 2020s, was working to apply them in volume production.

Challenges

Because the template touches the wafer directly, a trapped particle creates defects on both template and wafer, and a damaged template copies its defects again and again. Defect control is therefore the biggest challenge. The template also transfers the pattern at 1:1 without reduction, so the template itself must have the same fine dimensions and accuracy as the wafer pattern. Typically the master template is made by electron-beam writing, and replica templates copied from it are used in production. Overlay accuracy to the previous layer, throughput limited by the time for resist to fill the template, and template lifetime are further challenges.

Compared with optical lithography

Optical lithography projects a reduced image of the mask pattern onto the resist through optics, changes solubility through chemical reactions, and then develops it. Nanoimprint has no development step: the resist relief exists the moment the template is separated. A thin layer of resist (residual layer) remains at the bottom of the pressed areas, however, so it is removed by etching before the underlying layer is processed. As of the early 2020s, most layers of leading-edge logic were made with ArF immersion or EUV lithography, and nanoimprint was at the stage of being considered for selected applications.

FAQ

What is nanoimprint lithography, in simple terms?

A technique that presses a template with a fine relief pattern into resist on the wafer, cures it with UV light or similar, and copies the shape. Free of diffraction limits, it can make features in the 10 nm range without expensive optics.

What are its advantages and challenges compared with EUV lithography?

Its advantage is that it needs no large optics or high-power light source, keeping tool cost and power consumption down. On the other hand, because the template touches the wafer directly, defects from particles and template wear are more likely, and overlay accuracy and throughput are also challenges.

How are templates made?

Because the pattern is copied 1:1, the template must have the same fine dimensions as the wafer pattern. Typically a master template is made by electron-beam writing, and replica templates copied from it by nanoimprint are used in production.
