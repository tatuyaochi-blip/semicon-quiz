What is Implant dose?

The number of ions implanted per unit area in ion implantation, expressed in cm⁻². It is set by beam current and implant time.

Japanese name: ドーズ量

Implant dose appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Ion implantation.

Field: Ion implantation

Definition and unit of dose

Dose is the number of ions implanted per unit area of the wafer in ion implantation, expressed in ions/cm² (or atoms/cm², cm⁻²). It is an areal density that says nothing about the depth distribution, so it has different dimensions from a volume concentration (cm⁻³); integrating the implanted concentration over depth gives the dose. For example, 10¹⁵ cm⁻² is comparable to the number of atoms in a single layer of the silicon surface (about 7×10¹⁴ cm⁻²). Depth is set mainly by acceleration energy and concentration mainly by dose, and being able to set the two independently is a key strength of ion implantation.

Calculating dose from beam current and time

An ion implanter measures beam current with a Faraday cup and controls dose by integrating it. For singly charged ions, dose = current × time ÷ (elementary charge × area). Implanting 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻² into an area of 100 cm², for example, requires 1×10¹⁷ ions, or a charge of 1.6×10⁻² C, which takes 16 seconds at a beam current of 1 mA. A doubly charged ion carries twice the charge, so the same amount of charge counts only half as many ions. In practice the beam is scanned and in-wafer non-uniformity is corrected during implantation.

Dose ranges by application

Doses used in LSI manufacturing span more than five orders of magnitude, from about 10¹¹ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻². Threshold-voltage adjustment and similar steps use about 10¹¹–10¹³ cm⁻², lowering the resistance of sources, drains and gates uses around 10¹⁵ cm⁻², and Smart Cut wafer splitting implants hydrogen at 10¹⁶ cm⁻² or more. Medium-current, high-current and high-energy implanters are matched to these ranges. High-dose implants can amorphize the crystal and harden the resist mask so that it is hard to strip, which adds process concerns.

Dose, concentration and sheet resistance

Approximating the implanted profile as a Gaussian, the peak concentration is roughly dose ÷ (2.5 × ΔRp), where ΔRp is the standard deviation of the spread in depth. Implanting 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻² with ΔRp = 20 nm, for example, gives a peak of about 2×10²⁰ cm⁻³. After an activation anneal the sheet resistance is roughly 1/(q × active dose × average mobility), falling as dose rises, with about 100 Ω/□ at 10¹⁵ cm⁻² as a rule of thumb. Above the solid solubility, however, more of the dopant cannot be activated, so further increases in dose lower sheet resistance less and less.

Answer: A. It decreases

Higher dose means more dopant and carriers in the implanted layer, so sheet resistance falls—roughly 1/(q × active dose × average mobility), around 100 Ω/□ for 10¹⁵ cm⁻². Beyond the solid solubility, however, much of the extra dopant stays inactive and mobility declines, so further dose increases yield diminishing reductions in sheet resistance.

FAQ

What is the unit of implant dose?

Dose is the number of ions per unit area, so it is expressed in ions/cm² (atoms/cm², or cm⁻²). eV is the unit of acceleration energy and cm⁻³ the unit of volume concentration, which are different quantities from dose.

How do you get dopant concentration from dose?

Dividing the dose by the depth over which the dopant is distributed gives the average concentration. For example, 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻² spread evenly over 100 nm (1×10⁻⁵ cm) gives 1×10²⁰ cm⁻³. For a Gaussian approximation, the peak concentration can be estimated as dose ÷ (2.5 × ΔRp).

Is it the same as 'dose' in lithography?

No. In lithography, dose means the areal density of exposure energy delivered to the resist (for example in mJ/cm²) or of charge delivered in electron-beam writing (for example in µC/cm²). Implant dose is the areal density of implanted ions. What they share is that both express how much was delivered per unit area.
