What is Black's equation?

An empirical formula for interconnect lifetime under electromigration in terms of current density and temperature. Lifetime is inversely proportional to a power of current density and falls exponentially as temperature rises.

Japanese name: ブラックの式

Black's equation appears in 3 practice questions across 3 topics, including Interconnect processes, Metrology and analysis, Reliability.

Field: Interconnect processes

The equation and its terms

Black's equation is an empirical model for the median or mean time to failure of a wire under electromigration: MTTF = A·J^(−n)·exp(Ea/kT). J is the current density, n the current-density exponent (2 in Black's original form, with values between about 1 and 2 reported), Ea the activation energy of the dominant atomic diffusion path, k Boltzmann's constant, T the absolute temperature, and A a constant that captures material, geometry and microstructure. Ea is on the order of 0.5 to 1 eV, depending on the metal and on whether atoms move mainly along grain boundaries or interfaces.

Using it to extrapolate test data

Electromigration failures at use conditions would take years to appear, so test structures are stressed at elevated temperatures, around 300 °C, and high current densities, then the equation extrapolates the results. The acceleration factor is (J_test/J_use)^n multiplied by exp[(Ea/k)(1/T_use − 1/T_test)]. The sensitivity is large: with n = 2, halving current density quadruples lifetime, and with Ea near 0.9 eV, raising temperature from 105 °C to 125 °C shortens lifetime by roughly a factor of four. Fitting n and Ea requires tests at several currents and temperatures.

The physics behind it

Electromigration happens because conduction electrons transfer momentum to metal atoms, slowly pushing them in the direction of electron flow. Where more atoms leave a region than arrive, such as at a via or grain-boundary junction, voids form and eventually open the line; where atoms pile up, hillocks can short to neighbours. In copper, transport is dominated by the top interface with the dielectric cap, so cobalt caps can extend lifetime substantially. Black's equation does not capture back-stress effects: very short lines can be immune because opposing mechanical stress halts atom flow (the Blech effect).

How it shapes design rules

Foundries translate Black's equation and test data into maximum allowed current densities per metal layer, width and temperature, typically on the order of MA/cm². Power and ground lines carrying steady DC current in one direction are the most critical. Signal lines with alternating current partly recover because atoms move back and forth, so their limits are less strict. Joule heating raises the local temperature of heavily loaded wires, which feeds back through the exponential term. Sign-off flows therefore check electromigration together with IR drop on the power grid.

Answer: B. High current density and high temperature

Black's equation gives mean time to failure as MTTF = A·J⁻ⁿ·exp(Ea/kT): inversely proportional to current density J to the power n (about 2) and exponentially shorter at higher temperature. Tests therefore accelerate failure at elevated current density and temperature (e.g., around 300 °C) and extrapolate to use conditions with the equation. Designs impose per-wire current-density limits, on the order of MA/cm².

FAQ

What is Black's equation used for?

It estimates interconnect lifetime under electromigration as a function of current density and temperature. Engineers use it to extrapolate accelerated test results to operating conditions and to set current-density limits for wires.

How does temperature affect electromigration lifetime?

Lifetime falls exponentially with temperature through the exp(Ea/kT) term. For an activation energy near 0.9 eV, a 20 °C rise around 105 to 125 °C cuts lifetime by roughly four times.

What is the current density exponent n in Black's equation?

It describes how strongly lifetime depends on current density; Black originally used n = 2. Measured values range roughly from 1 to 2 depending on whether void nucleation or void growth limits lifetime.
