What is Dark current?

Current that flows in a photodiode even without light, caused for example by thermal generation of carriers. It rises with temperature and appears as noise in dark images.

Japanese name: 暗電流

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Where dark current comes from

In a reverse-biased photodiode, any electron–hole pair generated inside or near the depletion region is swept out and counted, whether light created it or not. Without light, pairs are still generated thermally, mostly through Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) generation at deep levels in the band gap; traps near midgap from metal contamination, crystal defects and silicon/oxide interface states are the most effective. Minority carriers diffusing in from neutral regions add a further component. Because both mechanisms depend on the intrinsic carrier concentration, which grows exponentially with temperature and with decreasing band gap, dark current is strongly temperature dependent.

How it appears in images

An image sensor pixel integrates dark current along with photocurrent, so dark signal grows with exposure time and temperature, roughly doubling every 6–8 °C in silicon. It adds an offset, and because the dark electrons arrive randomly it also adds shot noise proportional to the square root of the dark charge. Pixel-to-pixel variation in dark current is part of fixed-pattern noise, and isolated defective pixels become hot pixels in long exposures. Offset can be subtracted using optically black reference pixels or a dark frame, but the shot noise cannot, which is why astronomical cameras cool their sensors.

The pinned photodiode and other fixes

The silicon/oxide interface is rich in interface states, a major generation source. A pinned, or buried, photodiode places its n-type collection region below the surface and covers it with a heavily doped p+ layer, which fills those states with holes and greatly suppresses generation; the fully depleted n-region also allows complete charge transfer, reducing lag and reset noise. Clean processing and gettering reduce metallic traps, and the trench walls of deep trench isolation (DTI) between pixels must be passivated so the isolation that fights crosstalk does not itself add dark current.

Dark current vs. read noise, bias and materials

Dark current differs from read noise: read noise comes from the readout chain once per frame regardless of exposure, while dark current accumulates with time. Bias matters too; operating a photodiode at zero bias (photovoltaic mode) lowers dark current for precise weak-light measurement, whereas reverse bias speeds response at the cost of more leakage. Material choice is decisive. Germanium detectors for 1.31 and 1.55 µm telecom bands suffer higher dark current because of germanium's smaller band gap of about 0.66 eV and dislocations from the roughly 4% lattice mismatch with silicon.

Answer: B. Current that flows even without light, and increases with temperature

Dark current is charge generated thermally in the photodiode without light, arising mainly from interface states, crystal defects and metal-contamination levels. It causes noise and white spots ("hot pixels") in low-light shots and long exposures, and roughly doubles every 6–8 °C. This is why astronomical cameras cool their sensors.

Answer: C. Si and Ge differ in lattice constant by about 4%, so crystal defects such as dislocations readily form in the grown film

Dark current is current that flows even when no light is incident; it adds noise to the photodetector and degrades sensitivity. Because Si and Ge differ in lattice constant by about 4%, growing Ge on Si creates defects such as (threading) dislocations as strain is relieved, and carriers are readily generated through defect levels. In addition, Ge's band gap (about 0.66 eV) is smaller than Si's, so more carriers are thermally generated. Dark current is suppressed by reducing dislocations with post-growth annealing or by shrinking the detector area. Ge is an indirect-gap material, and light emission has almost no effect.

FAQ

Why does dark current increase with temperature?

Dark current comes from thermal generation of electron–hole pairs, and the intrinsic carrier concentration that drives it rises exponentially with temperature. In silicon image sensors it roughly doubles every 6–8 °C.

How do you reduce dark current noise in a camera?

Cool the sensor, shorten the exposure and subtract a dark frame or optically black reference to remove the offset. At the device level, pinned photodiodes and clean, well-passivated interfaces reduce the generation itself.

Is dark current the same as leakage current?

Dark current is the reverse leakage of a photodetector observed with no light, so it is a specific case of junction leakage. It is called dark current because it sets the detector's signal floor in darkness.
