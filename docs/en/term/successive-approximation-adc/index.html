What is Successive-approximation ADC?

An ADC that uses an internal DAC and comparator to determine one bit at a time, starting from the most significant. It balances speed, accuracy and power well.

Japanese name: 逐次比較型ADC

Field: A/D and D/A conversion

Binary search, one bit per step

A successive-approximation-register (SAR) ADC first samples the input onto a hold capacitor. The control logic then sets the most significant bit of an internal DAC to give mid-scale, and a comparator decides whether the input is above or below it; the bit is kept or cleared accordingly. The next bit is then tried, halving the remaining range each time. After N comparisons the register holds the N-bit result, so conversion time grows only linearly with resolution.

The capacitive DAC

In most CMOS SAR ADCs the DAC is an array of binary-weighted capacitors that also serves as the sampling capacitor, an approach called charge redistribution. During conversion the bottom plates of the capacitors are switched between reference voltages, and the comparator watches the resulting top-plate voltage. Because the array draws energy mainly when it switches and there is only one comparator, power scales roughly with sample rate, which is why SAR converters are popular in microcontrollers and battery-powered sensors.

Driving the input

At the start of acquisition the input sees a sampling capacitance of a few to tens of picofarads that must charge within a short acquisition time. Settling to within half an LSB of an N-bit converter takes about (N+1)·ln2 time constants, roughly 11.8 time constants at 16 bits. A high-impedance source cannot meet this, so a low-output-impedance op-amp driver is used together with an RC filter whose capacitor (a charge bucket) supplies the instantaneous charge kick.

SAR vs. flash and delta-sigma ADCs

A flash ADC uses 2ᴺ − 1 comparators to convert in a single step, so it is very fast but power-hungry and limited to low resolution. A delta-sigma ADC oversamples and digitally filters to reach high resolution, but its bandwidth is lower and its digital filter adds latency. SAR ADCs sit in between, commonly offering roughly 10 to 18 bits at sample rates up to a few megasamples per second, and they deliver each result with no pipeline delay, which suits multiplexed channels.

A SAR ADC connects its internal capacitance (a few to tens of pF) to the input at sampling, so a high-impedance source cannot charge it within the short acquisition time and the captured voltage is inaccurate. Settling to N-bit accuracy takes about (N+1)·ln2 time constants. The fix combines a low-output-impedance driver with an RC filter (charge bucket) that supplies the instantaneous charge.

  1. A1
  2. B2ᴺ
  3. CN

Answer: C. N

A SAR ADC performs a binary search, comparing the input with its internal DAC from the MSB down and settling one bit per comparison, so N bits basically need N comparisons. At 12 bits and 1 MS/s, each comparison gets only tens of nanoseconds. Some designs add redundant bits so that early decision errors can be corrected later, trading a few extra comparisons for higher speed and accuracy.

FAQ

How many clock cycles does a SAR ADC take?

At least one comparison per bit, so N cycles for N bits, plus the acquisition time and sometimes extra cycles for redundant bits that correct early decision errors.

Why does a SAR ADC need a driver amplifier?

Its switched sampling capacitor draws a burst of charge at each acquisition, and a high-impedance source cannot recharge it accurately in time; a low-impedance op-amp plus an RC charge-bucket filter solves this.

Should I use a SAR or a delta-sigma ADC?

SAR suits medium-to-high resolution with fast, latency-free conversions and multiplexed inputs; delta-sigma suits very high resolution at low bandwidth, such as precision sensor and audio measurement.
