What is 3D packaging?

Stacking chips vertically and connecting them with TSVs or hybrid bonding. Shorter connections improve speed and power, but heat removal becomes a challenge.

Field: Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets)

How stacked dies are connected

3D packaging stacks dies vertically and connects them through the stack instead of through package or board wiring. Through-silicon vias (TSVs) are vertical copper conductors through thinned silicon that carry signals and power from one die to the next, usually joined by microbumps. Hybrid bonding joins two dies or wafers directly, bonding copper pads to copper and the surrounding dielectric to dielectric without bumps, which allows much finer connection pitches. Dies can be bonded wafer-to-wafer, die-to-wafer or die-to-die.

Why stacking helps speed and power

Stacking puts dies only tens of micrometers apart instead of millimeters apart side by side. Shorter interconnects have lower capacitance, so signal delay and the energy spent charging wires fall. Connections can be spread across the entire die face rather than lined up at the edges, giving far more parallel links and high bandwidth. The footprint shrinks as well. Familiar examples include stacked DRAM such as HBM (high bandwidth memory), image sensors with the pixel array bonded onto a logic die, and extra cache memory stacked on a processor.

2.5D vs. 3D

2.5D packaging places dies side by side on an intermediate substrate, typically a silicon interposer with fine wiring, and connects them laterally. 3D packaging stacks dies on top of each other. 2.5D is easier to cool because every die can contact the heat spreader, while 3D gives the shortest connections. The two are often combined: HBM is itself a 3D stack of DRAM dies on a base die, and several HBM stacks are placed beside a GPU or accelerator on an interposer in a 2.5D arrangement.

Heat, yield and test challenges

Stacking concentrates power in a small footprint, and heat from inner dies must pass through bonding layers and thinned silicon to reach the heat sink, so temperatures rise and performance and reliability suffer. Thermal floorplanning, such as putting the hottest die next to the heat sink, is essential. Yield compounds as well: one bad die can scrap a whole stack, so dies are tested before stacking to select known-good dies, which favors die-to-wafer bonding. TSVs also create mechanical stress, so nearby transistors keep out of a surrounding zone.

Answer: C. Temperature rise from heat generated by stacked chips, and removing that heat

3D stacking shortens wiring, but stacking heat-generating dies traps heat and makes it hard to remove from inner dies. Heat per unit area rises with the number of layers, and heat must pass through multiple bonding layers and thin silicon to escape. Higher temperature degrades performance and reliability, so thermal design, such as placing the hottest die next to the heat sink, is critical. Wiring actually gets shorter.

Answer: A. 2.5D places chips side by side on an intermediate substrate (such as an interposer), while 3D stacks chips vertically

2.5D packaging places multiple dies side by side on an intermediary substrate such as a silicon interposer and connects them through its fine wiring. 3D packaging stacks dies vertically, connecting them with TSVs or hybrid bonding. 2.5D dissipates heat more easily, while 3D gives the shortest wiring. Combined structures are also used, such as 3D-stacked HBM placed beside a GPU in a 2.5D arrangement.

FAQ

What is the difference between 2.5D and 3D packaging?

2.5D packaging places dies side by side on an interposer and connects them laterally, while 3D packaging stacks dies vertically and connects them with TSVs or hybrid bonding.

What is the main challenge of 3D chip stacking?

Heat removal is the most prominent challenge, because heat from inner dies must flow through other dies and bonding layers; yield loss from a single bad die and test access are also major issues.

What is a TSV?

A TSV (through-silicon via) is a vertical electrical connection, usually copper, that passes through a thinned silicon die so that signals and power can travel directly to a die stacked above or below.
