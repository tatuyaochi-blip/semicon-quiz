What is Reactive sputtering?

Sputtering a metal target in an atmosphere containing a reactive gas such as nitrogen or oxygen to form nitride or oxide films. It is used for films such as titanium nitride (TiN).

Japanese name: 反応性スパッタリング

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

How the compound forms

A metal target, such as titanium, is sputtered in argon mixed with a reactive gas such as nitrogen or oxygen. Metal atoms leaving the target combine with the reactive species, mainly on the growing film surface but also partly on the target itself, producing nitride or oxide films such as TiN, TaN or AlN. Film stoichiometry, resistivity and stress are tuned through the reactive gas flow or partial pressure. The method avoids compound targets, which are often brittle, hard to manufacture and poorly conducting, so a robust metal target can run on simple DC power.

Target poisoning and hysteresis

As reactive gas flow rises, the target surface itself becomes covered with compound. Compounds have a much lower sputter yield than metals, so the deposition rate suddenly drops to a fraction of its metallic value; this is target poisoning. Reducing the gas does not immediately reverse it, because the compound layer must be sputtered away first, so rate and partial pressure versus flow trace a hysteresis loop. The highest-rate stoichiometric films lie in the unstable transition region, which is held with feedback that monitors plasma optical emission or gas partial pressure and adjusts the flow.

Reactive sputtering vs. sputtering a compound target

Sputtering a ceramic compound target directly gives composition close to the target, but insulating targets need RF power, deposition is slow and targets can crack. Reactive sputtering trades this for process-control complexity: composition depends on gas flow, pumping speed and power, and must be managed against poisoning. For insulating products such as oxides, compound buildup on target areas that are not fully cleaned can charge up and cause arcing, so pulsed DC or mid-frequency AC power supplies are often used to discharge the surface periodically.

Where you will meet it

Titanium nitride is the classic example: reactively sputtered TiN serves as a diffusion barrier and adhesion layer, a metal hard mask for etching, and a work-function layer in high-k metal gates, where its composition shifts the effective work function. TaN barriers under copper are made the same way. Outside logic, reactively sputtered aluminum nitride is used as a piezoelectric film in acoustic-wave RF filters, and a variety of oxide films are produced for optical and dielectric coatings.

FAQ

What is target poisoning in reactive sputtering?

It is the coating of the target surface with nitride or oxide when reactive gas flow is too high. Because compounds sputter much more slowly than metal, the deposition rate drops sharply.

Why does reactive sputtering show hysteresis?

Once the target is poisoned, the compound layer must be sputtered off before the target returns to metallic mode, so the transition happens at different gas flows when increasing and decreasing the flow.

How is TiN deposited by sputtering?

A titanium target is sputtered in an argon and nitrogen mixture, and the titanium reacts with nitrogen to form TiN on the wafer. The nitrogen flow sets the film's composition and properties.
