What is Yield?

The proportion of good products to the total produced. A key factor in semiconductor manufacturing cost.

Japanese name: 歩留まり

Yield appears in 56 practice questions across 21 topics, including Yield and quality control, Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets), Testing and inspection.

Field: Yield and quality control

How yield is broken down

Semiconductor yield is usually tracked as a chain of stages. Line yield is the fraction of wafers that survive the fab without being scrapped. Die yield, measured at wafer probe test, is the fraction of dies on a wafer that work electrically. Assembly and final-test yields cover packaging and the last screening. Overall yield is roughly the product of these numbers, so a few percent lost at each stage compounds. Because the cost of processing a wafer is nearly fixed, the number of good dies per wafer directly sets the cost of each sellable chip.

Why big dies yield worse

Random defects such as particles land on the wafer more or less independently of the circuit layout. A simple Poisson model gives die yield Y = exp(−A·D0), where A is die area and D0 is defect density. With D0 = 0.1 cm⁻², a 1 cm² die yields about 90 percent, but a 4 cm² die only about 67 percent. This is why large SoCs are expensive and why splitting a design into smaller dies that are tested before packaging is attractive. Real fabs use refined models that account for defect clustering, but the trend is the same.

What kills yield

In scaled chips a particle only tens of nanometres across, comparable to wire spacing, can short or open a circuit, and trace metal or organic contamination raises leakage or shifts threshold voltage. Process non-uniformity causes losses that cluster in regions, often at the wafer edge: uneven etch rate, CMP thickness that varies with pattern density, or slip dislocations from thermal stress. Engineers separate random defects from systematic ones, such as layout-sensitive patterns, because the cures differ: cleaner processing for the first, design and process tuning for the second.

How yield is improved

Fabs clean wafers before and after many steps, monitor defect counts with inspection tools, and record every wafer's laser-scribed ID so that failures can be traced to a specific tool or step. Wafer maps that combine wafer ID with die location reveal spatial signatures pointing to the cause. Design for manufacturability (DFM) helps too, for example dummy fill that evens out pattern density for CMP. Memories add redundancy: DRAMs carry spare rows and columns, and faulty addresses are rerouted to them through fuses or antifuses, even after packaging with post-package repair.

Which correctly describes redundancy repair, used to improve DRAM yield?

  1. ARows or columns containing defective cells are replaced with spare rows or columns prepared in advance
  2. BAll chips containing defective cells are discarded
  3. CThe supply voltage is raised to make defective cells work
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Rows or columns containing defective cells are replaced with spare rows or columns prepared in advance

Building billions of cells with no defects is rare, so DRAMs include spare rows and columns. Addresses of faulty locations found at wafer test are recorded in laser-cut fuses or electrically programmed antifuses, and accesses are redirected to spares; post-package repair (PPR) allows fixes even after packaging. Without this redundancy repair, high-capacity DRAM yields would be untenable.

What does "sputter yield" mean in sputtering?

  1. AThe time required for deposition
  2. BThe fraction of good chips per wafer
  3. CThe number of atoms ejected per ion striking the target
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The number of atoms ejected per ion striking the target

Sputter yield is the number of atoms ejected per incident ion, roughly 0.5–3 for argon ions at a few hundred eV. It increases with ion energy, with ion mass (especially as it approaches the target atom's mass) and at oblique incidence. Because it varies widely by material, metals deposit at different rates under the same conditions, and it also governs physical removal rates in etching.

FAQ

How do you calculate die yield from defect density?

The simplest estimate is the Poisson model Y = exp(−A·D0), with A the die area and D0 the defect density per unit area. More realistic models, such as negative-binomial ones, account for defects that cluster and predict somewhat higher yield for large dies.

Is sputter yield the same as manufacturing yield?

No. Sputter yield is a physics term for the number of target atoms ejected per incident ion during sputtering, typically about 0.5 to 3 for argon ions at a few hundred eV, and has nothing to do with the fraction of good chips.

Why does yield matter so much for chip cost?

Processing a wafer costs roughly the same whether its dies work or not, so the cost of each good chip is the wafer cost divided by the number of good dies. Lower yield therefore raises unit cost directly.
