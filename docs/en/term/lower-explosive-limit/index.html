What is Lower explosive limit (LEL)?

The lowest concentration in air at which a flammable gas can burn or explode. Gas detectors are often set to alarm at a percentage of this level.

Japanese name: 爆発下限界(LEL)

The flammable range

A flammable gas mixed with air can only sustain a flame between two concentrations. Below the lower explosive limit (LEL, also called the lower flammable limit) there is too little fuel; above the upper explosive limit (UEL) there is too little oxygen. Hydrogen, for example, burns from about 4% to about 75% by volume in air, and methane from about 5% to 15%. Silane has an LEL of about 1.4%, but it is also pyrophoric and can ignite on its own when it leaks into air, so its LEL understates the hazard.

%LEL readings and alarm setpoints

Combustible gas detectors, typically catalytic-bead or infrared types, report concentration as a percentage of the LEL rather than as volume percent. A reading of 100% LEL means the mixture has reached the LEL itself; for hydrogen, 10% LEL corresponds to about 0.4% by volume. Alarms are set well below the danger point, commonly at 10–25% LEL, often in two stages where the first warns and the second shuts off the gas supply or increases ventilation. A detector calibrated for one gas reads differently for another, so the calibration gas must match the hazard.

LEL vs. exposure limits and flash point

The LEL is about combustion, not health. Occupational exposure limits such as the 8-hour time-weighted average, the short-term exposure limit (STEL, usually over 15 minutes) and ceiling values address toxicity, and for gases like arsine they lie in the parts-per-billion range, far below any flammability concern. Toxic-gas monitors therefore read in ppm or ppb, not %LEL. For liquids, the flash point is the lowest temperature at which the vapor above the liquid reaches roughly the LEL and can be ignited.

Where you will meet it in a fab

Hydrogen, silane and other flammable process gases are stored in exhausted gas cabinets and piped through enclosed lines, with detectors at cabinets, valve boxes and tool gas panels. Ventilation and exhaust are designed so that a credible leak is diluted to a small fraction of the LEL, and detector alarms are interlocked with automatic shutoff valves. Solvent vapors from wet benches and flammable exhaust streams entering abatement systems are also managed against their LEL.

Answer: B. The lowest concentration in air at which the gas can burn or explode

Flammable gases burn or explode only within a concentration range in air, the flammable range. Its lower bound is the lower explosive limit (LEL), for example about 4% for hydrogen and about 1.4% for silane. Combustible gas detectors read concentration as a percentage of LEL (%LEL) and alarm at a safely low level, typically 10–25% of the LEL. It is unrelated to flame temperature or cylinder pressure.

FAQ

What does 10% LEL mean on a gas detector?

It means the gas concentration is one tenth of the lower explosive limit, so the mixture is still far too lean to burn. For hydrogen, whose LEL is about 4% by volume, 10% LEL is about 0.4% hydrogen in air.

What is the difference between LEL and UEL?

The LEL is the minimum fuel concentration in air that can burn, and the UEL is the maximum; between them lies the flammable range. Outside that range the mixture is too lean or too rich to sustain a flame, although a rich mixture becomes flammable again as it is diluted with air.

Is LEL the same as an exposure limit?

No. The LEL describes when a gas can ignite, while exposure limits such as TWA and STEL describe concentrations that are safe to breathe, which for toxic gases are often thousands of times lower or more.
