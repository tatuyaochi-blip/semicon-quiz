What is Backside power delivery?

Placing the power wiring on the back of the wafer so the front-side wiring can be used for signals. It reduces voltage drop and routing congestion.

Japanese name: 裏面電源供給

Chip terms in the news · In the news: April 2024 – January 2026

In a nutshell

Backside power delivery (BSPDN) moves the power wiring that feeds the transistors to the back of the wafer. It frees the front-side wiring for signals and reduces voltage drop, and it is starting to be adopted at generations around 2 nm and beyond.

Why it is in the news

  • April 2024 TSMC announced A16, which uses its "Super Power Rail" backside power delivery, and said it plans to start volume production in the second half of 2026.
  • October 2025 Intel announced that it is manufacturing Intel 18A processors, which combine RibbonFET with PowerVia backside power delivery, at Fab 52, its fab in Arizona, USA.
  • January 2026 At CES 2026, Intel announced Core Ultra Series 3 (Panther Lake), made on Intel 18A.

Until now, chips have stacked all their power and signal wiring on the front side. As scaling made the wires thinner and more crowded, voltage drop in the power supply became a serious problem, so major manufacturers announced they would adopt backside power delivery in their next generations, drawing attention to the technology.

Why move power to the back

In a conventional chip, both signal and power wiring sit above the transistors in a back-end-of-line (BEOL) stack of about 15 levels. Power enters at the top through thick wires and then descends through more than ten levels of increasingly fine wires and vias to reach each transistor, losing voltage along the way (IR drop). Power rails also occupy routing tracks inside every standard cell, crowding the signal wiring. Backside power delivery separates the two networks: signals stay on the front, while power is delivered from the back of the wafer.

How it is built

The front side, transistors and signal wiring, is processed first. The wafer is then bonded face-down to a carrier wafer and the original silicon is thinned from the back to hundreds of nanometers or a few micrometers. Very small vias are etched from the back to reach buried power rails or the transistors' source and drain contacts directly, and thick, low-resistance power wiring is built on the back. Announced implementations include Intel's PowerVia and TSMC's A16 process, among others.

Benefits and trade-offs

Feeding transistors from directly below shortens the power path and lowers IR drop, freeing front-side tracks for signals, which eases routing congestion and allows smaller standard cells. The costs are extra process complexity: wafer bonding and thinning, alignment of backside features to front-side devices despite distortion from bonding, and new approaches to failure analysis. Heat removal also changes, because the active layer ends up sandwiched between bonding and wiring layers, so thermal design must be reconsidered.

Why it matters for scaling now

As transistors shrink, performance is increasingly limited by parasitics rather than the channel: source, drain and contact resistance can approach half of total on-resistance, and wiring resistance rises as pitches shrink into the 20 nm range. Gains therefore come from extrinsic improvements such as better contact materials, low-k or air-gap spacers and backside power delivery. It is often paired with gate-all-around transistors and is considered especially important for stacked CFET devices, whose lower tier is hard to reach from above.

How it differs from existing technology

With conventional front-side power delivery, both power and signals run through the multilayer wiring above the transistors. With backside power delivery, the wafer is thinned and thick power lines are built on the back, bringing power directly close to the transistors. The shorter, wider power path reduces IR drop and frees the front-side wiring for signals. On the other hand, it adds process steps such as wafer bonding and thinning, and it changes how heat is removed.

FAQ

What is a backside power delivery network (BSPDN)?

It is a chip wiring scheme that places the power distribution network on the back of a thinned wafer and connects it to transistors from below, leaving the front-side metal levels for signals.

What is the difference between backside power delivery and a buried power rail?

A buried power rail embeds the cell's power lines below the transistors instead of in the first metal level. Backside power delivery moves the whole power network to the back of the wafer and can feed buried rails or transistor contacts directly through backside vias.

Why does backside power delivery reduce IR drop?

Power no longer has to descend through ten or more thin front-side metal levels and vias; it arrives through short, thick backside wires, so the resistance between the supply and each transistor is much lower.
