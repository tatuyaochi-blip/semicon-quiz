What is Diffusion capacitance?

Capacitance in a forward-biased PN junction caused by injected minority carriers stored in the neutral regions. It grows in proportion to forward current and limits high-speed operation.

Japanese name: 拡散容量

Diffusion capacitance appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including PN junctions and diodes.

Field: PN junctions and diodes

How diffusion capacitance arises

In a forward-biased PN junction, electrons are injected into the p side and holes into the n side as minority carriers, and they are stored in the neutral regions outside the depletion layer while they diffuse and until they recombine. A slight increase in forward voltage raises the injection exponentially, and the stored charge grows with it. Because the charge rises and falls with the voltage, it acts as a capacitance. This is the diffusion capacitance, a component separate from the junction (depletion) capacitance due to the fixed charge in the depletion layer. The stored charge is the current multiplied by the minority-carrier lifetime (or the transit time in short regions), Q ≈ τI.

Estimating its size

Differentiating the stored charge Q ≈ τI with respect to voltage gives a diffusion capacitance of Cd ≈ τI/(nkT/q), where kT/q is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at room temperature, and n is the ideality factor, 1 for an ideal diode. The capacitance is roughly proportional to current and larger for longer lifetimes. For example, with n = 1, τ = 10 ns and I = 10 mA, Cd ≈ 10 ns × 10 mA ÷ 26 mV ≈ 3.8 nF. Since the junction capacitance of a small diode is generally on the order of picofarads, the diffusion capacitance dominates the total when a large forward current flows.

Versus junction (depletion) capacitance

Junction capacitance comes from the fixed charge of ionized donors and acceptors that rises and falls as the depletion-layer width changes, and it dominates under reverse bias. Under reverse bias there is almost no minority-carrier injection, so diffusion capacitance is negligible. Under forward bias both exist in parallel, but as current increases, the diffusion capacitance grows rapidly and overtakes the junction capacitance. This is why varactor diodes use the reverse-biased junction capacitance. Note also that the stored carriers cannot follow changes faster than the minority-carrier lifetime, so the diffusion capacitance falls at high frequency and does not behave like an ideal capacitor.

Effect on high-speed operation

When a diode is switched from on to off, the charge stored in the diffusion capacitance keeps a reverse current flowing until it is extracted or recombines. This is reverse recovery, a source of loss and noise in switching power supplies and similar circuits. Remedies include fast-recovery diodes with shortened lifetimes and Schottky barrier diodes, which store almost no minority carriers. In a bipolar transistor, the charge stored in the base acts as a base-emitter diffusion capacitance and, together with the base transit time, limits the transition frequency fT. Thinning the base to shorten the transit time is the basic route to higher speed.

Answer: A. Diffusion capacitance

Under forward bias, minority carriers are injected and stored on both sides of the junction, and because their amount changes with voltage they behave as a capacitance—the diffusion capacitance. It grows roughly in proportion to current (Cd ≈ τI/(nkT/q)) and can reach several nF at high current. Under reverse bias, the depletion-layer junction capacitance dominates instead. Diffusion capacitance limits the speed of diodes and bipolar transistors.

FAQ

What is the difference between diffusion capacitance and junction capacitance?

Junction capacitance comes from the fixed charge in the depletion layer and dominates under reverse bias. Diffusion capacitance comes from minority carriers stored in the neutral regions under forward bias; it grows roughly in proportion to current and becomes much larger than the junction capacitance at high forward current.

How is diffusion capacitance calculated?

With minority-carrier lifetime τ and forward current I, it can be estimated as Cd ≈ τI/(nkT/q), where kT/q is about 26 mV at room temperature and n is the ideality factor. It is the derivative of the stored charge Q ≈ τI with respect to voltage, so it grows in proportion to current.

Is there diffusion capacitance under reverse bias?

Hardly any. Under reverse bias there is almost no minority-carrier injection and very little stored charge, so the capacitance is set almost entirely by the junction capacitance. Diffusion capacitance matters when forward current is flowing.
