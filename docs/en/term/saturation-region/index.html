What is Saturation region?

The state of a bipolar transistor in which both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions are forward-biased. The collector-emitter voltage falls to about 0.1-0.3 V, corresponding to a switch in the on state.

Japanese name: 飽和領域

Field: Bipolar transistors

Junction conditions in bipolar saturation

A bipolar transistor has four operating regions defined by its two junctions. In the active region the base-emitter junction is forward-biased and the base-collector junction reverse-biased, and IC ≈ hFE × IB. If the base current is raised until the collector circuit cannot supply hFE × IB, for example when VCC/RC is smaller, the collector voltage falls until the base-collector junction also becomes forward-biased. That is saturation. Collector current is now set by the external circuit and barely increases with more base current, and the ratio IC/IB, called the forced beta, falls below hFE.

Saturation as the on state of a switch

Because the two forward-biased junctions largely cancel, the collector-emitter voltage VCE(sat) is only about 0.1–0.3 V, close to a closed switch, and the conduction loss is about VCE(sat) × IC. Designers guarantee saturation by driving base current several times IC/hFE(min), often 2–10 times. The cost appears at turn-off: in saturation excess minority charge is stored in the base and collector, and it must be removed before the transistor can leave saturation, delaying switching. Schottky clamps or avoiding deep saturation are classic ways to speed this up.

MOSFET saturation means something else

The same word describes a very different condition in a MOSFET. A long-channel nMOSFET with VGS > Vth enters saturation when VDS ≥ VGS − Vth: the channel pinches off at the drain end, and the drain current becomes nearly independent of VDS, following ID = (1/2)μCox(W/L)(VGS − Vth)². Here the current is saturated, not the voltage. In short-channel devices velocity saturation makes current saturate at lower VDS and pushes the dependence on overdrive toward linear, so detailed compact models are used for design.

Mapping the regions between devices

For amplifiers, the useful operating regions correspond across the two devices: MOSFET saturation plays the role of the bipolar active region, where the output current is controlled by the input and is relatively insensitive to output voltage. For switches, bipolar saturation corresponds to the MOSFET triode (linear) region, where the device behaves like a low resistance. Mixing these up is a common source of confusion. An amplifier must keep a bipolar transistor out of saturation, but must keep a MOSFET in saturation, with VDS above VGS − Vth setting the minimum output swing.

IC/IB is then below hFE; this ratio, the "forced beta," indicates how deep the saturation is. It is the on state of a switch with VCE only about 0.1–0.2 V, but stored charge delays turn-off.

FAQ

What is VCE(sat) of a bipolar transistor?

VCE(sat) is the collector-emitter voltage when the transistor is saturated, typically about 0.1–0.3 V for small silicon devices, rising at high current because of collector resistance. It sets the on-state voltage drop and conduction loss of a BJT switch.

Is the saturation region of a BJT the same as that of a MOSFET?

No. BJT saturation has both junctions forward-biased and acts like a closed switch, whereas MOSFET saturation is the pinched-off region with nearly constant drain current used for amplification. The MOSFET region analogous to BJT saturation is the triode or linear region.

Why is a bipolar transistor slow to turn off from saturation?

In saturation, excess minority carriers are stored in the base and collector regions. This stored charge must be removed before the collector current can fall, which adds a storage delay to turn-off.
