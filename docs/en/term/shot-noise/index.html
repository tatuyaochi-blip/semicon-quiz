What is Shot noise?

Noise from statistical fluctuation in the number of photons or electrons. Its size is proportional to √N for a signal of N electrons, so it is relatively larger when light is scarce.

Japanese name: ショットノイズ

Shot noise appears in 5 practice questions across 2 topics, including Image sensors and optical devices, Lithography.

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Where shot noise comes from

Light and current are made of discrete particles that arrive at random times. When events are independent, their count follows Poisson statistics: the variance equals the mean, so a signal averaging N photoelectrons fluctuates by about the square root of N. In electronic circuits the same effect appears when carriers cross a barrier independently, as in a diode junction or photodiode, giving a noise current spectral density of 2qI (the Schottky formula). It differs from thermal noise in a resistor, which arises from random thermal motion and exists even with no current.

Signal-to-noise ratio in images

Because noise grows as the square root of N, the shot-noise-limited signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is N divided by its square root, which is again the square root of N: 10 for 100 electrons and 100 for 10,000. Dark scenes collect few electrons, so photon noise and read noise are large relative to the signal and images look grainy. Raising ISO mainly amplifies signal and noise together and cannot reduce shot noise. Full-well capacity caps the best possible SNR of a single pixel exposure.

Measuring sensors with photon transfer

Since photon shot noise has variance equal to its mean in electrons, it serves as a built-in calibration source. In the photon transfer curve (PTC) method, flat-field images are captured at several light levels and the variance of the output in digital numbers is plotted against its mean. The slope gives the conversion gain between electrons and digital numbers, the low-signal floor gives read noise, and the point where variance stops rising marks full-well capacity, from which dynamic range follows.

Living with it: binning, stacking and EUV

Shot noise is a property of the light itself, so better circuits cannot remove it; only collecting more photons helps. Larger pixels, brighter lenses, multi-frame stacking in night modes and pixel binning all do this. Binning a 2×2 block quadruples the signal and roughly doubles SNR when shot noise dominates. Photon shot noise also limits extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography: each 13.5 nm photon carries about 92 eV, roughly fourteen times the energy of a 193 nm photon, so the same dose delivers far fewer photons and causes random line edge roughness and stochastic defects.

Answer: B. Proportional to √N

Photons arrive randomly, so a signal averaging N electrons fluctuates by about √N (Poisson statistics). This shot noise gives S/N = N/√N = √N, improving with more light: 10 for 100 electrons, 100 for 10,000. Being inherent to light itself, it cannot be eliminated by better circuits; gathering more light with larger pixels or lenses is the fundamental remedy.

FAQ

Why is shot noise proportional to the square root of the signal?

Randomly arriving photons or electrons follow Poisson statistics, where the variance equals the mean. The standard deviation, which is the noise, is therefore the square root of the mean count.

What is the difference between shot noise and thermal noise?

Shot noise comes from the discreteness of charge or photons and scales with the current or signal. Thermal noise comes from random thermal motion of carriers in a resistance and is present even when no current flows.

Does increasing ISO reduce noise in low light?

Not meaningfully. Higher ISO amplifies the signal and the shot noise equally. Only gathering more light, through longer exposure, a larger aperture or pixel, or averaging frames, improves the signal-to-noise ratio.
