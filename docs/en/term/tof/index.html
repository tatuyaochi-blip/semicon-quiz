What is ToF (time of flight)?

Measuring distance from the time light takes to travel to an object and back. ToF sensors are used in face recognition, robotics and automotive LiDAR.

Japanese name: ToF(Time of Flight)

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

The basic relation and its timing demands

Distance equals the speed of light times the round-trip time divided by two, because the light travels out and back. A target 1 m away returns light after about 6.7 ns, and a LiDAR echo arriving after 400 ns means about 60 m; forgetting the factor of two gives 120 m. The challenge is timing precision: a 1 cm change in distance shifts the round trip by only about 67 ps. Sensors therefore either time individual pulses with very fast detectors and circuits or measure the phase of modulated light.

Direct ToF

A direct ToF (dToF) sensor fires short laser pulses and timestamps the returning photons, typically with single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) and time-to-digital converters. Because a single pulse may return only a few photons among ambient-light counts, the measurement is repeated many times and the arrival times are collected in a histogram whose peak marks the target distance. This approach suits long ranges and is common in automotive and robotic LiDAR, where a scanning or flash laser builds a 3D point cloud.

Indirect ToF

An indirect ToF (iToF) sensor illuminates the scene with light modulated at tens of megahertz and measures the phase shift of the returning signal. Each pixel steers photo-generated charge into two or more storage nodes, or taps, during different phase windows, and the charge ratios give the phase. Distance follows from the phase and modulation frequency, but it wraps around: at 20 MHz the unambiguous range is 7.5 m, so several frequencies are often combined. iToF gives dense depth maps at short range, while multipath reflections and strong sunlight are its main error sources.

Wavelength and silicon limits

ToF systems use near-infrared light around 850 nm or 940 nm so the illumination is invisible; 940 nm also falls in a dip of the sunlight spectrum caused by atmospheric water absorption. Silicon absorbs these wavelengths weakly, with absorption depths of about 20 µm at 850 nm and over 50 µm at 940 nm, so thin photodiodes lose much of the light. Pixels add light-trapping surface textures and deeper collection regions. Eye-safety limits on laser power and scattering in fog or heavy rain further limit range.

Answer: A. Distance is calculated from the time it takes emitted light to reflect off an object and return

A ToF sensor measures the round-trip time of light reflected from an object and computes distance = speed of light × time ÷ 2. A 1 m distance takes only about 6.7 ns round trip, so there are direct ToF (dToF, timing pulses with SPADs) and indirect ToF (iToF, measuring the phase shift of modulated light). They capture 3D shape for face authentication, AR, robots and automotive LiDAR.

FAQ

What is the difference between direct ToF and indirect ToF?

Direct ToF times short light pulses with fast detectors such as SPADs, which suits long range. Indirect ToF measures the phase shift of continuously modulated light, giving dense, high-resolution depth at shorter range.

How do you calculate distance from time of flight?

Multiply the speed of light by the measured round-trip time and divide by two. For example, 400 ns gives 3×10⁸ m/s × 400 ns ÷ 2 = 60 m.

Why do ToF sensors use 940 nm light?

Near-infrared light is invisible, and around 940 nm sunlight is weakened by atmospheric water absorption, which reduces ambient-light interference outdoors. The drawback is that silicon absorbs 940 nm light weakly.
