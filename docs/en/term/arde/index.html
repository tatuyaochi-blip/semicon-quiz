What is ARDE (aspect-ratio-dependent etching)?

The slowdown of etch rate in narrower, deeper features because ions and radicals reach the bottom less easily. Also called the microloading effect.

Japanese name: ARDE(アスペクト比依存エッチング)

ARDE (aspect-ratio-dependent etching) appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Etching.

Field: Etching

What happens in ARDE

ARDE (aspect-ratio-dependent etching) is the phenomenon in which the etch rate falls as the aspect ratio, the depth of a hole or trench divided by its width, increases. Even on the same wafer etched for the same time, narrow features end up shallow and wide features deep, so depth is not uniform across feature sizes. The rate also keeps falling as a hole gets deeper during the etch, so deeper holes take disproportionately longer to etch. It is also called the microloading effect, although some usage distinguishes the two, reserving "microloading" for local etch-rate differences caused by pattern density and "ARDE" for those caused by aspect ratio.

Cause: ions and radicals struggle to reach the bottom

Etching proceeds as ions and radicals (highly reactive neutral species) react at the bottom of the feature and the by-products leave it. In a narrow, deep hole, radicals travelling in all directions collide with the sidewalls and are lost, so fewer reach the bottom. Ions, too, hit the sidewalls because of their slight angular spread, or are deflected by charging of the sidewalls and bottom, and reach the bottom less easily. In addition, by-products have difficulty escaping and may redeposit at the bottom. The thickness of the protective film on the sidewalls also varies with aspect ratio, and together these effects lower the etch rate as the aspect ratio rises.

Where it causes problems in manufacturing

ARDE becomes a major problem in high-aspect-ratio etching. In 3D NAND memory holes, DRAM capacitor holes, TSVs and deep MEMS trenches, the rate drops as the feature deepens, so the etch may stall before reaching the bottom or the profile may taper. Also, when contact holes or trenches of different widths share a layer, timing the etch for the wide features leaves the narrow ones unopened, while timing it for the narrow ones over-etches the underlayer beneath the wide ones. This tightens the design of the over-etch amount and of selectivity to the underlayer.

Mitigation and inverse ARDE

Countermeasures include tuning the process conditions: lowering the pressure to make ion directions more uniform so more ions reach the bottom, adjusting ion energy, suppressing charging with pulsed plasma, and adjusting the gas chemistry to control how the sidewall passivation film deposits. Another approach places a highly selective etch-stop layer underneath so that the etch halts there even when wide features arrive first. Under some conditions deposition is weaker in narrower features, so they etch faster, producing inverse ARDE; process engineers look for conditions in which the balance between etching and deposition cancels the two effects.

Answer: B. Aspect-ratio-dependent etching (ARDE, microloading)

At the bottom of narrow, deep features, radicals are lost to wall collisions, ions are deflected by charging, and reaction products escape slowly, so etch rate falls as aspect ratio rises. This aspect-ratio-dependent etching (ARDE, or microloading) leaves narrow holes shallower than wide ones on the same wafer, so depths vary with size. Etch-stop layers and process tuning compensate.

FAQ

Are ARDE and the microloading effect the same thing?

They are often used interchangeably, but sometimes they are distinguished. When they are, ARDE (also called RIE lag) refers to the rate dropping with increasing aspect ratio, while microloading refers to a local rate drop where dense patterns consume the reactive species. A rate change driven by the open area of the whole wafer is called the loading effect.

Why do narrower holes etch more slowly?

In a narrow, deep hole, radicals are lost by hitting the sidewalls, and ions also hit the sidewalls or are deflected by charging, so fewer reach the bottom. By-products also have difficulty escaping. As a result, the etch rate at the bottom falls as the aspect ratio rises.

How is ARDE suppressed?

The basics are process tuning: lowering the pressure to align ion directions, adjusting the gas chemistry to change how sidewall passivation deposits, and suppressing charging with pulsed plasma. Because eliminating ARDE completely is difficult, this is combined with highly selective etch-stop layers so that depth variation is absorbed at the underlayer.
