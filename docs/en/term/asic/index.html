What is ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit)?

An IC designed specifically for one application or customer. It beats general-purpose chips on performance and power, but high development costs suit it to high-volume uses.

Japanese name: ASIC(特定用途向けIC)

The economics of an ASIC

An application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) carries large one-time costs, called non-recurring engineering (NRE): architecture and logic design, verification, licensed IP blocks, physical design, and a mask set that can cost millions to tens of millions of dollars at advanced nodes, plus months of fabrication lead time. In return, the chip contains only the needed functions, so each unit is small, fast, power-efficient and cheap to make. ASICs therefore pay off when volume is high enough to spread the NRE, or when no off-the-shelf part meets the performance or power target.

How an ASIC is designed

Most digital ASICs follow a standard-cell flow. Engineers write register-transfer level (RTL) code in Verilog or VHDL, verify it by simulation and, for large designs, emulation or FPGA prototyping, then synthesize it into gates from the foundry's cell library, with memories mapped to memory macros. Place-and-route builds the physical layout, static timing analysis and physical verification (DRC and LVS) confirm it, and the final layout is taped out to make masks. Full-custom design is used for the most performance-critical analog or memory blocks.

ASIC vs. FPGA vs. ASSP

An FPGA implements logic in reprogrammable fabric, with no masks or fab wait, so it suits prototypes, low volumes and changing requirements; the same function as an ASIC is faster, smaller and lower power. An application-specific standard product (ASSP) is also dedicated to one application, but is sold as a catalog part to many customers, whereas an ASIC is typically built for one product or company. General-purpose CPUs and GPUs trade efficiency for flexibility through software.

Where you will meet it

ASICs appear wherever volume or efficiency justifies custom silicon: networking switches, storage controllers, consumer electronics, automotive systems, cryptocurrency mining and AI accelerators. Many are mixed-signal. A MEMS microphone, for example, pairs its capacitive sensing element with a small readout ASIC in the same package that converts the tiny capacitance change into an electrical signal. In these sensor uses, the ASIC is often designed jointly with the sensor itself.

Answer: C. Detecting the change in capacitance between a membrane vibrated by sound pressure and a fixed electrode

A typical MEMS microphone forms a capacitor from a diaphragm about 1 µm thick that vibrates with sound pressure and a perforated fixed backplate; an ASIC in the same package converts the capacitance change into an electrical signal. Smaller than electret condenser microphones, tolerant of solder reflow and highly uniform, they are used in multiples in smartphones for features such as noise cancellation.

Which correctly describes a feature of FPGAs?

  1. AThe circuit is fixed at manufacture and cannot be changed
  2. BThey are devices dedicated to analog circuits
  3. CUsers can rewrite the configuration of the logic circuit after manufacturing
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Users can rewrite the configuration of the logic circuit after manufacturing

An FPGA consists of logic blocks with small lookup tables (LUTs) and programmable interconnect whose configuration can be rewritten after manufacture. With no fabrication lead time or mask costs, unlike an ASIC, it suits prototypes, low volumes and equipment needing updates. Its reconfiguration circuitry adds overhead, so the same function in an ASIC is faster, lower power and smaller. It is not an analog-only device.

FAQ

What does ASIC stand for?

ASIC stands for application-specific integrated circuit, a chip designed for one particular product or use rather than as a general-purpose part.

What is the difference between an ASIC and an FPGA?

An ASIC's logic is fixed in silicon at manufacture, giving the best speed, power and unit cost but high up-front cost and long lead time. An FPGA can be reconfigured after manufacture with no mask cost, but is slower, larger and more power-hungry for the same function.

What is the difference between an ASIC and an ASSP?

Both target a specific application, but an ASIC is usually designed for a single customer or product, while an ASSP is a standard product a chip vendor sells to many customers.
