What is BGA?

Ball grid array. A high-pin-count package with solder balls arranged in a grid on its underside.

Japanese name: BGA

BGA appears in 11 practice questions across 4 topics, including Packaging and assembly, Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets), Metrology and analysis.

Field: Packaging and assembly

How a BGA is built

A ball grid array places the die on a substrate, usually a multilayer organic laminate, connected by wire bonds or flip-chip bumps, then covers it with mold compound or a lid. The substrate's wiring fans signals out to an array of solder balls across the whole underside, typically at a pitch of about 0.4–1 mm. Using the full area rather than just the perimeter gives far more terminals than a QFP of the same size, with shorter, lower-inductance paths. During reflow, surface tension of the molten balls helps pull the package into alignment with its board pads.

Power, ground and routing

In high-pin-count BGAs, many balls carry power and ground rather than signals, and more than half is not unusual. High-performance chips can draw over 100 A, and spreading that current across many parallel balls lowers resistance and inductance, limiting IR drop and supply noise. Balls under the die can also conduct heat into the board. The dense array has a cost on the board side: escaping signals from inner rows requires multilayer printed circuit boards with vias between pads, and fine-pitch arrays may need finer board features.

Inspection and reliability

Because the joints are hidden under the package, they cannot be inspected visually. X-ray imaging reveals bridges, missing balls and voids, and X-ray CT reconstructs a three-dimensional view that separates overlapping structures. Reliability concerns differ by use. Temperature cycling fatigues joints because package and board expand at different rates. In phones, drop shock can crack joints or the brittle intermetallic layer at the pad, so board-level drop tests are emphasized, and underfill is often added to reinforce the joints.

BGA vs. other packages

An LGA uses flat lands in the same grid instead of balls, so it can sit in a socket with spring pins, as with many PC processors, or be soldered with paste printed on the board. QFP and SOP use gull-wing leads at the edges, easy to inspect but limited in pin count, and QFN has exposed pads under its edges with a central thermal pad. A CSP shrinks the package to no more than about 1.2 times the die area, often with BGA-style balls. Inside advanced packages, microbumps at tens of micrometres and hybrid bonding go far finer than BGA balls.

In high-pin-count BGA packages, why are many of the balls assigned to power and ground?

  1. ATo make the package heavier
  2. BTo reduce the number of signals
  3. CTo spread large currents, lowering supply resistance and inductance and suppressing power noise and voltage drop
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. To spread large currents, lowering supply resistance and inductance and suppressing power noise and voltage drop

High-performance chips can draw over 100 A, and abrupt current changes cause voltage drop from path resistance (IR drop) and fluctuations from inductance. Paralleling many power and ground balls spreads the current and lowers path resistance and inductance, stabilizing the supply. It is not unusual for more than half the balls to be assigned to power and ground.

FAQ

What is the difference between BGA and LGA?

A BGA has solder balls on its underside, while an LGA has flat lands in the same grid. LGAs are often used with sockets that press spring contacts onto the lands, allowing replacement, and give a lower mounted height.

How are BGA solder joints inspected?

Since the joints are hidden under the package, they are inspected with X-ray imaging, and X-ray CT when a 3D view is needed to find voids, bridges or cracks.

What is the typical ball pitch of a BGA?

Package-to-board BGAs typically use pitches of about 0.4–1 mm. Finer connections inside packages use microbumps with pitches of tens of micrometres.
