What is Dry oxidation?

Thermal oxidation with dry oxygen. Growth is slow, but it produces dense, high-quality oxide, used for gate oxides and the like.

Japanese name: ドライ酸化

Dry oxidation appears in 4 practice questions across 1 topic, including Oxidation and diffusion.

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

How dry oxidation grows oxide

In dry oxidation, silicon wafers are heated in a furnace, typically somewhere between 800 and 1200 °C, in pure oxygen, and the reaction Si + O2 → SiO2 forms silicon dioxide. The reaction takes place at the silicon/oxide interface, not at the surface: oxygen molecules must diffuse through the oxide already grown to reach the silicon. The oxide therefore grows downward into the wafer as well as upward, consuming silicon equal to about 44% of the final oxide thickness. Because the original surface is consumed, the interface moves into fresh, clean silicon, which is one reason thermal oxide interfaces are of such high quality.

Deal-Grove kinetics

The Deal-Grove model describes thermal oxidation with two rate constants. While the oxide is thin, oxygen crosses it easily and the interface reaction limits growth, so thickness increases linearly with time at the rate B/A. As the film thickens, diffusion through it becomes the bottleneck, and thickness grows with the square root of time with the parabolic constant B. The crossover occurs near a thickness of A/2, tens of nanometers to about 100 nm for dry oxidation. Below a few tens of nanometers, dry oxidation runs faster than the model predicts, so thin gate oxides are set by empirical calibration.

Dry vs. wet oxidation

Wet oxidation uses steam instead of oxygen, Si + 2H2O → SiO2 + 2H2. Water dissolves in SiO2 several hundred times more readily than O2, so wet oxidation grows oxide several to ten times faster: at 1000 °C for an hour, roughly 70 nm dry versus about 400 nm wet. Some of the hydrogen remains in wet oxide, leaving it slightly less dense. Dry oxide is denser, has fewer interface states and allows precise thickness control, while wet oxide is chosen for thick isolation and masking layers where speed matters more than perfection.

Where dry oxide is used

The slow, controllable growth made dry oxidation the standard method for MOS gate oxides for decades, and it remains common for thin, high-quality layers such as pad oxides beneath silicon nitride and tunnel or interface layers. Small amounts of chlorine-containing gas are sometimes added to getter mobile ions such as sodium and to improve oxide quality. Growth rate also depends on crystal orientation, with (111) silicon oxidizing faster than (100). At advanced nodes, high-k dielectrics have replaced pure SiO2 gate oxides, but a thin silicon oxide interface layer still sits beneath them.

Answer: B. Wet oxidation grows oxide faster

Answer: A. Initial oxidation proceeds faster than the model predicts

The Deal-Grove model describes thick-oxide growth well, but dry oxidation in the initial regime below a few tens of nm proceeds faster than predicted. Proposed causes include alternative oxidant transport through thin films and interfacial stress effects. The model compensates with an effective initial thickness (τ, equivalent to about 20 nm), but setting gate oxides of a few nm still relies on empirical calibration.

FAQ

What is the difference between dry and wet oxidation?

Dry oxidation uses oxygen gas and grows a dense, high-quality oxide slowly, while wet oxidation uses steam and grows oxide several to ten times faster with slightly lower density. Dry oxide suits thin gate-quality layers; wet oxide suits thick isolation and masking films.

What is the chemical reaction in dry oxidation?

Silicon reacts with oxygen to form silicon dioxide: Si + O2 → SiO2. The reaction occurs at the silicon/oxide interface after oxygen diffuses through the existing oxide.

Why is dry oxide used for gate oxides?

Its slow growth allows precise control of very thin films, and the resulting oxide is dense with a low density of interface states. These properties give stable threshold voltages and reliable gate dielectrics.
