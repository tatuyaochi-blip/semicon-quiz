What is Photoresist?

A light-sensitive material whose solubility in developer changes when exposed. In positive resist the exposed areas dissolve; in negative resist they remain.

Japanese name: フォトレジスト

What must be considered when using photoresist as a mask for ion implantation?

  1. AThe thinner the resist, the better
  2. BThe resist does not need to be removed after implantation
  3. CThe resist must be thick enough for the implant energy so that ions do not penetrate it
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The resist must be thick enough for the implant energy so that ions do not penetrate it

Resist that is too thin lets high-energy ions punch through into masked regions. Being less dense than silicon, resist stops ions deeper, so it must be several times thicker than the ion range (Rp plus several ΔRp); MeV implants use resists several µm to about 10 µm thick. High-dose implants carbonize and harden the resist surface, making it hard to strip, so ashing conditions also need care.

What problem tends to arise when removing photoresist that has received a high-dose ion implant?

  1. AThe resist becomes transparent
  2. BThe surface becomes a carbonized, hardened crust, and rapid heating during normal ashing tends to make it burst from gas inside (popping)
  3. CThe resist dissolves away in water
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The surface becomes a carbonized, hardened crust, and rapid heating during normal ashing tends to make it burst from gas inside (popping)

Resist exposed to high-dose implants loses hydrogen at its surface and carbonizes into a hard crust tens of nm thick. Ashing it hot right away lets gas from the soft resist underneath burst the crust (popping), leaving residue and particles. Processes therefore start at low temperature and ramp up or remove the crust first with hydrogen-based plasmas; post-implant strip, residue removal included, is one of the trickier steps.
