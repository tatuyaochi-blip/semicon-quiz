What is Photoresist?

A light-sensitive material whose solubility in developer changes when exposed. In positive resist the exposed areas dissolve; in negative resist they remain.

Japanese name: フォトレジスト

Photoresist appears in 13 practice questions across 6 topics, including Ion implantation, Process materials (gases and chemicals), Etching.

Field: Lithography

How exposure changes solubility

Most resists for KrF (248 nm), ArF (193 nm) and EUV (13.5 nm) lithography are chemically amplified resists, consisting of a base resin, a photoacid generator (PAG), solvent and additives such as quenchers that limit acid diffusion. Exposure releases acid from the PAG; during the post-exposure bake (PEB), each acid molecule catalytically removes many protecting groups from the resin, changing its solubility and greatly raising sensitivity. A positive resist is then developed in 2.38% aqueous TMAH (tetramethylammonium hydroxide), which dissolves the exposed regions. In negative-tone development, an organic solvent dissolves the unexposed regions instead.

The resist process flow

Resist is spin-coated to a thickness from tens of nanometers to several micrometers, soft-baked to drive out solvent, exposed, baked again and developed. Supporting films help: a bottom antireflective coating (BARC) suppresses reflections from the substrate, and in ArF immersion lithography a topcoat keeps the immersion water from leaching resist components and keeps the surface hydrophobic during fast stage motion. After the pattern has served its purpose, the resist is removed by ashing, oxidizing it in an oxygen plasma to CO2 and H2O, followed by a wet clean.

Resist as an etch and implant mask

The pattern is only useful if the resist survives the next step. For ion implantation the resist must be several times thicker than the ion range, so MeV implants use films from several micrometers to about 10 µm. High-dose implants carbonize the resist surface into a hard crust; ashing it hot right away can make gas from the soft resist underneath burst the crust, called popping, so stripping starts at low temperature or uses hydrogen-based plasmas. For deep etches that would consume a thin resist, the pattern is first transferred into a hard mask of nitride, oxide or amorphous carbon.

Handling and storage

Because photoresist reacts to light and heat, it is stored in light-tight bottles at the specified temperature, often refrigerated, and used before its expiration date. A cold bottle must warm to room temperature before opening, or condensed moisture will change its performance, and the liquid is filtered through very fine filters just before coating. Lithography areas are lit with yellow light that lacks the short wavelengths resists respond to. Formulations balance resolution, sensitivity, line-edge roughness and etch resistance, which cannot all be maximized at once.

What must be considered when using photoresist as a mask for ion implantation?

  2. BThe resist does not need to be removed after implantation
  3. CThe resist must be thick enough for the implant energy so that ions do not penetrate it

Answer: C. The resist must be thick enough for the implant energy so that ions do not penetrate it

Resist that is too thin lets high-energy ions punch through into masked regions. Being less dense than silicon, resist stops ions deeper, so it must be several times thicker than the ion range (Rp plus several ΔRp); MeV implants use resists several µm to about 10 µm thick. High-dose implants carbonize and harden the resist surface, making it hard to strip, so ashing conditions also need care.

What problem tends to arise when removing photoresist that has received a high-dose ion implant?

  1. AThe resist becomes transparent
  2. BThe surface becomes a carbonized, hardened crust, and rapid heating during normal ashing tends to make it burst from gas inside (popping)
  3. CThe resist dissolves away in water
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The surface becomes a carbonized, hardened crust, and rapid heating during normal ashing tends to make it burst from gas inside (popping)

Resist exposed to high-dose implants loses hydrogen at its surface and carbonizes into a hard crust tens of nm thick. Ashing it hot right away lets gas from the soft resist underneath burst the crust (popping), leaving residue and particles. Processes therefore start at low temperature and ramp up or remove the crust first with hydrogen-based plasmas; post-implant strip, residue removal included, is one of the trickier steps.

FAQ

What is the difference between positive and negative photoresist?

In a positive resist the exposed areas become soluble and are removed by the developer, so the pattern matches the opaque parts of the mask. In a negative resist the exposed areas remain and the unexposed areas dissolve, reversing the image.

What is a chemically amplified resist?

It is a resist in which exposure generates acid from a photoacid generator, and that acid catalyzes many solubility-changing reactions during a post-exposure bake. One photon's effect is multiplied, giving the high sensitivity needed for KrF, ArF and EUV lithography.

How is photoresist removed after etching or implantation?

It is usually removed by ashing, in which an oxygen plasma or similar oxidizes the organic resist into CO2 and water vapor, followed by a wet clean to remove residues.
