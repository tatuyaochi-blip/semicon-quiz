What is RTP (rapid thermal processing)?

Heating single wafers to high temperature for a few seconds, typically with lamps. It allows annealing and thin oxide growth while limiting unwanted dopant diffusion.

Japanese name: RTP(急速熱処理)

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

How an RTP chamber heats a wafer

Rapid thermal processing (RTP) treats one wafer at a time in a chamber whose walls stay relatively cool. An array of tungsten-halogen lamps shines through a quartz window, and the wafer absorbs the radiation and heats at tens to hundreds of degrees per second, holds above 1000 °C for seconds, and cools quickly when the lamps dim. Because only the wafer has to change temperature, the thermal mass is tiny. Temperature is usually read by pyrometers, and since emissivity depends on films and patterns on the wafer, accurate temperature control is one of the main engineering challenges.

Why short times help: thermal budget

Dopant activation after ion implantation needs high temperature, but every second at temperature also lets dopants diffuse. Diffusion length grows roughly as the square root of diffusivity times time, so cutting time from many minutes to seconds sharply limits junction movement while activation still proceeds. A spike anneal ramps up and immediately down with essentially no hold. For even shallower junctions, flash-lamp and laser anneals shorten heating to milliseconds or less, heating mainly the near-surface region while the bulk of the wafer stays cooler.

What RTP is used for

Typical jobs include activation anneals after implantation, growth of thin oxides by rapid thermal oxidation, nitridation of thin dielectrics, and the anneals that react deposited metal with silicon to form silicides, often in two steps with a selective etch between them. RTP is also used on bare wafers to engineer oxygen precipitation, for example to form a near-surface denuded zone free of precipitates above a bulk gettering region. In each case the attraction is precise, short exposure to high temperature with fast switching of gas ambient.

RTP versus a batch furnace

A vertical furnace stacks around 100 wafers in a quartz tube and ramps slowly, so it suits long processes such as thick oxidation and long anneals, offering excellent temperature uniformity and high throughput per run. RTP processes wafers singly and quickly, which favors short, tightly controlled steps but makes uniformity harder: the wafer edge loses heat differently from the center, and steep temperature gradients at high temperature can generate slip dislocations. Fabs use both, choosing by required time at temperature and diffusion tolerance.

Answer: B. DZ (denuded zone)

Annealing above 1100 °C drives oxygen out of the near-surface region, creating a denuded zone (DZ) tens of µm deep free of precipitates. Subsequent low- and medium-temperature anneals nucleate precipitates in the bulk, forming a gettering layer. Devices sit in the defect-free surface while metals are trapped in the bulk. Today such profiles are also produced by hydrogen or argon anneals and rapid thermal processing.

  2. BAn ion implanter
  3. CA CMP tool

Answer: A. A vertical furnace (diffusion furnace)

Thermal oxidation, diffusion and annealing often use vertical furnaces that stack about 100 wafers in a quartz tube. Temperature in the tube is uniform, each run processes many wafers, and loading from below limits air ingress. Leading suppliers include Tokyo Electron and Kokusai Electric. When anneals of only seconds are needed, RTP tools heat wafers one at a time with lamps.

FAQ

What is the difference between RTP and RTA?

RTP is the general family of lamp-based single-wafer thermal processes. RTA (rapid thermal anneal) is one application of it, used for implant activation or silicide formation, while rapid thermal oxidation is another.

What is a spike anneal?

A spike anneal is an RTP recipe that ramps quickly to peak temperature and immediately cools with essentially no hold time, activating dopants while minimizing diffusion.

Why use RTP instead of a furnace?

RTP limits the time at high temperature to seconds, keeping dopant diffusion small, and lets each wafer get a precisely controlled thermal profile. Furnaces remain better for long, batch processes.
