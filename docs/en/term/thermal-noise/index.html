What is Thermal noise (Johnson noise)?

Noise caused by the thermal motion of electrons in a resistor. Its mean-square voltage is 4kTRΔf, proportional to temperature, resistance and bandwidth.

Japanese name: 熱雑音(ジョンソン雑音)

Try it: questions about Thermal noise (Johnson noise)

What increases the thermal noise (Johnson noise) voltage generated in a resistor?

  1. AHigher resistance, higher temperature and wider bandwidth
  2. BLower resistance
  3. CLower temperature
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Higher resistance, higher temperature and wider bandwidth

Thermal noise voltage is √(4kTRB), growing with resistance, temperature and bandwidth. As a rule of thumb, 1 kΩ at room temperature produces about 4 nV/√Hz, or about 0.57 µVrms over 20 kHz. Basic countermeasures are lower resistances and minimal bandwidth; radio telescopes and quantum-computer readout chains cool amplifiers to cryogenic temperatures. Notably, it occurs even with no current flowing.

When a voltage is sampled onto a capacitor C through a switch, what is the RMS thermal noise voltage left on the capacitor? (k: Boltzmann constant, T: absolute temperature)

  1. AIt grows as the capacitance C increases
  2. B√(kT/C), independent of the switch resistance
  3. CIt grows as the switch resistance increases
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. √(kT/C), independent of the switch resistance

A larger switch resistance increases thermal-noise density but also narrows the RC bandwidth, and the two cancel, leaving an rms noise of √(kT/C) on the capacitor (kT/C noise)—about 64 µVrms for 1 pF at room temperature. Halving the noise requires four times the capacitance, costing area and power, which makes it a key constraint in high-resolution ADCs and image sensors. Image sensors remove it with correlated double sampling.
