What is Thermal noise (Johnson noise)?

Noise caused by the thermal motion of electrons in a resistor. Its mean-square voltage is 4kTRΔf, proportional to temperature, resistance and bandwidth.

Japanese name: 熱雑音(ジョンソン雑音)

Thermal noise (Johnson noise) appears in 6 practice questions across 4 topics, including Analog and electronic circuits, A/D and D/A conversion, Image sensors and optical devices.

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

Where thermal noise comes from

Electrons in any resistor jiggle randomly with thermal energy, producing a tiny fluctuating voltage across its terminals even when no current flows. This thermal noise, also called Johnson-Nyquist noise, is white, meaning its power spectral density is flat over the frequencies of interest, and its amplitude follows a Gaussian distribution. Its mean-square voltage is 4kTRΔf, where k is Boltzmann's constant, T the absolute temperature, R the resistance and Δf the bandwidth. Equivalently, it can be modeled as a noise current with mean square 4kTΔf/R in parallel with the resistor.

Typical numbers

A 1 kΩ resistor at room temperature produces about 4 nV/√Hz, or about 0.57 µVrms over a 20 kHz audio bandwidth; since voltage noise scales with the square root of resistance, 100 kΩ gives about 40 nV/√Hz. When a voltage is sampled onto a capacitor through a switch, the resistor's noise density and the RC bandwidth cancel, leaving √(kT/C) regardless of switch resistance, about 64 µVrms for 1 pF. Halving kT/C noise requires four times the capacitance, which costs area and power in high-resolution ADCs and image sensors.

Thermal noise vs. shot noise and 1/f noise

Thermal noise exists in any resistance at equilibrium. Shot noise, by contrast, arises from discrete charges crossing a barrier such as a PN junction and requires current, with mean-square current 2qIΔf. Flicker or 1/f noise rises at low frequencies; in MOSFETs it comes mainly from traps at the gate dielectric interface, and its corner frequency, where it equals thermal noise, ranges from kHz to MHz. Fixed-pattern noise in image sensors is not temporal noise at all but repeatable pixel-to-pixel variation.

Designing around it

Since thermal noise depends on R, T and bandwidth, designers use lower resistances where possible, limit bandwidth to what the signal needs and, in radio astronomy or quantum-computer readout, cool amplifiers to cryogenic temperatures. Image sensors remove the kT/C reset noise with correlated double sampling, and ADC designers budget thermal noise against the ideal quantization-limited SNR of 6.02N + 1.76 dB. Thermal noise can also be useful: its unpredictability makes it a physical entropy source for on-chip true random number generators.

FAQ

What is the formula for thermal noise voltage?

The RMS thermal noise voltage of a resistor is √(4kTRΔf), where k is Boltzmann's constant, T the absolute temperature, R the resistance and Δf the bandwidth. At room temperature, 1 kΩ gives about 4 nV/√Hz.

Does thermal noise occur without current flowing?

Yes. Thermal noise comes from the random thermal motion of carriers and exists in any resistor at a temperature above absolute zero, even with no applied current, unlike shot noise.

What is kT/C noise?

It is the thermal noise left on a capacitor after sampling through a switch, with RMS value √(kT/C) independent of the switch resistance. For 1 pF at room temperature it is about 64 µV.
