What is Donor?

An impurity that supplies electrons to a semiconductor. For silicon, group V elements such as phosphorus (P) and arsenic (As) act as donors and make n-type semiconductors.

Japanese name: ドナー

Donor appears in 10 practice questions across 4 topics, including Semiconductor physics, Compound semiconductors, PN junctions and diodes.

Field: Semiconductor physics

How a donor supplies an electron

Phosphorus, a group V atom, replaces a silicon atom and uses four of its five valence electrons for bonds. The fifth is bound only weakly, because the surrounding silicon screens the ion's charge, so the system behaves like a large, shallow hydrogen atom. Its donor level lies about 0.045 eV below the conduction band for phosphorus and about 0.054 eV for arsenic, small compared with room-temperature thermal energy of about 0.026 eV, so nearly every donor ionizes. Each one releases a mobile electron and becomes a fixed positive ion in the lattice.

Carrier concentrations and the Fermi level

With all donors ionized, the electron density equals the donor concentration, and the law of mass action, n·p = ni², sets the holes: 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ donors with ni = 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ leave only 10⁴ holes per cm³, yet these minority carriers govern junction leakage and bipolar action. The Fermi level moves toward the conduction band by about 60 mV per decade of doping, and at very high doping enters it, making the material degenerate. Doping is extremely effective: silicon has about 5 × 10²² atoms/cm³, so 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ is one atom in five million, yet resistivity falls to roughly 0.5 Ω·cm.

Freeze-out and intrinsic regions

Electron density stays close to the donor concentration only over a middle temperature range, the extrinsic region where devices are meant to operate. Below roughly 100 K in silicon, thermal energy is too small to free electrons from donors, and carrier density drops sharply in what is called freeze-out. At high temperatures, intrinsic carriers generated across the band gap grow steeply and eventually exceed the donor density, so doping no longer controls conduction. Even before that, the rising minority-carrier density increases leakage, one reason hot chips leak more.

Donors in practice

Phosphorus and arsenic are the workhorse donors in silicon. Arsenic is heavier and diffuses more slowly, making it suitable for shallow n+ source and drain regions, and antimony is also used where slow diffusion is wanted, such as heavily doped substrates and buried layers. Donors can also appear unintentionally: oxygen in Czochralski silicon can form thermal donors during heating around 450 °C. In compound semiconductors, behavior can depend on the lattice site; silicon in GaAs normally sits on gallium sites and acts as the standard donor.

  2. B1×10⁶ cm⁻³
  3. C1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 1×10⁴ cm⁻³

With all donors ionized, the electron density is n ≈ 1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³. From the law of mass action n × p = ni², p = (1×10¹⁰)² ÷ 1×10¹⁶ = 1×10⁴ cm⁻³. These minority carriers are a trillionth of the majority carriers, yet they carry the reverse current of a PN junction and the operation of a bipolar transistor. Since ni rises steeply with temperature, the minority carrier density also grows sharply when hot.

  3. CNear the middle of the band gap

Answer: A. Just below the bottom of the conduction band (about 0.045 eV below)

The phosphorus donor level lies only about 0.045 eV below the conduction band, so even room-temperature thermal energy (about 0.026 eV) ionizes nearly all donors. Arsenic is about 0.054 eV, and the boron acceptor level is about 0.045 eV above the valence band. These shallow levels are weakly bound because the host's dielectric constant screens them, so they can be estimated with a hydrogen-atom model. Deep levels near midgap come from impurities such as gold and act as recombination centers.

FAQ

Why does phosphorus act as a donor in silicon?

Phosphorus has five valence electrons, one more than silicon needs for its four bonds. The extra electron is weakly bound, about 0.045 eV below the conduction band, so it is freed at room temperature and makes the silicon n-type.

How do you calculate the hole concentration in n-type silicon?

Use the mass-action law p = ni²/n with n ≈ ND. For ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and ni = 10¹⁰ cm⁻³, p = 10⁴ cm⁻³.

What is carrier freeze-out?

At cryogenic temperatures, below roughly 100 K in silicon, there is too little thermal energy to ionize donors, so electrons stay bound and the free-carrier density falls sharply.
