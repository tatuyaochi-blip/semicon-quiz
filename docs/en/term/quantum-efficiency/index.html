What is Quantum efficiency?

The fraction of incident photons converted into signal electrons. The higher it is, the better the sensitivity.

Japanese name: 量子効率

Quantum efficiency appears in 5 practice questions across 3 topics, including Image sensors and optical devices, Compound semiconductors, Silicon photonics.

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Internal and external quantum efficiency

External quantum efficiency (EQE) counts electrons collected per incident photon. It is the product of three factors: the fraction of light not reflected at the surface, the fraction absorbed in the active material, and the fraction of generated carriers collected before they recombine. Internal quantum efficiency (IQE) leaves out the reflection and counts per absorbed photon. For light-emitting diodes the direction reverses: EQE is photons emitted per injected electron, the product of IQE and light-extraction efficiency. Datasheets that say QE usually mean EQE at a stated wavelength.

Wavelength dependence in silicon

Silicon absorbs blue light within a fraction of a micrometre of the surface, red light over several micrometres and near-infrared over tens of micrometres, and becomes transparent beyond about 1.1 µm, where photon energy falls below its band gap. Blue carriers generated near the surface are easily lost to surface recombination, while long wavelengths pass through a thin photodiode unabsorbed. Visible quantum efficiency of CMOS image sensors is typically about 50 to 80%, raised by back-side illumination, anti-reflection coatings and microlenses, and some cooled scientific sensors exceed 90%.

From quantum efficiency to responsivity

Responsivity R, in amperes per watt, links QE to the current a photodiode produces: R = ηqλ/hc, or about η × λ[µm]/1.24. Longer wavelengths carry less energy per photon, so one watt contains more photons and gives more current at the same η. At 1.55 µm an 80% efficient detector gives about 1.0 A/W, and even a perfect one cannot exceed about 1.25 A/W. Silicon cannot absorb this wavelength, so telecom receivers use InGaAs or germanium absorbers.

Why it matters for low-light imaging

Photon arrival is random, so even a perfect detector has shot noise equal to the square root of the signal. A sensor with lower QE loses photons before noise is added, and its shot-noise-limited signal-to-noise ratio scales with the square root of QE, so halving QE is like halving exposure time. Avalanche or electron-multiplying detectors can produce more than one output electron per photon, but that is gain applied after detection; QE itself remains at most 100% in ordinary photodiodes.

Answer: B. The ratio of electrons generated and collected as signal to the number of incident photons

Quantum efficiency is the fraction of incident photons converted to electrons and read out as signal, and it varies with wavelength. In silicon sensors it peaks near green; blue is absorbed near the surface and partly lost, while red and near-infrared penetrate deeply and are not fully absorbed. Back-side illumination, anti-reflection coatings and microlenses raise it, and some scientific cooled sensors exceed 90%.

What is the problem called in which LED external quantum efficiency is high for blue (InGaN) and red (AlGaInP) but low in the green-to-yellow range between them?

  1. ARed-out
  2. BBlue shift
  3. CGreen gap
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Green gap

Green emission from InGaN needs high indium content, bringing compositional fluctuation, strain and strong polarization fields (the quantum-confined Stark effect) that sap efficiency. AlGaInP, pushed to shorter wavelengths, approaches an indirect gap as its conduction-band valleys reorder, and efficiency falls. So efficient LEDs are hard to make at about 530–590 nm—the green gap—precisely where the eye is most sensitive, which also limits lighting efficiency.

FAQ

What is the difference between internal and external quantum efficiency?

External quantum efficiency counts collected electrons per incident photon, including reflection losses. Internal quantum efficiency counts per absorbed photon, isolating the material and collection efficiency.

How do you convert quantum efficiency to responsivity?

Use R ≈ η × λ / 1.24, with λ in micrometres and R in A/W. For example, 80% quantum efficiency at 1.55 µm gives about 1.0 A/W.

Can quantum efficiency exceed 100 percent?

Not in an ordinary photodiode without gain, since at visible and infrared wavelengths each photon generates at most one collected electron-hole pair. Devices with avalanche multiplication give more output current, but that is counted as gain separate from quantum efficiency.
