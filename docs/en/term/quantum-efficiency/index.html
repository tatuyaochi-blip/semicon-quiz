What is Quantum efficiency?

The fraction of incident photons converted into signal electrons. The higher it is, the better the sensitivity.

Japanese name: 量子効率

Quantum efficiency appears in 5 practice questions across 3 topics, including Image sensors and optical devices, Compound semiconductors, Silicon photonics.

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Try it: questions about Quantum efficiency

What is the definition of the quantum efficiency of an image sensor?

  1. AThe number of outputs relative to the number of pixels
  2. BThe ratio of electrons generated and collected as signal to the number of incident photons
  3. CThe ratio of output voltage to supply voltage
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The ratio of electrons generated and collected as signal to the number of incident photons

Quantum efficiency is the fraction of incident photons converted to electrons and read out as signal, and it varies with wavelength. In silicon sensors it peaks near green; blue is absorbed near the surface and partly lost, while red and near-infrared penetrate deeply and are not fully absorbed. Back-side illumination, anti-reflection coatings and microlenses raise it, and some scientific cooled sensors exceed 90%.

What is the problem called in which LED external quantum efficiency is high for blue (InGaN) and red (AlGaInP) but low in the green-to-yellow range between them?

  1. ARed-out
  2. BBlue shift
  3. CGreen gap
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Green gap

Green emission from InGaN needs high indium content, bringing compositional fluctuation, strain and strong polarization fields (the quantum-confined Stark effect) that sap efficiency. AlGaInP, pushed to shorter wavelengths, approaches an indirect gap as its conduction-band valleys reorder, and efficiency falls. So efficient LEDs are hard to make at about 530–590 nm—the green gap—precisely where the eye is most sensitive, which also limits lighting efficiency.
