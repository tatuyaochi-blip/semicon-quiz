What is HDP-CVD (high-density plasma CVD)?

CVD using a high-density plasma that deposits while ion sputtering trims film at the trench openings. It has been used to fill narrow trenches with insulator without voids.

Japanese name: HDP-CVD(高密度プラズマCVD)

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

Deposition and sputtering at the same time

HDP-CVD is a CVD process that uses a source such as an inductively coupled plasma (ICP) to create a dense plasma, above 10¹¹ cm⁻³, at low pressure, depositing SiO2 from gases such as SiH4 and O2. Separately from the power that generates the plasma, an RF bias on the substrate draws in ions such as argon. Its defining feature is that plasma density and ion energy can be tuned independently: while film deposits, ions simultaneously sputter it away. The ratio of deposition rate to sputter rate (the D/S ratio) is a key parameter that determines how a trench fills.

Why it fills without voids

When ordinary CVD fills a narrow trench, film can build up thickly at the opening corners and overhang, sealing the entrance before the bottom fills and leaving a void. In HDP-CVD the sputter yield is higher on sloped surfaces than on flat ones, so the opening corners are cut back into facets and the entrance stays open. Ions also arrive nearly perpendicular to the substrate, so film builds quickly on the trench bottom and less on the sidewalls. Some of the sputtered material redeposits on the opposite sidewall. In this way the trench fills from the bottom up.

Main uses and generations

From roughly the 0.25 µm generation to around the 65 nm generation, HDP-CVD was widely used to fill STI (shallow trench isolation) trenches, for the pre-metal dielectric (PMD) between transistors and wiring, and for the inter-metal dielectric (IMD) between aluminum lines. Because the surface is etched during deposition, the film is relatively dense, and the process can also deposit fluorine-doped FSG with a lower dielectric constant and phosphorus-doped PSG. After gap fill the surface keeps bumps that follow the underlying topography, so it is generally planarized by CMP before the next step.

Limits and successor techniques

As trenches become narrower and deeper, with higher aspect ratios, sputtering alone can no longer keep the entrance from closing. Stronger sputtering brings its own problems: clipping trench corners and underlying films (such as the nitride liner in STI or the corners of metal lines), plus plasma damage and charging. Finer generations have therefore turned to alternating deposition and etch steps, sub-atmospheric CVD using ozone and TEOS, flowable CVD that fills trenches with a film that flows and then cures it, spin-on dielectrics, and ALD.

Answer: A. Because ion sputtering during deposition trims film at the openings, allowing filling with few voids

Filling a narrow trench by ordinary CVD can let film build up at the opening corners and pinch off first, trapping a void. HDP-CVD deposits from a dense plasma (above 10¹¹ cm⁻³) while ions such as argon, drawn in by substrate bias, sputter the corners at an angle, so the trench fills from the bottom up. It is used for STI and inter-metal gap fill in the 0.25 µm to 65 nm generations; narrower trenches use flowable CVD and similar methods.

FAQ

What is the difference between HDP-CVD and PECVD?

Both are plasma CVD, but compared with typical parallel-plate PECVD, HDP-CVD creates a denser plasma at lower pressure and controls ion energy separately with a substrate bias. Because sputtering occurs during deposition, it is suited to filling narrow trenches.

What is the D/S ratio in HDP-CVD?

It is the ratio of the deposition rate to the sputtering rate. If it is too high, openings tend to close and leave voids; if too low, deposition is slow and trench corners or underlying layers get etched. It is tuned to the trench shape through gas flows and bias power.

Is HDP-CVD still used?

It may still be used for gap fill in trenches with relatively low aspect ratios and between interconnect lines. For very narrow STI at leading-edge nodes, however, methods with better gap fill, such as flowable CVD, ALD and alternating deposition-etch processes, have come into use.
