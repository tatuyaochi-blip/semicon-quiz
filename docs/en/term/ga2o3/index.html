What is Ga2O3 (gallium oxide)?

An oxide semiconductor with a band gap of about 4.5-4.9 eV, even wider than SiC or GaN. Its crystals can be grown from the melt, and it is being researched as a next-generation power semiconductor material.

Japanese name: Ga2O3(酸化ガリウム)

Where it sits among power materials

Gallium oxide is grouped with diamond and aluminum nitride as an ultra-wide-bandgap semiconductor, with a gap of about 4.5-4.9 eV compared with about 3.3 eV for SiC and 3.4 eV for GaN. Its most studied form is beta-Ga2O3, the thermodynamically stable monoclinic phase. A wider gap goes with a higher critical breakdown field, expected to be around 8 MV/cm, versus roughly 3 MV/cm for SiC and GaN and about 0.3 MV/cm for silicon, and also with far fewer intrinsic carriers at high temperature.

Why the breakdown field matters

For unipolar power devices such as Schottky diodes and MOSFETs, Baliga's figure of merit, the product of permittivity, mobility and the cube of the breakdown field, indicates how low the drift-layer resistance can be for a given voltage rating. A higher breakdown field lets the drift layer be thinner and more heavily doped for the same blocking voltage. Ga2O3's electron mobility is modest, lower than that of SiC or GaN, but the cubed breakdown field dominates, so its figure of merit is expected to exceed both, which is the core of the interest in it.

Growth from the melt

SiC boules are grown by sublimation at well over 2000 °C, and GaN is mostly grown on foreign substrates or by vapor-phase methods, so both have costly substrates. Ga2O3, by contrast, melts congruently and can be grown from the melt with methods related to those used for sapphire, such as edge-defined film-fed growth or Czochralski-type pulling. This opens a route to large, relatively inexpensive single-crystal substrates. Device layers are then grown epitaxially, and n-type conductivity is readily controlled with donors such as tin or silicon.

The open problems

Practical p-type Ga2O3 has not been achieved: acceptor levels are deep and holes tend to localize, so they contribute little conduction. Without p-type layers, conventional pn junctions, inversion-channel MOSFETs and bipolar devices are unavailable, so work focuses on Schottky barrier diodes, depletion-mode or specially engineered transistors, and heterojunctions with other p-type oxides. The second issue is thermal conductivity more than ten times lower than SiC, which makes removing heat difficult; approaches include thinning the substrate and bonding the device layer to a better heat spreader.

Answer: C. It has a band gap (about 4.5–4.9 eV) even larger than SiC and GaN

Ga2O3 has a band gap of about 4.5–4.9 eV, wider than SiC or GaN, and an expected breakdown field of about 8 MV/cm, promising even higher voltage ratings and lower losses. Unlike SiC and GaN, it can be grown from a melt, potentially enabling large, inexpensive substrates; Japanese companies and institutes lead its development. Its challenges are the difficulty of making p-type material and its low thermal conductivity.

FAQ

What is the band gap of gallium oxide?

Beta-Ga2O3 has a band gap of about 4.5-4.9 eV, wider than SiC (about 3.3 eV) and GaN (about 3.4 eV), which places it among the ultra-wide-bandgap semiconductors.

Why is Ga2O3 hard to make p-type?

Candidate acceptors sit deep in the band gap and holes tend to become localized, so very few mobile holes are produced at normal temperatures. Device designs therefore rely on n-type material, Schottky contacts and heterojunctions.

Is Ga2O3 better than SiC for power devices?

On paper its higher breakdown field promises lower conduction loss at a given voltage rating, and melt growth could make substrates cheaper. In practice the lack of p-type doping and its poor thermal conductivity are major hurdles that SiC does not have.
