What is Short-channel effect?

Phenomena in which, as gate length shrinks, the drain's electric field lowers the threshold voltage and increases off-state leakage current.

Japanese name: 短チャネル効果

Short-channel effect appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA).

Field: MOSFET

What short-channel effects are

Short-channel effects are the collective name for phenomena in which a MOSFET's characteristics deviate from long-channel theory and degrade as its gate length shrinks. Typical examples are threshold-voltage roll-off, where the threshold voltage drops as gate length decreases; DIBL (drain-induced barrier lowering), where the threshold voltage drops as drain voltage rises; degraded subthreshold swing; and increased off-state leakage. In the extreme, the gate can no longer shut off the current, which is punch-through. Another problem is that a slight change in gate length from process variation causes a large change in threshold voltage.

Cause: weakened gate control

In a long-channel MOSFET, the channel potential is set almost entirely by the gate. As the gate length shrinks, the source and drain junction depletion regions occupy a larger share of the channel, and part of the channel depletion charge is supported by the source and drain rather than the gate (charge sharing). Less gate voltage is then needed for inversion, so the threshold voltage drops. When the drain field also penetrates the channel and lowers the source-side potential barrier, the result is DIBL. The scale length λ estimates how far this influence reaches; with a gate length about 5–10 times λ, the effects stay small.

Metrics for their severity

DIBL is expressed as the change in threshold voltage divided by the change in drain voltage, in mV/V. The subthreshold swing (S) is the change in gate voltage needed to raise the drain current tenfold in the near-off region; its ideal value at room temperature is about 60 mV/dec. The stronger the short-channel effects, the larger the DIBL and the more S rises above 60 mV/dec. Threshold roll-off is evaluated by plotting threshold voltage against gate length and seeing at what gate length it starts to drop sharply.

How suppression techniques evolved

The basic remedy is to strengthen the gate's control of the channel and weaken the drain's influence. Planar MOSFETs thinned the gate dielectric, then introduced high-k films, made source/drain junctions shallower and locally increased doping at the source/drain edges with halo implants. As these approached their limits, the industry moved to structures in which the gate covers more surfaces: fully depleted SOI with a thin silicon channel layer, FinFETs whose gate wraps the fin on three sides, and GAA (nanosheet) transistors that surround the channel completely. FinFETs require a fin narrower than the gate length.

Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Shortening the gate lowers the threshold voltage and increases off-state leakage

As gate length shrinks, the source and drain depletion regions become relatively large and control part of the channel charge instead of the gate. Consequences include threshold roll-off, drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), degraded subthreshold swing and rising off-state leakage. Device characteristics also become highly sensitive to small process variations in gate length.

FAQ

What are short-channel effects, in simple terms?

When a transistor's gate length gets short, the drain's electric field reaches into the channel and weakens the gate's control, lowering the threshold voltage and increasing off-state leakage. They are one of the major barriers to scaling.

How are short-channel effects related to DIBL?

DIBL (drain-induced barrier lowering) is one of the representative short-channel effects. The drain voltage lowers the source-side potential barrier, reducing the threshold voltage. The shorter the gate, the larger the DIBL, and in the extreme it leads to punch-through.

What is the reverse short-channel effect?

It is a phenomenon in which, over a certain range, the threshold voltage instead rises as gate length decreases. It occurs because halo implants, or defects created by implantation that cause dopants to accumulate at the channel ends, make the average doping higher in shorter channels. At even shorter lengths, the ordinary short-channel effect wins out and the threshold voltage falls.
