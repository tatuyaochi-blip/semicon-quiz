What is Switching loss?

Loss that occurs while a power device switches between on and off, when voltage and current are present at the same time. It grows with switching frequency.

Japanese name: スイッチング損失

Switching loss appears in 5 practice questions across 2 topics, including Power semiconductors, Power Electronics Circuits.

Field: Power semiconductors

Where the energy goes

In a hard-switched converter driving an inductive load, the switch cannot change voltage and current simultaneously. At turn-on, current must first rise to the full load current while the voltage is still near the bus voltage, held there by the freewheeling diode; only then does the voltage fall. Turn-off reverses the sequence. During each overlap, power V × I is dissipated. With linear ramps, each transition costs about ½ × V × I × transition time: at 400 V, 10 A and 20 ns, roughly 40 µJ. Turning on and off at 100 kHz then wastes around 8 W.

Switching loss vs. conduction loss

Switching loss equals the energy per cycle, Eon plus Eoff, times the switching frequency, so it scales linearly with frequency. Conduction loss, I² × RDS(on) for a MOSFET or current times saturation voltage for an IGBT (insulated-gate bipolar transistor), depends on how long the device conducts but not on how often it switches. At low frequencies conduction loss dominates; as frequency rises, switching loss takes over. Designers choose frequency as a compromise, since higher frequency shrinks inductors, transformers and capacitors but raises switching loss and electromagnetic noise.

Extra contributions and device technology

Besides voltage-current overlap, hard turn-on discharges the device's own output capacitance through its channel, and the reverse recovery charge of a silicon PN diode in the same leg adds a current spike and further loss. IGBTs also show a tail current at turn-off, which limits them to relatively low frequencies, typically up to a few tens of kHz. Silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) transistors have smaller capacitances and gate charge, and SiC Schottky diodes and GaN devices have little or no reverse recovery, which is why they allow much higher switching frequencies.

Controlling it in practice

Gate resistance sets how fast the gate capacitance charges, trading switching loss against voltage overshoot, ringing and electromagnetic interference; separate turn-on and turn-off resistances or active gate drivers refine that balance. Minimizing stray inductance in the power loop allows faster switching without destructive overshoot. Soft-switching topologies, such as resonant and LLC converters, arrange zero-voltage or zero-current switching so the overlap nearly disappears. Datasheet Eon and Eoff values are measured with a double-pulse test under stated voltage, current, gate resistance and temperature, and should be rescaled for the actual operating conditions.

Try it: questions about Switching loss

Assume that the power lost during a transition is, on average, half of voltage × current. For a power device switching 400 V and 10 A with turn-on and turn-off transition times of 50 ns each and a switching frequency of 100 kHz, which of the following is closest to the switching loss?

  1. A10 W
  2. B20 W
  3. C40 W
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 20 W

The energy lost in one transition is (1/2) × V × I × t, so turn-on and turn-off together lose (1/2) × 400 × 10 × (50 + 50) ns = 200 µJ per period. Multiplying by the number of periods per second (100 kHz) gives a switching loss of 200 µJ × 100,000 = 20 W. Switching loss is proportional to frequency, so at 200 kHz it rises to 40 W. SiC and GaN devices, which switch faster, and soft switching are ways to reduce this loss.

FAQ

How do you calculate switching loss?

Multiply the total energy per switching cycle, Eon plus Eoff from the datasheet or measurement, by the switching frequency. A rough estimate per transition is ½ × V × I × transition time for a hard-switched inductive load.

Why does switching loss increase with frequency?

Each switching event dissipates a roughly fixed energy, so doubling the number of events per second doubles the average power lost.

Why do SiC and GaN have lower switching losses than silicon IGBTs?

They switch faster thanks to smaller capacitances and gate charge, have no IGBT-style tail current, and have little or no reverse recovery, so the voltage-current overlap and related losses are much smaller.
