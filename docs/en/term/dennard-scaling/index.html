What is Dennard scaling?

The rule that shrinking transistor dimensions and supply voltage by the same factor gives faster, denser circuits at constant power density. It broke down in the mid-2000s when voltages could no longer be lowered easily.

Japanese name: デナードスケーリング

The scaling rules

Dennard scaling, described by Robert Dennard and colleagues in 1974, says how to shrink a MOSFET so every quantity improves together. Divide channel length, width, oxide thickness and supply voltage by a factor k and multiply doping by k. Gate capacitance then falls by k, gate delay falls by k, and power per transistor falls by k² because both voltage and current shrink. Since each transistor also occupies 1/k² of the area, power per unit area stays constant while the chip gets faster and denser.

Why it broke down

The rule assumes threshold voltage scales with supply voltage, but subthreshold leakage sets a floor. At room temperature the subthreshold slope cannot be steeper than about 60 mV per decade of current, because thermal voltage does not shrink with dimensions. Each step down in threshold voltage therefore multiplies off-state leakage. Gate-oxide tunneling grew as oxides approached a few nanometres. By the mid-2000s supply voltages stalled near 1 V while transistor density kept rising, so power density was no longer constant.

Consequences for chip design

With voltage stuck, raising clock frequency raised power density beyond what packages could cool, so single-core frequency growth flattened and designers turned to multiple cores. Chips became power-limited rather than area-limited, which led to dark silicon: more transistors than can switch at full speed simultaneously. The industry's answers include fine-grained DVFS, power gating, specialized accelerators that perform a task with far less energy than a general-purpose core, and device innovations such as strained silicon, high-k metal gates and FinFETs that improve performance per watt.

Dennard scaling vs. Moore's law

Moore's law is an observation about integration: the number of transistors on a chip at minimum cost doubles roughly every two years. Dennard scaling is a recipe for making each of those transistors better at constant power density. The two moved together for decades, which is why each new node used to bring higher clock speeds for free. Density scaling continued after Dennard scaling ended, so Moore's law outlived it, but the extra transistors no longer came with matching power savings.

FAQ

What is the difference between Moore's law and Dennard scaling?

Moore's law describes how many transistors fit on a chip over time. Dennard scaling describes how shrinking dimensions and voltage together keeps power density constant, and it stopped holding in the mid-2000s while density growth continued.

Why did Dennard scaling end?

Threshold voltage could not keep falling without an exponential rise in leakage current, because the subthreshold slope is limited by temperature, not dimensions. Supply voltage therefore stalled and power density began to rise.

What does the end of Dennard scaling have to do with multicore processors?

Higher clock frequencies became too power-hungry once voltage stopped scaling, so designers used the extra transistors for more cores at moderate frequency instead of one faster core.
