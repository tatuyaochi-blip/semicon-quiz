What is ArF excimer laser?

A laser using argon and fluorine that emits 193 nm ultraviolet light. Combined with immersion, ArF immersion lithography is a leading-edge technique alongside EUV. The 248 nm version is the KrF excimer laser.

How an ArF laser produces 193 nm light

The laser chamber holds a gas mixture of a small amount of argon and fluorine diluted in neon as a buffer gas. A fast electrical discharge excites the mixture and forms ArF molecules that are bound only in the excited state, an excimer or excited dimer. When the molecule emits a 193 nm photon it drops to a repulsive ground state and immediately falls apart, so the lower laser level is effectively always empty and population inversion is easy to achieve. The laser is pulsed, with lithography lasers running at repetition rates of several kHz.

Why lithography needs a narrow line

Few materials are transparent at 193 nm, so projection lenses are built from fused silica with some calcium fluoride elements. With so little choice of glass, chromatic aberration cannot be corrected the way it is in visible optics, and the laser output must instead be line-narrowed to a bandwidth of well under a picometer. Lithography lasers also stabilize pulse energy and wavelength from pulse to pulse, and the fluorine-based gas fill degrades over time and is periodically refreshed. Photoresists had to change as well, because phenolic KrF resists absorb too strongly at 193 nm.

Resolution and immersion

Resolution follows the Rayleigh relation R = k1·λ/NA. Dry ArF scanners reach a numerical aperture (NA) of about 0.93. ArF immersion fills the gap between the final lens and the wafer with water, whose refractive index at 193 nm is about 1.44, allowing NA up to 1.35. With k1 near its practical limit, single exposures resolve roughly 38 to 40 nm half-pitch. Smaller pitches are made with multiple patterning, such as litho-etch-litho-etch or self-aligned double and quadruple patterning, which is how ArF immersion reached nodes far below its single-exposure limit.

ArF versus KrF and EUV

The KrF excimer laser uses krypton and fluorine to emit 248 nm and serves less critical layers where cost matters more than resolution. EUV lithography at 13.5 nm does not use an excimer laser at all: its light comes from a tin plasma, and its optics are reflective mirrors. Even in leading-edge chips patterned with EUV for the tightest layers, many other layers are still exposed with ArF immersion, which has higher throughput and a mature infrastructure.

Answer: C. Argon and fluorine (with a buffer gas such as neon)

An ArF excimer laser excites, by electrical discharge, a gas mixture of small amounts of argon and fluorine diluted in a neon buffer gas, emitting 193 nm light. An excimer (excited dimer) is a molecule bound only in its excited state; it falls apart on returning to the ground state, enabling efficient UV emission. KrF excimer lasers (248 nm) use krypton and fluorine. Oxygen–nitrogen or hydrogen–helium mixtures do not produce 193 nm light.

FAQ

What wavelength does an ArF excimer laser emit?

It emits deep-ultraviolet light at 193 nm. The KrF excimer laser, the previous lithography generation, emits at 248 nm.

What is the difference between dry ArF and ArF immersion lithography?

Dry ArF has air between the lens and the wafer, limiting the numerical aperture to about 0.93. Immersion fills that gap with water, raising NA to about 1.35 so that features roughly 30 percent smaller can be resolved at the same wavelength.

Why is it called an excimer laser?

Excimer is short for excited dimer, a molecule such as ArF that is bound only in its excited state. Because it dissociates as soon as it emits, the lower laser level stays empty, making efficient ultraviolet lasing possible.
