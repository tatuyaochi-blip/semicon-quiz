What is Oxygen precipitates (BMD)?

Tiny defects formed inside a CZ silicon wafer when its oxygen precipitates during heat treatment. Also called BMDs (bulk micro defects), they serve as gettering sites that capture metal impurities.

Japanese name: 酸素析出物(BMD)

Field: Silicon wafers

How oxygen precipitates form

Czochralski (CZ) silicon is pulled from a melt held in a quartz crucible, which dissolves and releases oxygen into the crystal, giving interstitial oxygen concentrations on the order of 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. At typical device-processing temperatures this oxygen is supersaturated, so during heat treatment it clusters into silicon-oxide precipitates. Nucleation is favored at lower temperatures and growth at higher ones. Because the oxide occupies more volume than the silicon it replaces, growing precipitates emit silicon interstitials and strain the lattice, often producing punched-out dislocations or stacking faults around them. Float-zone silicon, grown without a crucible, contains little oxygen.

Intrinsic gettering and the denuded zone

Precipitates and their strained surroundings act as sinks for fast-diffusing metals such as iron, copper and nickel, which are captured in the bulk as the wafer cools instead of degrading junctions and gate oxides near the surface. This is intrinsic gettering. It works only if the top layer of tens of micrometers, where devices are built, stays free of precipitates. That denuded zone was classically formed by a high-low-high anneal: oxygen out-diffuses near the surface, then nuclei form and grow in the bulk. Rapid thermal anneals that engineer the vacancy profile are a modern alternative.

Getting the density right

BMD density is typically controlled to about 10⁸ to 10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Too few precipitates give weak gettering; too many, or precipitates too close to the surface, cause junction leakage and can nucleate oxidation-induced stacking faults. Large precipitates also lower the wafer's resistance to slip and warpage during high-temperature steps. Modern processes have small thermal budgets, so precipitates may not nucleate on their own. Adding trace nitrogen, about 10¹³ to 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, during crystal growth promotes nucleation, shrinks vacancy-cluster voids (COPs) and strengthens the wafer, with negligible electrical effect.

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic gettering

Extrinsic gettering uses deliberately introduced sinks outside the device region, such as backside mechanical damage or a polysilicon film on the back, while heavily doped substrates also attract certain metals. Epitaxial wafers combine approaches: a lightly doped p-type epi layer on a p+ substrate provides a surface nearly free of precipitates and COPs, and the substrate below handles gettering while also suppressing CMOS latch-up. BMD density is commonly evaluated by preferential etching of cleaved cross-sections and counting the revealed pits, or by infrared light-scattering techniques.

Answer: B. A technique that captures harmful metal impurities at locations away from the device region

Gettering traps metallic impurities such as iron, copper and nickel—which degrade devices—at sites away from the surface device region, inside the wafer or at the back. Metals raise leakage current and degrade oxides, and contamination as low as about 10¹⁰ atoms/cm² can matter. Intrinsic gettering uses oxygen precipitates in the wafer bulk; extrinsic gettering uses backside damage or polysilicon films.

Answer: A. To use them as gettering sites that capture metal impurities

Oxygen in CZ wafers forms precipitates during heat treatment—bulk micro defects (BMDs). The precipitates and their surrounding strain and dislocations trap metal impurities, providing intrinsic gettering that keeps contamination away from the surface device region. BMD density is controlled to about 10⁸–10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Because BMDs near the surface would cause leakage, a defect-free surface layer is preserved.

FAQ

What is a BMD in a silicon wafer?

A bulk micro defect is an oxygen precipitate, often with associated dislocations or stacking faults, that forms inside a CZ wafer during heat treatment. Controlled BMDs serve as gettering sites for metal contamination.

What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic gettering?

Intrinsic gettering uses oxygen precipitates inside the wafer bulk as sinks for metals. Extrinsic gettering uses sinks introduced from outside, such as backside damage, a backside polysilicon layer or a heavily doped substrate.

Why must the wafer surface be free of oxygen precipitates?

Devices are built in the top few micrometers, and precipitates or the defects around them there would cause junction leakage and oxide problems. A precipitate-free denuded zone keeps the active region clean while the bulk does the gettering.
