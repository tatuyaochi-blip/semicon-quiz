What is MBE (molecular beam epitaxy)?

Epitaxial growth in ultra-high vacuum by supplying source materials to the substrate as molecular beams. It controls thickness and composition at the atomic-layer level and is used in compound-semiconductor research and quantum structures.

Japanese name: MBE(分子線エピタキシー)

Field: Epitaxial growth

How MBE grows a crystal

Molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) takes place in ultra-high vacuum, around 1e-8 Pa. Source materials such as gallium, aluminum, indium and arsenic are heated in effusion cells, and the evaporated atoms or molecules travel as beams to a heated substrate without colliding with gas molecules. Mechanical shutters in front of each cell switch a source on or off in a fraction of a second. Growth is slow, around 1 µm per hour, roughly one atomic layer per second for GaAs, so layer thickness can be set at the atomic-layer level and interfaces are abrupt.

Watching growth with RHEED

Reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) directs a 10-30 keV electron beam at a grazing angle of about 1-3° onto the surface and displays the diffraction pattern on a phosphor screen. The pattern reveals whether the surface is smooth or rough and which surface reconstruction is present. During layer-by-layer growth, the intensity oscillates once per completed monolayer, so operators can count layers and calibrate growth rate in real time. RHEED needs ultra-high vacuum to keep the electron beam from scattering, which is why it pairs naturally with MBE.

MBE versus MOCVD and LPE

Metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) supplies metal-organic and hydride gases in a flowing ambient at much higher pressure; it grows faster, handles multiple large wafers and dominates production of LEDs and many lasers. Liquid-phase epitaxy (LPE) grows near equilibrium from a metal solution and gives excellent crystal quality with simple equipment, but it is poor at very thin layers and abrupt interfaces. MBE trades throughput for control and cleanliness, making it the tool of choice for research and for devices that need atomically sharp layers.

What it is used for

MBE builds quantum wells, superlattices, modulation-doped heterostructures for high-electron-mobility transistors (HEMTs), and other quantum structures in III-V compound semiconductors. Very thin, sharply confined doping layers, known as delta doping, are another strength. Variants extend the method to other materials, such as plasma-assisted MBE that supplies active nitrogen for nitride growth. The ultra-high vacuum also lets researchers combine growth with surface analysis tools without exposing samples to air.

MBE heats source materials in ultra-high vacuum (about 10⁻⁸ Pa) to form molecular (atomic) beams that strike the substrate and build the crystal. Shutters switch sources instantly, and RHEED monitors growth in situ, enabling atomic-layer control of thickness and composition. Growing only about 1 µm per hour, it is less productive than MOCVD but serves R&D, high-performance HEMTs and quantum devices.

Answer: B. RHEED (reflection high-energy electron diffraction)

RHEED directs a 10–30 keV electron beam at a grazing angle of about 1–3° onto the growing surface and displays the diffraction pattern on a phosphor screen. It reveals surface flatness and reconstruction, and because roughness cycles with each completed monolayer, the intensity oscillates, letting operators count layers in real time. It is uniquely suited to MBE, whose ultra-high vacuum keeps the beam from scattering off gas molecules.

FAQ

What is the difference between MBE and MOCVD?

MBE evaporates elemental sources into ultra-high vacuum and grows slowly with atomic-layer control, while MOCVD uses reactive gases at much higher pressure and offers higher throughput. MBE is favored for research and precise heterostructures, MOCVD for volume production.

Why does MBE need ultra-high vacuum?

The vacuum lets source atoms travel as collision-free beams to the substrate, keeps the growing surface free of contamination, and allows in-situ electron diffraction monitoring such as RHEED.

What do RHEED oscillations mean?

Each oscillation period of the RHEED intensity corresponds to the completion of one monolayer, so counting oscillations gives the number of layers grown and the growth rate.
