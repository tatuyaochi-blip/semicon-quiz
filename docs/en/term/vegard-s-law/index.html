What is Vegard's law?

The empirical rule that the lattice constant of an alloy varies almost linearly with composition. It is used to find the composition that lattice-matches a given substrate.

Japanese name: ベガード則

Field: Compound semiconductors

The idea behind Vegard's law

Vegard's law is the empirical rule that the lattice constant of an alloy of two materials A and B equals the linear interpolation of their lattice constants by composition; for composition x, a(x) = (1 − x)·aA + x·aB. L. Vegard proposed it in 1921 for solid solutions of salts, and in semiconductors it is widely used to estimate the lattice constants of III-V alloys such as AlGaAs and InGaAs and of SiGe. It is not an exact law, and real values can deviate slightly from the straight line, so precise designs add correction terms based on measurements.

Lattice constant of SiGe

For SiGe, a straight line between silicon (about 0.543 nm) and germanium (about 0.566 nm) gives the lattice constant of Si1−xGex as about 0.543 + 0.023x nm. The two differ by about 4%, so SiGe with 20% Ge has a lattice constant about 0.8% larger than silicon. As a result, a thin SiGe layer grown on a silicon substrate shrinks in-plane to match the silicon and is under compressive strain. Measured lattice constants are known to fall slightly below the straight line, so precise work uses formulas with a quadratic correction term. Determining SiGe film composition by X-ray diffraction likewise combines this relation with a correction for strain.

Finding lattice-matched compositions

A typical use of Vegard's law is to find the composition that lattice-matches a substrate. For InxGa1−xAs, drawing a straight line between GaAs (0.565 nm) and InAs (0.606 nm) and solving for the x that matches InP (0.587 nm) gives about 0.53. The resulting In0.53Ga0.47As can be grown as thick layers with little strain and few dislocations and is used in photodetectors for optical communications and in HEMTs. Quaternary alloys such as InGaAsP and AlGaInP have two compositional degrees of freedom, so the band gap can be tuned separately while keeping the lattice match.

Band gaps are not linear

Unlike the lattice constant, an alloy's band gap often sags below the straight line as composition varies, a curvature called bowing. Band-gap calculations therefore add a quadratic term with a bowing parameter b, as in Eg(x) = (1 − x)·EgA + x·EgB − b·x(1 − x). In SiGe, the conduction-band minimum switches from silicon-like to germanium-like at some composition, so the band gap changes with a kink partway along. Note also that the lattice constant of a film strained on a substrate differs from the unstrained value given by Vegard's law.

FAQ

What is Vegard's law?

It is the empirical rule that an alloy's lattice constant equals the linear interpolation, by composition, of the lattice constants of its constituents. Estimated as a(x) = (1 − x)·aA + x·aB, it is used to find compositions that lattice-match a substrate.

How do you calculate the lattice constant of SiGe?

Interpolate by composition between about 0.543 nm for silicon and about 0.566 nm for germanium, giving roughly 0.543 + 0.023x nm for Si1−xGex. Measured values fall slightly below the straight line, so when precise values are needed, use a formula with a correction term.

Does Vegard's law apply to band gaps?

Not directly. Band gaps often sag below the straight line with composition (bowing), so they are calculated with a quadratic correction term. In some materials, such as SiGe, the conduction-band minimum also changes position partway through the composition range.
