What is CCD image sensor?

An image sensor that shifts pixel charges along like a bucket brigade and converts them to voltage at an output stage. It offered low noise and high image quality but used more power, and CMOS sensors have replaced it in most uses.

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Reading out by transferring charge

In a CCD (charge-coupled device) image sensor, the charge generated by light in each pixel is passed along from neighbor to neighbor as charge and finally converted to voltage by an amplifier at the output. Sequencing the voltages on many electrodes laid over the silicon moves the potential wells holding the charge step by step, carrying it along like a bucket brigade. Very little charge is lost in each transfer, and transfer efficiency exceeds 99.999%. The CCD was invented at Bell Labs in 1969, and its inventors later received the Nobel Prize in Physics.

Interline and frame-transfer types

CCDs come in several types depending on how charge is transferred. The interline type places a light-shielded vertical transfer register beside each column of photodiodes and, at the end of exposure, moves the charge from all pixels into the registers at once. Because every pixel ends exposure at the same time, it acts as a global shutter that does not distort moving subjects. The frame-transfer type adds a light-shielded storage area the same size as the imaging area, moves all the charge there at once, and then reads it out. With no transfer register inside the pixels, it has a high fill factor and is used in scientific measurement. In both, a horizontal register finally sends one row at a time to the output.

Image-quality strengths and weaknesses

Because a CCD converts the charge from every pixel to voltage with the same output amplifier, it has little fixed-pattern noise from pixel-to-pixel variation and gives low-noise, uniform images. On the other hand, transferring charge thousands of times requires drive voltages above 10 V and multiple clocks, so power consumption is high and readout slow. CMOS circuits are hard to put on the same chip, and a dedicated fabrication process is needed. In addition, when part of a strong light leaks into the transfer registers, vertical streaks (smear) appear, and charge overflowing from pixels spreads to their surroundings (blooming).

Contrast with CMOS image sensors and current uses

A CMOS image sensor gives each pixel its own amplifying transistor and reads out voltages row by row. It can be made in a standard CMOS process, integrates ADCs and image-processing circuits on the same or a stacked chip, and reads out quickly at low voltage and power. With pinned photodiodes and correlated double sampling greatly reducing noise as well, CMOS has replaced CCDs in most uses. As of the early 2020s, CCDs were used in some applications such as astronomy and scientific measurement, and variants include the EMCCD, which multiplies charge during transfer, and TDI (time delay integration), which accumulates exposure while shifting charge in step with a moving subject.

Answer: A. Pixel charges are transferred in sequence like a bucket brigade and converted to voltage at the output

A CCD shifts stored charge pixel to pixel like a bucket brigade by sequencing electrode voltages, converting it to voltage at a single output amplifier. Transfer efficiency exceeds 99.999%, and the single amplifier minimizes pixel-to-pixel variation, giving low noise. But CCDs need drive voltages above 10 V and dedicated processes, consume more power and read out slowly, so CMOS has replaced them except in niches such as astronomy.

FAQ

What is a CCD image sensor, in simple terms?

An image sensor that transfers the charge generated by light in each pixel like a bucket brigade by sequencing electrode voltages, then converts it to voltage at an output amplifier. Its strengths are low noise and uniform image quality, and it was once the mainstream sensor in digital cameras.

What is the difference between CCD and CMOS image sensors?

A CCD transfers charge and reads it out through the output amplifier, while a CMOS sensor converts charge to voltage with an amplifier in each pixel and reads out row by row. CMOS sensors can be made in standard CMOS processes, integrate peripheral circuits easily, use less power and are faster, so they are now mainstream.

Are CCDs still used today?

In consumer cameras they have been almost entirely replaced by CMOS, but they remain in some applications such as astronomy, scientific measurement and inspection equipment. Variants that exploit CCD strengths also exist, such as the EMCCD, which multiplies charge during transfer, and TDI, used to image moving objects.
