What is MSL (moisture sensitivity level)?

A rating of how readily a surface-mount part absorbs moisture, defined by JEDEC's J-STD-020. It sets the floor life allowed between opening the moisture barrier bag and reflow: 168 hours at or below 30 °C/60% RH for MSL 3. Parts past their floor life are baked dry before mounting.

Why absorbed moisture is dangerous

Plastic mold compound and package substrates absorb moisture from the air. During reflow the package heats within minutes to a peak of about 245–260 °C for lead-free tin-silver-copper (SAC) solder, and the absorbed water turns to vapor. The pressure can delaminate internal interfaces or crack the package, sometimes audibly, hence the name popcorn effect. The damage may be invisible from outside and show up only later as reliability failures, so it must be prevented rather than screened.

Levels and floor life

IPC/JEDEC J-STD-020 classifies parts and J-STD-033 defines their handling. Levels run from 1 to 6, stricter as the number rises. MSL 1 has unlimited floor life at 30 °C/85% RH or less. At 30 °C/60% RH or less, MSL 2 allows one year, 2a four weeks, 3 one week (168 hours), 4 72 hours, 5 48 hours and 5a 24 hours, while MSL 6 must be baked before use and reflowed within the time on its label. Floor life starts when the moisture barrier bag is opened.

Dry packing, baking and tracking

Moisture-sensitive parts ship in a sealed moisture barrier bag with desiccant and a humidity indicator card, labeled with the MSL and the peak reflow temperature. If the indicator shows too much humidity or floor life has run out, parts are baked, commonly at 125 °C for a time set by level and package thickness, or at much lower temperatures for long periods when the tape and reel cannot take 125 °C. Storage in dry cabinets can pause the floor-life clock under J-STD-033 rules.

Lead-free reflow raised the stakes

Tin-lead solder melts at 183 °C and its reflow peaked around 220–235 °C. SAC lead-free solder melts near 217–220 °C, so peaks rose to about 245–260 °C and component heat-resistance ratings were raised to 260 °C. Vapor pressure climbs steeply with temperature, so the same package can earn a worse MSL when classified at 260 °C than at 235 °C. An MSL is therefore always quoted together with the peak reflow temperature it was classified at.

Answer: A. After opening, reflow within 168 hours at 30 °C/60%RH or less; if exceeded, bake before mounting

MSL is a moisture sensitivity classification defined in joint IPC/JEDEC standards (J-STD-020 for classification, J-STD-033 for handling) that indicates the time allowed between opening the moisture barrier bag and reflow (floor life). For MSL 3, it is 168 hours (one week) at 30 °C/60%RH or less. If a part is heated in reflow after absorbing moisture, the internal moisture vaporizes suddenly and the package can crack or delaminate (the popcorn effect), so parts past their limit are dried by baking at 125 °C or similar. Only MSL 1 can be handled without a time limit, and higher numbers require stricter control.

FAQ

What does MSL 3 mean?

After the dry bag is opened, the parts must be reflowed within 168 hours (one week) at 30 °C/60% RH or less. If that floor life is exceeded, they must be baked before mounting.

What is the popcorn effect in IC packages?

Moisture absorbed by a plastic package turns to vapor during reflow heating, and the pressure cracks the package or delaminates internal interfaces. MSL control and baking prevent it.

Is MSL 1 better than MSL 3?

Yes, in handling terms. MSL 1 parts have unlimited floor life under 30 °C/85% RH, while higher numbers mean the part absorbs moisture more critically and needs tighter time control.
