What is Reverse recovery time?

The time during which reverse current keeps flowing after a conducting diode is reverse-biased, until the stored minority carriers are removed. It causes switching loss and noise.

Japanese name: 逆回復時間

Field: PN junctions and diodes

What happens during reverse recovery

When a conducting diode is driven to reverse bias, the external circuit forces its current to fall at a rate di/dt and then reverse. The junction cannot block yet because minority carriers stored on both sides keep it forward-biased internally, so reverse current flows and rises to a peak, the peak reverse recovery current. Once the carriers near the junction are depleted, the diode starts blocking and the current decays toward zero. The time from zero crossing until the current has decayed to a specified fraction is trr.

Stored charge and test conditions

The charge to be removed, Qrr, is roughly the forward current times the minority-carrier lifetime, so trr grows with forward current and with temperature, which lengthens lifetime. Faster di/dt raises the peak reverse current and changes the waveform. For this reason datasheets quote trr with conditions such as forward current, di/dt and temperature. General-purpose rectifiers recover in the order of microseconds, while fast-recovery types reach several tens of nanoseconds by adding lifetime-killing recombination centers.

Soft vs. snappy recovery

trr is often split into the time to reach peak reverse current and the decay time after it; the ratio of decay time to rise time is called softness. A snappy diode whose current drops abruptly causes a large voltage spike across circuit inductance, ringing and electromagnetic interference (EMI). A soft diode decays more gradually. Designers therefore look at Qrr and the recovery waveform, not only at trr, when choosing a freewheeling diode.

Impact in power converters and the alternatives

In a hard-switched converter, the recovery current of the freewheeling diode flows through the transistor as it turns on, adding turn-on loss and stress that grow with switching frequency. MOSFET body diodes often recover slowly, a known issue in bridge circuits. Schottky diodes, including silicon carbide (SiC) Schottky diodes, conduct with majority carriers and store almost no minority charge, so they show essentially only a small capacitive charge instead of true reverse recovery.

Answer: B. Because minority carriers stored during forward conduction do not disappear immediately

While a diode conducts forward, injected minority carriers accumulate on both sides of the junction. After switching to reverse, current keeps flowing backward until those carriers are swept out or recombine. This interval is the reverse recovery time (trr), and the reverse recovery current causes loss and noise. Remedies include fast-recovery diodes with lifetime control, and Schottky barrier diodes (SiC Schottky diodes for high voltage), which do not rely on minority carriers.

Answer: B. It varies with conditions such as forward current IF, the rate of current decrease di/dt and temperature, so read it together with the test conditions

trr is the time during which reverse current flows, after switching from forward conduction to reverse bias, until the stored minority carriers are swept out. The amount of stored charge varies with forward current and temperature, and the waveform also changes with how fast the current is switched (di/dt), so datasheets specify it with conditions such as "IF = 1 A, di/dt = 100 A/µs." It is on the order of µs for general-purpose rectifiers and several tens of ns for fast-recovery types. Schottky diodes operate with majority carriers, so they have almost no reverse recovery.

FAQ

Do Schottky diodes have reverse recovery time?

Practically none, because they conduct with majority carriers and store no minority charge; only a small junction-capacitance charge flows when they switch off.

What is the difference between trr and Qrr?

trr is the duration of the reverse recovery current, while Qrr is the total charge it carries; Qrr relates more directly to the switching loss caused in the circuit.

How can I reduce diode reverse recovery losses?

Use fast or soft-recovery diodes, Schottky or SiC Schottky diodes, lower di/dt with snubbers or gate control, or soft-switching topologies that let the diode current fall to zero before reverse voltage is applied.
