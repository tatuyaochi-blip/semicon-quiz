What is Local exhaust ventilation?

Equipment that draws in gases and mists at their source, such as tools handling chemicals, and exhausts them. It keeps workers from inhaling hazardous substances.

Japanese name: 局所排気装置

Field: Environment, health and safety

How local exhaust captures contaminants

Local exhaust ventilation (LEV) removes hazardous gases, vapors, mists and dusts at the point where they are released, before they reach a worker's breathing zone. A typical system consists of a hood or enclosure around the source, ducting, an air cleaner such as a scrubber where needed, a fan and a discharge stack. The key design quantity is capture velocity: the air speed at the emission point needed to pull contaminants into the hood against drafts and the motion of the release itself.

Local exhaust vs. general ventilation

General, or dilution, ventilation mixes clean air into the whole room to lower average concentration, so a worker standing near the source can still be exposed, and large airflows are needed. Local exhaust removes contaminants before they spread, so it is more effective for toxic substances and needs much less airflow. In a cleanroom, the large recirculating airflow is designed to control particles, not chemical exposure; chemical fumes are handled by dedicated exhausts at each tool and chemical station.

Where you meet it in a fab

Wet benches draw air through slots around their chemical baths, chemical storage and dispensing cabinets are exhausted, and gas cabinets holding toxic gases such as arsine and phosphine are kept under negative pressure so any leak is swept into the exhaust. Process tools have exhaust connections for their chambers and load areas. Grinding and polishing of GaAs, which releases arsenic-containing dust, also relies on local exhaust together with protective equipment. Exhaust is usually segregated by type, such as acid, alkali and solvent, and treated before release.

Keeping it effective

A hood only protects if it actually achieves its design airflow, so systems are inspected and tested periodically, for example by measuring capture velocity or face velocity at the hood opening, and many jurisdictions require such checks by law. Exhaust pressure is monitored continuously, and tools handling hazardous chemicals are often interlocked to stop or alarm if exhaust is lost. Practical habits matter too: not blocking hood openings, keeping sashes at the correct height and working inside the capture zone.

Answer: B. Because it contains arsenic and must be properly managed and treated as a hazardous substance

GaAs is about half arsenic by weight (about 52%), so grinding and polishing dust, waste liquids and scrapped wafers must be handled as hazardous industrial waste under regulations, with arsenic recovery. Local exhaust ventilation and protective equipment prevent worker exposure. Arsine (AsH3) gas used in manufacturing is even more toxic, requiring rigorous gas detection and abatement systems.

FAQ

What is local exhaust ventilation?

Local exhaust ventilation is a system that captures hazardous gases, vapors, mists or dust right at their source with a hood or enclosure and carries them away through ducts, so workers do not inhale them.

What is capture velocity?

Capture velocity is the air speed needed at the point where a contaminant is released to draw it into the exhaust hood. It is a basic design and inspection criterion for local exhaust systems.

What is the difference between local exhaust and general ventilation?

General ventilation dilutes contaminants throughout a room with large airflows, while local exhaust removes them at the source before they spread, giving better protection with much less air.
