What is WDM (wavelength division multiplexing)?

A method of carrying separate signals on different wavelengths of light and sending them together over a single fiber. CWDM uses wide 20 nm spacing, while DWDM packs many wavelengths at narrow spacings such as 100 GHz (about 0.8 nm).

Japanese name: WDM(波長多重)

Field: Silicon photonics

How wavelength multiplexing works

Light of different wavelengths travels through the same fiber with almost no mutual interference at normal power levels, so each wavelength can carry an independent signal. At the transmitter, several lasers at distinct wavelengths are modulated separately and combined by a multiplexer; at the receiver, a demultiplexer separates the wavelengths and sends each to its own photodetector. Capacity scales with the channel count: four wavelengths at 100 Gb/s each deliver 400 Gb/s over one fiber. This differs from time-division multiplexing, which interleaves signals in time slots on a single carrier.

CWDM versus DWDM

Coarse WDM (CWDM) spaces channels 20 nm apart, for example 1271, 1291, 1311 and 1331 nm, near the zero-dispersion region of standard single-mode fiber. A DFB laser's wavelength drifts by about 0.1 nm per degree Celsius, so tens of degrees shift it by only a few nanometers, well within the spacing; uncooled lasers keep cost and power low for data-center links. Dense WDM (DWDM) packs dozens of channels on the ITU-T frequency grid at spacings such as 100 GHz (about 0.8 nm) or 50 GHz (about 0.4 nm), so its lasers need active temperature control to stay on their channels.

Converting frequency spacing to wavelength

Because frequency and wavelength are inversely related, a frequency spacing converts to a wavelength spacing as Δλ ≈ λ² × Δf / c. At 1.55 µm, 100 GHz gives (1.55×10⁻⁶ m)² × 10¹¹ Hz ÷ 3×10⁸ m/s, or about 0.8 nm. The same frequency spacing corresponds to a slightly narrower wavelength spacing at shorter wavelengths. Long-haul DWDM uses the C-band around 1530 to 1565 nm because fiber loss is lowest there and erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) can amplify all channels at once without converting them to electrical signals.

Multiplexers, demultiplexers and silicon photonics

Common wavelength filters include thin-film interference filters, arrayed waveguide gratings (AWGs), cascaded Mach-Zehnder interferometers and ring resonators. A ring passes wavelengths whose round trip fits an integer number of wavelengths, and its free spectral range is approximately λ² / (ng × L), so shrinking the circumference widens the spacing between resonances and keeps a ring from also responding to other channels. Smaller rings suffer more bending loss, however. Silicon's refractive index changes noticeably with temperature, so silicon ring filters are usually tuned and stabilized with integrated heaters.

Answer: B. Separate signals are placed on multiple lights of different wavelengths and sent together over a single fiber

WDM (Wavelength Division Multiplexing) exploits the fact that light of different wavelengths travels through the same fiber with almost no mutual interference, placing a separate signal on each wavelength. For example, sending 100 Gb/s signals on four wavelengths delivers 400 Gb/s over a single fiber. A multiplexer is used on the transmit side and a demultiplexer on the receive side; in silicon photonics they are implemented with ring resonators, arrayed waveguide gratings (AWGs), Mach-Zehnder filters and the like. Sending signals in divided time slots is called time-division multiplexing.

In CWDM (coarse wavelength division multiplexing) for data center optical transceivers, wavelengths are spaced 20 nm apart, for example 1271, 1291, 1311 and 1331 nm. What is the main reason for the wide spacing?

  1. AWider wavelength spacing reduces fiber loss and dispersion, so light reaches farther without amplifiers
  2. BEven without control to keep the laser temperature constant, temperature-induced wavelength drift is unlikely to overlap the adjacent wavelength
  3. CMultiplexers and demultiplexers with spacing narrower than 20 nm are fundamentally impossible to build
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Even without control to keep the laser temperature constant, temperature-induced wavelength drift is unlikely to overlap the adjacent wavelength

The lasing wavelength of a semiconductor laser changes with temperature; a DFB laser shifts by about 0.1 nm per 1 °C. A temperature change of several tens of °C shifts it by several nm, so with narrow spacing it would overlap the adjacent wavelength. CWDM's wide 20 nm spacing allows lasers to be used without temperature control by Peltier elements or similar, lowering cost and power consumption. In contrast, DWDM, used for long-haul communication, packs many wavelengths at narrow spacings such as 0.8 nm (100 GHz), so temperature control is essential. Widening the spacing does not reduce fiber loss.

FAQ

What is the difference between CWDM and DWDM?

CWDM uses a few channels spaced 20 nm apart so inexpensive uncooled lasers can be used, typically for short reaches. DWDM packs many channels at spacings such as 100 GHz (about 0.8 nm) or 50 GHz, needs temperature-stabilized lasers and is used for long-haul, high-capacity links.

How do you convert DWDM channel spacing from GHz to nm?

Use Δλ ≈ λ² × Δf / c. Around 1550 nm, 100 GHz corresponds to about 0.8 nm and 50 GHz to about 0.4 nm.

Why is DWDM used in the 1550 nm band?

Silica fiber has its lowest loss near 1550 nm, and erbium-doped fiber amplifiers work in this C-band, amplifying every wavelength channel simultaneously. Together these allow long spans with many channels.
