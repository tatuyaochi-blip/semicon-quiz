What is Moore's law?

The empirical rule that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles about every two years. It has served as a target for semiconductor scaling.

Japanese name: ムーアの法則

What Moore actually observed

In 1965 Gordon Moore noted that the number of components on the most cost-effective integrated circuit was doubling about every year, and in 1975 he revised the pace to about every two years. It is an empirical observation about technology and economics, not a law of physics. The frequently quoted 18-month figure is not Moore's own and usually refers to performance rather than transistor count. Because the whole industry planned equipment, materials and design tools around this cadence, it worked partly as a self-fulfilling target.

Moore's law vs. Dennard scaling

Moore's law concerns how many transistors fit on a chip. Dennard scaling, described in 1974, explained why shrinking was so beneficial: if dimensions and supply voltage scale down by the same factor, power density stays constant while circuits get faster. Around the mid-2000s supply and threshold voltages stopped scaling, because the subthreshold swing of about 60 mV per decade at room temperature makes leakage explode at low thresholds. Clock frequencies plateaued and designers turned to multiple cores and power management, while transistor counts kept rising.

How density kept increasing

Keeping pace required repeated reinvention. Lithography moved to shorter wavelengths, immersion optics, multiple patterning and extreme ultraviolet (EUV). Transistors gained strained silicon, high-k dielectrics with metal gates, then FinFETs and gate-all-around nanosheets to keep the channel under gate control. Layout tricks such as fewer tracks per standard cell, together with 3D stacking and chiplets, add density beyond pure shrinking. Modern node names such as 5 nm or 3 nm are generation labels and no longer equal any physical gate length.

Cost per transistor and the slowdown

The underlying goal of scaling is better PPA, meaning performance, power and area, plus lower cost per transistor. For decades each node delivered all of these. Today, rising fab, mask and lithography costs and the added process steps of multiple patterning have slowed the fall in cost per transistor, and the doubling interval has stretched. The industry still advances density, but gains increasingly come from packaging, specialization and design rather than from shrinking alone.

Moore's law is the empirical observation that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles at regular intervals. Gordon Moore proposed annual doubling in 1965 and revised it to about every two years in 1975. It is not a law of physics but has served as the industry's development target. It does not say prices halve every year or frequency rises tenfold in 18 months. The pace of doubling is said to have slowed recently.

FAQ

Is Moore's law a law of physics?

No. It is an empirical observation and industry roadmap target describing how transistor counts have grown, roughly doubling every two years, driven by engineering and economics.

Is Moore's law dead?

Transistor density is still increasing, but more slowly and at higher cost per wafer, and the old link to lower cost per transistor and higher clock speed has weakened. Much of the progress now comes from new device structures, 3D integration and chiplets.

What is the difference between Moore's law and Dennard scaling?

Moore's law describes the growth in transistor count per chip. Dennard scaling described how shrinking transistors along with voltage kept power density constant, and it broke down when voltage stopped scaling.
