What is TDP (thermal design power)?

A guide to the heat a CPU or similar chip generates under a typical heavy load. Cooling systems are designed around it.

Japanese name: TDP(熱設計電力)

TDP (thermal design power) appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Power Management, CPUs and architecture.

Field: CPUs and architecture

What TDP actually specifies

Thermal design power (TDP) is a cooling target in watts: the heat that a cooler must be able to remove continuously, under a sustained heavy workload defined by the vendor, while keeping the chip within its temperature limits. It is not the maximum electrical power the chip can draw, and definitions differ between vendors. Some derive it from a target case temperature, an ambient temperature and the thermal resistance of a reference cooler. TDP is best treated as a design guide for heat sinks and fans rather than a power measurement.

Why chips run above it for a while

The die, heat spreader and heat sink all have heat capacity, so after a step in power the temperature climbs over seconds to tens of seconds rather than instantly. A processor can therefore draw more than the cooler's steady-state capacity for a short time without exceeding its temperature limit. Modern CPUs exploit this with two limits: a long-term average power close to TDP and a higher short-term limit, which Intel, for example, calls PL1 and PL2. If the heavy load continues, power falls back to the long-term limit.

Thermal resistance arithmetic

Steady-state junction temperature is approximately ambient temperature plus power times the total thermal resistance from junction to ambient. Suppose a 125 W chip must stay below 100 °C inside a case where the air reaches 35 °C. The allowed temperature rise is 65 °C, so the whole path, including the thermal interface material, heat spreader and cooler, must have a thermal resistance no higher than about 0.52 °C/W. This simple calculation shows why a higher TDP demands a disproportionately larger or better cooler.

TDP vs. other power figures

Because boost behavior lets real power exceed TDP, vendors increasingly specify a base or sustained power and a maximum turbo power separately. Power-delivery designers need yet another figure: the peak current the voltage regulators must supply during fast load transients, which can far exceed what the average power suggests. Laptop processors often support configurable TDP, letting the system maker trade performance for cooler size. Graphics cards are commonly rated by total board power, which includes memory and voltage-regulator losses as well as the chip.

Answer: A. Chips and coolers have heat capacity, so temperature does not rise instantly and limits can be respected under brief high power

Heat goes into raising the temperature of the die, heat spreader and heat sink, and reaching steady-state temperature takes seconds to tens of seconds. So for short periods, power above the cooling capacity can be used without hitting the temperature limit. CPUs have a long-term average power limit and a higher short-term limit, speeding up brief tasks such as responding to user input. If the load persists, power falls back to the long-term limit.

FAQ

Is TDP the maximum power a CPU uses?

No. TDP is a sustained cooling target, and modern CPUs routinely draw more for short boost periods when temperature and power limits allow.

Why does my CPU draw more power than its TDP?

It is running under a short-term power limit that is higher than the long-term limit near TDP, using the thermal mass of the chip and cooler as a buffer. Some motherboards also raise or remove these limits by default.

Can I use a cooler rated for less than my CPU's TDP?

The chip will usually protect itself by throttling, but it will lose performance under sustained load. For full sustained performance, the cooler should handle at least the long-term power limit.
