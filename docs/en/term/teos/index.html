What is TEOS?

Short for tetraethoxysilane, a liquid organosilicon compound widely used as the source for CVD silicon oxide. It gives oxide films with good step coverage.

Japanese name: TEOS

Field: Deposition (CVD and PVD)

Chemistry of the precursor

Tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), Si(OC2H5)4, is a liquid at room temperature with a boiling point of about 168 °C. Each silicon atom is already bonded to four oxygen atoms, so silicon dioxide can form when the ethoxy groups decompose and leave as volatile organic byproducts, with oxygen or ozone added to help the reaction and burn off carbon. Unlike silane (SiH4), which is a pyrophoric gas, TEOS is comparatively safe to store and handle. The name is so common that the deposited film itself is often called TEOS oxide, or just TEOS.

Why step coverage is good

Oxide from TEOS forms mainly through reactions on the surface, and the reactive intermediates have a low sticking probability, so they bounce around before reacting and reach sidewalls and the bottoms of trenches. The result is a conformal film whose thickness is similar on top, side and bottom surfaces. Oxide deposited from silane and oxygen at low temperature, by contrast, involves species that stick where they first land, giving thicker deposits at upper corners and poorer coverage. This difference is the main reason TEOS became the standard source for CVD oxide.

Deposition variants

Low-pressure CVD (LPCVD) TEOS runs in a furnace at about 700 °C and gives conformal, good-quality oxide, but that temperature rules it out once metal is present, so it is used in the front end, for example for sidewall spacers. Plasma-enhanced CVD (PECVD) TEOS deposits at about 400 °C for interlayer dielectrics in the back end. TEOS with ozone at atmospheric or sub-atmospheric pressure gives flowing, gap-filling films. Adding boron and phosphorus sources produces doped oxides such as borophosphosilicate glass, which soften and reflow when heated.

Film quality and delivery

TEOS oxide is less dense than thermal oxide and can contain hydrogen, moisture or residual carbon depending on conditions, so it typically etches faster in dilute hydrofluoric acid; annealing densifies it toward thermal-oxide behavior. As a liquid, the precursor reaches the chamber either by bubbling a carrier gas through a temperature-controlled container or by metering the liquid into a vaporizer, which gives tighter flow control. Delivery lines are heated so the vapor does not recondense before it reaches the reactor.

Liquid precursors are delivered as gas either by bubbling, in which carrier gas passes through a temperature-controlled container and carries vapor, or by metering the liquid and flash-vaporizing it. Bubbling supply fluctuates with liquid temperature, while vaporizer systems give tighter control. TEOS is a liquid boiling at about 168 °C, so lines are heated to prevent recondensation. Pouring liquid into the reactor would not yield uniform films.

FAQ

What does TEOS stand for?

TEOS stands for tetraethoxysilane, also called tetraethyl orthosilicate, with the formula Si(OC2H5)4. It is the most common liquid precursor for CVD silicon dioxide.

What is the difference between TEOS oxide and silane oxide?

TEOS oxide forms through surface reactions with low sticking intermediates, so it is more conformal; silane-based oxide tends to deposit more at the top of features. Silane is also a pyrophoric gas, whereas TEOS is a safer liquid.

Is TEOS oxide the same as thermal oxide?

Both are silicon dioxide, but thermal oxide is grown by consuming silicon and is denser and cleaner. Deposited TEOS oxide usually etches faster in HF and is used where oxide must be added on top of other layers.
