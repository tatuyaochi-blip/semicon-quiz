What is Effective mass?

An apparent mass used to treat electrons and holes in a crystal as if they were free particles responding to external forces. It is set by the curvature of the band; a smaller effective mass tends to give higher mobility.

Japanese name: 有効質量

Where effective mass comes from

An electron in a crystal moves through the periodic potential of the atoms, so its response to an applied force differs from that of a free electron. Band theory absorbs this into the energy–wavevector relation E(k). Near a band edge E is roughly parabolic, E ≈ ħ²k²/2m*, and the effective mass is defined by the curvature, m* = ħ² / (d²E/dk²). A sharply curved band gives a light carrier and a flat band a heavy one. The electron's actual rest mass does not change; m* is simply the parameter that makes Newton's law F = m*a work inside the crystal.

Effective mass and mobility

Mobility is roughly μ = qτ/m*, where τ is the mean time between scattering events, so lighter carriers accelerate more between collisions. In lightly doped silicon at room temperature, electron mobility is about 1400 cm²/V·s and hole mobility about 450 cm²/V·s, mainly because holes are heavier. This is why a CMOS pMOS is commonly drawn 2–3 times wider than its nMOS partner to match drive current. GaAs electrons, with m* of about 0.067 times the free-electron mass, reach about 8500 cm²/V·s, which suits high-frequency amplifiers.

Holes, valleys and direction dependence

Near the top of the valence band the curvature is negative, so an electron there would have negative effective mass; treating the missing electron as a hole with positive charge and positive mass restores intuitive behavior, and the sign of the Hall voltage confirms it. Effective mass can also depend on direction. Silicon's conduction band has six equivalent valleys, each with a heavy longitudinal mass of roughly 0.9–1.0 m0 and a light transverse mass of about 0.19 m0. In GaAs, a strong field can push electrons into the higher, heavier L valley, the transferred-electron effect behind the Gunn diode.

Engineering effective mass with strain

Because effective mass comes from band structure, changing the lattice changes it. Tensile strain in silicon, for example from a thin layer grown on relaxed SiGe, splits the six conduction valleys so electrons crowd into those offering the light transverse mass in the transport direction, while intervalley scattering drops; electron mobility can rise by roughly 1.5–2 times. For pMOS, compressive strain from embedded SiGe source/drain regions lightens the holes. SiGe and germanium channels are pursued because their hole effective mass is intrinsically lower than silicon's.

Answer: A. Electron mobility is higher

In silicon at room temperature and low doping, electron mobility is about 1400 cm²/V·s and hole mobility about 450 cm²/V·s, so electrons are about three times faster, mainly because holes have a larger effective mass. An nMOS of the same size therefore carries more current, and in CMOS the pMOS is typically made 2–3 times wider than the nMOS to balance drive strength. Advanced processes boost pMOS mobility with compressive strain from SiGe.

What is the mass introduced so that the motion of an electron in a crystal can be treated like that of a free electron in response to external forces?

  1. ARest mass
  2. BAtomic mass
  3. CEffective mass
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Effective mass

Electrons in a crystal feel the periodic potential of the atoms, so they respond to forces differently from electrons in vacuum. The effective mass lumps this effect into a different mass and is set by the band curvature (sharper curvature means lighter). A smaller effective mass gives higher mobility, which is why electrons in GaAs (about 0.067 times the free-electron mass) are faster than in silicon. Holes have lower mobility because their effective mass is larger.

FAQ

Is effective mass the real mass of an electron?

No. The electron's rest mass is unchanged; effective mass is a parameter derived from band curvature that lets you use F = ma for a carrier inside the crystal's periodic potential.

Why do holes have lower mobility than electrons in silicon?

Holes in silicon have a larger effective mass than conduction-band electrons, so the same field accelerates them less between scattering events. The result is roughly a threefold mobility gap at room temperature.

Can effective mass be negative?

Yes, near the top of a band the curvature is negative, so an electron there would have negative effective mass. Physicists describe those states with holes of positive mass and positive charge instead.
