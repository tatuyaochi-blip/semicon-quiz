What is Solid solubility?

The maximum concentration of an impurity that can dissolve in a crystal at a given temperature. It sets the upper limit of electrically active dopant concentration.

Japanese name: 固溶度

Solid solubility appears in 3 practice questions across 1 topic, including Ion implantation.

Field: Ion implantation

An equilibrium limit that depends on temperature

Solid solubility is the largest concentration of an impurity that a crystal can hold in equilibrium at a given temperature without forming a second phase. For dopants in silicon it varies with temperature, typically reaching its maximum in the range of roughly 1000-1300 °C and falling at lower temperatures. Near 1000 °C, boron's solubility is about 2e20 cm⁻³, while arsenic and phosphorus reach around 1e21 cm⁻³. Compared with silicon's atomic density of 5e22 cm⁻³, these limits correspond to at most about 2 atomic percent, and only about 0.4 percent for boron.

Total versus electrically active dopant

Dopant above the solubility limit does not sit on lattice sites as a donor or acceptor; it forms clusters or precipitates and contributes no carriers. Implanted dose can easily exceed solubility, so the total concentration measured by secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) can be much higher than the active carrier concentration measured electrically. This is why sheet resistance falls with dose at first and then saturates: additional dopant stays inactive, and mobility also drops at high doping because of ionized-impurity scattering.

Going beyond it: metastable activation

Very fast thermal processes can trap dopants on substitutional sites before they have time to cluster. Millisecond flash and laser anneals, and solid-phase epitaxial regrowth of a layer amorphized by implantation, can activate concentrations above the equilibrium solubility. These states are metastable: later thermal steps at moderate temperature give the dopants time to deactivate by clustering, so the whole downstream thermal budget has to be considered when relying on above-solubility activation.

Why it matters for contacts

Source/drain regions and contact areas are doped to around 1e20 cm⁻³ or higher, which is close to solid solubility for boron. Specific contact resistivity between a silicide or metal and silicon falls steeply as active doping rises, because the Schottky barrier becomes thin enough for carriers to tunnel through it. At small contact areas, contact resistance becomes a large part of total transistor series resistance, so maximizing active dopant concentration at the interface is a recurring target in junction engineering.

Answer: A. It decreases

Higher dose means more dopant and carriers in the implanted layer, so sheet resistance falls—roughly 1/(q × active dose × average mobility), around 100 Ω/□ for 10¹⁵ cm⁻². Beyond the solid solubility, however, much of the extra dopant stays inactive and mobility declines, so further dose increases yield diminishing reductions in sheet resistance.

Answer: A. Solid solubility

Solid solubility is the maximum dopant concentration a crystal can dissolve in equilibrium—about 2×10²⁰ cm⁻³ for boron in silicon near 1000 °C and around 10²¹ cm⁻³ for arsenic and phosphorus. Excess dopant beyond it precipitates or clusters and stays electrically inactive. Millisecond anneals and solid-phase epitaxial regrowth quench before equilibrium is reached, achieving metastable activation above solubility.

FAQ

What is the solid solubility of boron in silicon?

It is about 2e20 cm⁻³ near 1000 °C. Phosphorus and arsenic have higher limits, on the order of 1e21 cm⁻³.

Why does sheet resistance stop decreasing at high implant dose?

Once the dopant concentration exceeds solid solubility, the extra atoms cluster or precipitate and remain electrically inactive, and mobility also declines at high doping. Additional dose therefore gives diminishing returns.

Can active dopant concentration exceed solid solubility?

Yes, temporarily. Millisecond anneals and solid-phase epitaxial regrowth can freeze in metastable concentrations above solubility, but later heating can deactivate them.
