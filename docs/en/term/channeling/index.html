What is Channeling?

A phenomenon in which ions pass through gaps between rows of crystal atoms and penetrate deeper than expected. It is prevented by tilting the wafer during implantation and similar measures.

Japanese name: チャネリング

Channeling appears in 3 practice questions across 1 topic, including Ion implantation.

Field: Ion implantation

Why ions channel

A single crystal viewed along a low-index direction, such as the silicon ⟨110⟩ or ⟨100⟩ axis, shows open corridors between rows of atoms. An ion entering nearly parallel to such a corridor is gently steered by a series of glancing collisions with the atomic rows instead of suffering hard, large-angle nuclear collisions. It therefore loses energy mainly through weaker electronic stopping and travels far deeper than an ion in an amorphous target. Channeling happens only within a small critical angle of the crystal axis, and that angle shrinks at higher ion energy, so precise beam-to-crystal alignment is what makes it likely.

Effect on the implant profile

Without channeling, an implanted depth profile is roughly Gaussian, with its peak at the projected range Rp and a width set by the straggle ΔRp; the peak concentration is about dose divided by 2.5 × ΔRp. Channeled ions add a long tail extending well beyond this peak, making the profile asymmetric and pushing the junction deeper than designed. The effect is most troublesome for light ions such as boron and for low-energy implants intended to form shallow junctions, where the tail can dominate the junction depth. Simulators therefore use more elaborate distribution functions than a simple Gaussian.

How channeling is suppressed

The standard countermeasure is to tilt the wafer about 7° from the beam axis and add a twist rotation, so the beam does not line up with any major crystal axis or plane. A thin screen oxide on the surface scatters ions into random directions before they enter the silicon, which also protects the surface from contamination. Pre-amorphization, implanting a heavy species such as germanium or silicon first, destroys the crystal order near the surface so there are no channels to follow. High-dose implants of heavy ions partly amorphize the surface by themselves, which reduces channeling for the later part of the dose.

The tilt trade-off: shadowing

Tilting the wafer creates a new problem. Tall features such as resist masks or gate stacks block the angled beam on one side, leaving a strip near their edge unimplanted, an effect called shadowing. The doped region becomes asymmetric, so source-side and drain-side characteristics can differ. Common remedies are implanting in four steps with the wafer rotated by 90° between them (quad mode), and choosing resist height and tilt angle together.

Answer: C. Tilting the wafer a few degrees from the crystal axis during implantation

Ions entering along crystal rows travel through open channels between atomic columns with few collisions and penetrate deeper than intended—channeling. It is suppressed by tilting the wafer about 7° from the crystal axis with a twist offset, implanting through a thin screen oxide that randomizes directions, or pre-amorphizing the surface. This trims the deep tail of the profile, giving shallower junctions.

FAQ

Why is ion implantation done at a 7 degree tilt?

A tilt of around 7°, combined with a twist rotation, keeps the ion beam away from the major crystal axes and planes of silicon, so ions see a more random arrangement of atoms and do not channel deep into the crystal.

Does channeling occur in amorphous silicon or oxide?

No. Channeling requires ordered rows of atoms, so amorphous layers such as screen oxides or pre-amorphized silicon randomize ion directions; that is exactly why they are used to suppress it.

What is the difference between channeling and shadowing?

Channeling makes ions go too deep because they travel along open crystal directions; shadowing leaves areas undoped because tall features block a tilted beam. Tilting the wafer fixes the first but can cause the second.
