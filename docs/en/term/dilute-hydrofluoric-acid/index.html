What is Dilute hydrofluoric acid (DHF)?

Hydrofluoric acid diluted with water. It removes native oxide from silicon and leaves a hydrophobic, hydrogen-terminated surface.

Japanese name: 希フッ酸(DHF)

Field: CMP and cleaning

What the HF dip does

Hydrofluoric acid dissolves silicon dioxide, converting it to soluble fluorosilicic acid and water, while barely attacking silicon itself. Dilute HF (DHF), typically 0.5-1 percent, strips the roughly 1 nm native oxide from silicon in tens of seconds. Concentrations are often written as volume ratios of water to concentrated 49 percent HF, such as 50:1 or 100:1. Because the etch stops once the oxide is gone, the dip is a gentle and selective way to expose bare silicon, though prolonged exposure still thins any oxide layers present elsewhere on the wafer.

A hydrogen-terminated, hydrophobic surface

After the oxide is removed, the silicon surface is terminated with Si-H bonds and becomes hydrophobic: water beads up and runs off, a quick visual check that the oxide is gone. This passivation resists reoxidation in air for tens of minutes to hours, but a hydrophobic surface also attracts particles and is prone to watermarks during drying. Fabs therefore limit the queue time (Q-time) between the HF step and the next high-temperature process such as gate oxidation, epitaxy or silicide formation, and use carefully controlled drying.

Place in the cleaning sequence

In RCA-type cleaning, SC-1 (ammonia and hydrogen peroxide) removes particles and organics and SC-2 (hydrochloric acid and hydrogen peroxide) removes metals; both leave a thin chemical oxide that makes the surface hydrophilic. DHF may be inserted between steps to remove that oxide along with contaminants trapped in it, or used last, called an HF-last clean, when the following step needs a bare silicon surface. Buffered HF, which adds ammonium fluoride to stabilize the etch rate, is preferred for patterned oxide etching.

Etch rate as a film-quality probe

Thermal oxide is dense and nearly defect-free, so it etches slowly in dilute HF, only a few nanometers per minute in 1 percent HF. Deposited oxides that are less dense or contain hydrogen and moisture etch faster. The wet etch rate ratio relative to thermal oxide is therefore a simple index of film quality, and annealing a CVD oxide lowers its ratio toward 1. Silicon nitride etches far more slowly than oxide in HF, which allows oxide to be removed selectively from nitride.

Answer: A. Thermal oxide etches more slowly (it is denser)

Thermal oxide is dense and nearly defect-free, so it etches slowly in dilute HF (for example, 1%)—only a few nm per minute. CVD oxides, depending on deposition conditions, may be less dense or contain hydrogen and moisture, so they usually etch faster. The wet etch rate ratio relative to thermal oxide (WERR) is used as an index of film quality, and annealing densifies CVD oxide toward thermal-oxide behavior.

Answer: C. Removing native oxide

HF dissolves SiO2, so tens of seconds in 0.5–1% dilute HF strips the roughly 1 nm native oxide from silicon. The cleaned surface is hydrogen-terminated and hydrophobic—water beading confirms removal. The treatment is often performed just before gate oxidation, epitaxy or silicide formation. SC-1 removes organics and particles, and SC-2 removes metals.

FAQ

Why is silicon hydrophobic after an HF dip?

HF removes the oxide and leaves the surface covered with Si-H bonds, which do not form hydrogen bonds with water. Water therefore beads up instead of wetting the surface.

What is the difference between DHF and BHF?

DHF is hydrofluoric acid simply diluted with water, used mainly for cleaning and native oxide removal. BHF adds ammonium fluoride as a buffer, giving a more stable etch rate for patterned oxide etching.

How long does an HF-last silicon surface stay oxide-free?

The hydrogen-terminated surface resists regrowth of native oxide for roughly tens of minutes to hours in clean air, which is why fabs set queue-time limits before the next process step.
