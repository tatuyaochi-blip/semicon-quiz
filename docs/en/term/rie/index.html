What is RIE (reactive ion etching)?

Dry etching that combines reactive species in a plasma with ions accelerated by an electric field. It enables vertical, anisotropic etching.

Japanese name: RIE(反応性イオンエッチング)

Field: Etching

Ions and radicals working together

A reactive-gas plasma supplies two ingredients: neutral radicals, such as fluorine or chlorine atoms, that react chemically with the film, and ions that bombard the surface. Neither alone is efficient. In the classic experiment by Coburn and Winters, XeF2 gas or an argon ion beam each etched silicon slowly, but together they etched about ten times faster than the sum. Ion impact disorders the surface and helps volatile products desorb, so surfaces struck by ions etch much faster than those that are not.

Where the directionality comes from

In a classic parallel-plate RIE tool, radio-frequency power, commonly at 13.56 MHz, is applied to the electrode holding the wafer. Electrons respond faster than ions, so the wafer develops a negative DC self-bias and a sheath forms above it. Ions crossing the sheath are accelerated nearly perpendicular to the wafer and hit feature bottoms, not sidewalls. Many chemistries also deposit a thin passivation film, such as fluorocarbon polymer, on sidewalls, which blocks lateral etching and further improves anisotropy.

Process knobs and trade-offs

Lower pressure lengthens the ion mean free path and makes ion bombardment more directional, while higher ion energy speeds etching but reduces selectivity to masks and underlayers and can damage devices. In a simple RIE reactor the same RF power sets both plasma density and ion energy, so high-density sources such as inductively coupled plasma (ICP) add a separate bias supply to control them independently. Narrow, deep features also etch more slowly than wide ones, an effect called RIE lag or aspect-ratio-dependent etching.

RIE vs. wet etching and ion milling

Wet etching is mostly isotropic, undercutting the mask by roughly the etch depth, which rules it out for fine vertical patterns. Ion milling is purely physical sputtering: directional but slow and poorly selective. RIE combines the selectivity of chemistry with the directionality of ions. Typical chemistries are fluorocarbons for oxide, chlorine or HBr for silicon and polysilicon, chlorine-based gases for aluminum, and oxygen for resist. Deep silicon etching uses the Bosch process, alternating SF6 etch and C4F8 passivation steps.

Answer: A. Etching that combines chemical reactions and ion bombardment in a plasma of reactive gases

Reactive ion etching (RIE) combines chemical reactions driven by radicals from a reactive-gas plasma (fluorine- or chlorine-based, etc.) with bombardment by ions accelerated in an electric field. Ions striking perpendicular to the wafer promote reactions at the feature bottom, so etching proceeds vertically with high anisotropy. Despite the name, ions do not etch alone; the synergy with radicals is the essence.

FAQ

Is reactive ion etching isotropic or anisotropic?

It is primarily anisotropic: ions accelerated perpendicular to the wafer drive etching at feature bottoms, and sidewall passivation limits lateral attack. Radical-only conditions can add an isotropic component.

What gases are used in RIE?

It depends on the film: fluorocarbons such as CF4, CHF3 or C4F8 for silicon oxide, Cl2 or HBr for silicon, chlorine-based mixtures for aluminum, SF6 for fast silicon etching and O2 for photoresist.

What is the difference between RIE and ICP etching?

ICP is a type of RIE that uses an inductively coupled source to make a dense plasma, with a separate wafer bias to set ion energy, giving independent control that a simple parallel-plate RIE lacks.
