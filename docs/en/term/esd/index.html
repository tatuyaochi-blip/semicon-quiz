What is ESD (electrostatic discharge)?

A sudden discharge of static electricity from a charged person or object. Applied to an IC pin it can destroy gate oxides and other structures, so I/O pins have protection circuits.

Japanese name: ESD(静電気放電)

Field: Reliability

Why ICs are vulnerable

A person or object can easily charge to thousands of volts, while a gate dielectric a few nanometres thick breaks down at around 10 MV/cm, only a few volts for a 2 nm film. An electrostatic discharge into a pin can therefore rupture gate oxides instantly, and the peak current can melt junctions or metal lines. Not every hit kills a part outright: some leave latent damage, such as increased leakage, that fails later in the field. Factories therefore use grounded wrist straps, conductive floors and work surfaces, and ionizers.

On-chip protection

Each I/O pin has diodes to the supply and ground rails, which turn on when the pin is driven beyond the rails and steer the discharge current away from internal circuits. A power clamp between the rails, often a large transistor triggered by the fast edge of an ESD event, completes the current path. Protection must trigger above the normal operating voltage but below the level that damages the circuit it guards. Protection devices add capacitance, so high-speed I/O keeps them small. A 5 V tolerant input omits the diode to VDD so a higher input voltage does not flow into the supply.

Test models and ratings

Component ratings describe handling in ESD-controlled factories. The human body model (HBM), a 100 pF capacitor discharged through 1.5 kΩ, represents a charged person touching a pin; about 2 kV is a common target. The charged device model (CDM) represents a charged IC discharging when a pin touches metal, with events around a nanosecond or shorter, and is the main concern in automated handling. A datasheet rating such as HBM ±2 kV and CDM ±500 V does not cover finished equipment, where system-level tests like IEC 61000-4-2 apply ±8 kV contact discharges and board-level TVS diodes are needed.

ESD vs. latch-up

The two are often tested together, as in automotive AEC-Q100 qualification, but they are different failures. ESD is a brief, high-energy transient that damages structures directly. Latch-up is a sustained high-current state in which parasitic pnp and npn transistors in CMOS form a thyristor and turn each other on, triggered by current injection or overvoltage on a pin. Once latched, current keeps flowing until power is removed and can overheat the chip. Higher temperature raises the parasitic gains and well resistance, so latch-up testing is done at maximum operating temperature.

Answer: A. To divert excessive voltages such as static discharge to the supply or ground and protect internal circuits

Electrostatic discharge (ESD) from people or equipment can reach thousands of volts, while gate oxides a few nm thick fail at a few volts. Diodes between each I/O pin and the supply and ground rails conduct when an excessive voltage appears, diverting the current and protecting the internal circuitry. IC robustness was traditionally targeted at 2 kV under the human body model (HBM), but about 1 kV is increasingly used as the target, assuming ESD-controlled factories. Because protection capacitance degrades fast signals, it is kept small on high-speed I/O.

Answer: B. Component-level tests assume handling during manufacturing and assembly, while system-level tests assume cases such as a person touching the equipment in use, with much higher discharge energy

HBM (human body model) and CDM (charged device model) are tests that assume static electricity when ICs are handled in controlled factories; a rating of around 2 kV in the human body model, for example, is one common target. System-level tests, which assume discharges such as from a person touching a door or switch, use high voltages from a few kV to more than 10 kV (up to about 25 kV for air discharge), with much higher discharge energy. The IC's internal protection circuits alone often cannot withstand this, so protection components such as TVS diodes are added on the board at externally connected pins.

FAQ

What is the difference between HBM and CDM ESD?

HBM models a charged person discharging into a pin through 1.5 kΩ from 100 pF. CDM models the IC itself being charged and discharging very quickly when a pin touches metal, which is the dominant risk in automated assembly.

Is an IC's HBM rating enough to pass system-level ESD tests?

No. HBM and CDM ratings cover factory handling, while system-level tests such as IEC 61000-4-2 deliver much more energy. Pins exposed to the outside world normally need external protection such as TVS diodes.

What is the difference between ESD and latch-up?

ESD is a brief static discharge that destroys oxides or junctions directly. Latch-up is a parasitic thyristor in CMOS turning on and holding a large current until power is cycled.
