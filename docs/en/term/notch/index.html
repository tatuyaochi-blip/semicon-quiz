What is Notch?

A small cut in a wafer's edge that indicates its crystal orientation. Used on 200 mm and larger wafers; smaller wafers used an orientation flat (a straight edge) instead.

Japanese name: ウエハのノッチ

Field: Silicon wafers

What the notch marks

The notch is a small V-shaped cut, roughly 1 mm deep, ground into the wafer edge at a position tied to the crystal lattice. On common (100) silicon wafers it usually points along a ⟨110⟩ direction, although wafers with other notch orientations can be specified. Prealigners in lithography, inspection and other tools spin the wafer, detect the notch optically and rotate it to a fixed angle, so every layer is printed with the same orientation relative to the crystal and to the previous layers.

Why crystal orientation matters

Many properties depend on direction within the crystal. Anisotropic wet etchants such as KOH attack (100) planes much faster than {111} planes, so cavities on a (100) wafer are bounded by sidewalls at 54.7° that line up with ⟨110⟩ directions. Carrier mobility, and the benefit of stress engineering, depend on the channel direction of a transistor. (100) wafers also break most easily along lines parallel to ⟨110⟩ directions, so dicing lanes and test structures are laid out parallel or perpendicular to the notch direction.

Notch vs. orientation flat

Wafers of 150 mm and smaller carry an orientation flat, a straight segment ground along part of the edge, often with a shorter secondary flat whose position encodes conductivity type and surface orientation. On 200 mm wafers notches became common, and 300 mm wafers use a notch. A notch removes far less usable area than a flat and keeps the wafer nearly circular, which helps uniformity in spin, polish and thermal steps. Information once encoded by secondary flats is carried by a laser-scribed identification mark instead.

Other notches: package index marks

The word notch also appears on IC packages, where a notch, dimple, dot or chamfer serves as the index mark for pin 1. On packages such as QFP and SOP, pin numbers then increase counterclockwise when viewed from the top. Datasheet pin diagrams may be drawn as top or bottom views, and confusing the two produces a mirror-image footprint. Ball grid arrays use row letters and column numbers instead, with A1 at the marked corner.

Answer: A. To indicate the reference crystal orientation

A notch is a V-shaped cut about 1 mm deep on the wafer edge marking the crystal orientation (for example, the <110> direction). Tools detect it to align wafers so patterns are oriented correctly relative to the crystal, which matters because etch rates and mobility depend on orientation. Wafers of 150 mm and below used a flat ground along part of the edge (an orientation flat); notches replaced flats to waste less area.

FAQ

Why do 300 mm wafers have a notch instead of a flat?

A notch wastes much less of the wafer area than a flat and keeps the edge nearly circular, while still giving tools a precise reference for crystal orientation.

Which crystal direction does the wafer notch indicate?

On standard (100) silicon wafers it typically marks a ⟨110⟩ direction, but the notch orientation is part of the wafer specification and other orientations are available.

How do tools find the wafer notch?

A prealigner rotates the wafer and senses the edge optically; the notch shows up as a sharp dip in the edge profile, and the wafer is then rotated to the required angle.
