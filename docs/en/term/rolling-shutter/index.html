What is Rolling shutter?

A readout method that exposes and reads pixel rows one after another. It is simple, but fast-moving subjects appear skewed. Exposing all pixels at once is a global shutter.

Japanese name: ローリングシャッター

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Exposing rows one after another

A rolling shutter resets and starts exposing the pixel rows from top to bottom, then reads them out in the same order after a set exposure time. Every row is exposed for the same length of time, but the start of exposure shifts slightly from row to row. The shift per row is set by the time to read one row, and the top and bottom of the frame end up captured several to tens of milliseconds apart. Because the readout circuits are arranged per column and can handle only one row at a time, staggering exposure to match the readout order is the natural approach. The pixel needs no charge-storage element, and many CMOS image sensors use this method.

Motion distortion and banding

Because each row is captured at a different moment, motion of the subject or camera causes characteristic distortion. A car moving sideways, or utility poles seen from a train window, appear slanted, and a spinning propeller comes out bent. Handheld shooting can make the frame wobble like jelly from small vibrations, the so-called jello effect. Under light sources whose brightness varies periodically, such as fluorescent or LED lighting, each row receives a different amount of light, producing horizontal bands (flicker). A short burst of light such as a camera flash may brighten only some of the rows.

Reducing the distortion

The amount of distortion is set by the time needed to read out the whole frame, so faster readout is the basic remedy. Sensors convert in parallel with column ADCs, or read out quickly using a stacked design with signal-processing circuits on a lower chip. Digital cameras may combine the sensor with a mechanical shutter to define the exposure, or estimate motion after capture and correct the image. For lighting flicker, the exposure time is set to an integer multiple of the brightness-variation period; where mains power is 50 Hz, lights flicker 100 times a second, so multiples of 1/100 s are used. When moving objects must be captured accurately, a global shutter is the choice.

Why rolling shutters are so widely used

Despite the distortion, rolling shutters are used in many CMOS image sensors, including those in smartphones, digital cameras and security cameras. Without an in-pixel charge-storage element, the photodiode can take up more of a given pixel area, which helps sensitivity and full-well capacity. In a four-transistor pixel, correlated double sampling (CDS), which takes the difference between the value right after reset and the signal value, readily cancels reset noise for low noise. The simple circuitry also makes higher resolution and lower cost easier. For applications where subjects mostly move slowly, these image-quality advantages win out.

A rolling shutter exposes and reads pixels row by row, so top and bottom rows are captured several to tens of milliseconds apart. Fast-moving subjects appear skewed and flickering lights produce bands. A global shutter exposes all pixels simultaneously, avoiding distortion, and is used in industrial inspection and automotive cameras, but it needs in-pixel charge storage, making pixels larger.

FAQ

What is the difference between a rolling shutter and a global shutter?

A rolling shutter exposes rows one after another, so the capture time varies by row and fast-moving objects can appear distorted. A global shutter exposes all pixels at once and avoids this distortion, but each pixel needs a charge-storage element, which tends to cost sensitivity and money.

Why do rolling-shutter images get distorted?

The top and bottom rows of the frame are exposed several to tens of milliseconds apart, so if the subject or camera moves in that time, each row records a different position. Objects moving sideways appear slanted, and rotating objects appear bent.

Are horizontal bands in video under artificial light caused by the rolling shutter?

Bands seen under fluorescent or LED lighting usually do involve the rolling shutter. The light's brightness varies with the mains cycle, and rows exposed at different moments receive different amounts of light. Setting the exposure time to an integer multiple of the brightness-variation period reduces the effect.
