What is Laplace transform?

A method that transforms a function of time f(t) (t ≥ 0) into a function of the complex variable s, F(s) = ∫₀^∞ f(t)e^(−st) dt. With zero initial values, differentiation corresponds to multiplying by s and integration to dividing by s, so linear differential equations can be handled as algebraic expressions in s. It is the basic tool for finding transfer functions in control engineering, and for discrete-time systems the z-transform plays the same role. A unit step transforms to 1/s and e^(−at) to 1/(s + a).

Commonly used transform pairs

A unit impulse δ(t) becomes 1, a unit step 1/s, a ramp t 1/s², e^(−at) 1/(s + a) and sin ωt ω/(s² + ω²). Delaying in time by L multiplies by e^(−Ls), and multiplying by e^(−at) replaces s with s + a. Combining these pairs with partial-fraction expansion lets many responses be inverse-transformed by looking them up in a table.

The final value and initial value theorems

The final value of an output can be found as lim[t→∞] f(t) = lim[s→0] sF(s), but only when all poles of sF(s) lie in the left half of the s-plane. For example, the final value of the step response of a system with transfer function G(s) is G(0), called the steady-state gain (DC gain). The initial value is found as lim[t→+0] f(t) = lim[s→∞] sF(s).

Relation to the Fourier transform and the z-transform

Evaluating the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis s = jω gives the Fourier transform (the frequency response). For sampled signals the z-transform with z = e^(sT) plays the same role, and the left half of the s-plane corresponds to the inside of the unit circle in the z-plane. The continuous-time rule "stable if all poles have negative real parts" therefore becomes "stable if all poles lie inside the unit circle" in discrete time.

Answer: A. Differentiation is multiplying by s, and integration is dividing by s

Under the Laplace transform, the derivative of f(t) corresponds to sF(s) − f(0) and the integral to F(s)/s. With zero initial values, differentiation becomes multiplication by s and integration becomes division by s, so a differential equation turns into calculations with polynomials in s. For example, the RC-circuit equation RC·dv/dt + v = vin becomes (RCs + 1)V(s) = Vin(s), which gives the transfer function 1/(1 + RCs). The unit step function transforms to 1/s, and e^(−at) to 1/(s + a).

FAQ

Why is the Laplace transform used in control engineering?

Because it turns differential equations into algebraic expressions, so the transfer function of a system of connected elements can be built up by multiplication and division. The pole positions also show the stability and speed of response.

What happens when the initial values are not zero?

The transform of a derivative becomes sF(s) − f(0), and an initial-value term remains. Transfer functions are defined with zero initial values, so the response due to initial values is considered and added separately.
