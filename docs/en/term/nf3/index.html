What is NF3 (nitrogen trifluoride)?

A gas used for cleaning CVD chambers and the like. Its global warming potential is high, so emission reduction is required.

Japanese name: NF3(三フッ化窒素)

How NF3 cleans a CVD chamber

Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) coats not only the wafer but also the chamber walls, showerhead and heater. Left to build up, these films crack and flake, shedding particles onto wafers. Chambers are therefore cleaned periodically, at intervals set by the accumulated film thickness. NF3 is dissociated in a plasma into fluorine radicals, which react with silicon-based deposits such as silicon oxide, silicon nitride or amorphous silicon to form volatile SiF4 that the vacuum pumps carry away. The nitrogen leaves as harmless N2, and the chamber never has to be opened for a wet clean.

Remote plasma vs. in-chamber plasma

Older cleans fed perfluorocarbons (PFCs) such as C2F6 into the chamber and struck a plasma there. Much of the gas passed through undecomposed, and ion bombardment wore chamber parts. A remote plasma source instead dissociates NF3 in a separate cavity upstream, with over 95% efficiency, and sends mostly neutral fluorine radicals into the chamber. NF3 suits this because its nitrogen-fluorine bonds are much weaker than the carbon-fluorine bonds in PFCs, so it breaks apart readily. Higher gas utilization gives faster cleans, less wear and far less unreacted greenhouse gas in the exhaust.

Greenhouse impact and abatement

NF3 is a potent, long-lived greenhouse gas: its global warming potential is about 16000 times that of CO2 on a 100-year basis in the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) (about 17000 in AR6), and it persists in the atmosphere for centuries. Fabs therefore treat tool exhaust with point-of-use abatement systems that decompose residual fluorinated gases by combustion, plasma or heat, then wet-scrub the resulting hydrogen fluoride. Whatever escapes counts as a direct (Scope 1) emission in corporate greenhouse gas accounting, so abatement efficiency and gas utilization in the tool both matter for a fab's reported footprint.

Other uses and handling

Besides chamber cleaning, NF3 is a fluorine source in some etch steps and in dry pre-cleans. In SiCoNi-type processing, a remote NF3/NH3 plasma converts native oxide into an ammonium fluorosilicate salt that is then removed by gentle heating, with no liquid that could collapse tall structures. Unlike nitrogen, which is piped in bulk for purging, NF3 is a specialty gas delivered in cylinders. It is toxic and becomes a strong oxidizer at high temperature, so it is kept in ventilated gas cabinets with leak detection.

Answer: C. Cleaning films deposited inside CVD chambers

Repeated CVD deposits film on chamber walls, which flakes off and causes particles. NF3 is broken down in a plasma to produce fluorine radicals that convert these deposits into volatile fluorides. Remote plasma sources, which generate the plasma outside the chamber and deliver only radicals, are now standard and reduce damage to chamber parts. NF3's 100-year global warming potential is about 16000 times that of CO2 in the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) (about 17000 in AR6), so exhaust is abated before release.

FAQ

Why is NF3 used instead of C2F6 or CF4 for chamber cleaning?

NF3 dissociates much more completely in a plasma, especially in a remote plasma source, so it delivers more fluorine radicals per molecule and cleans faster. Less gas leaves the tool unreacted, which sharply cuts greenhouse gas emissions compared with PFC-based cleans.

What is the global warming potential of NF3?

About 16000 times that of CO2 over 100 years in the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) (about 17000 in AR6), and it stays in the atmosphere for hundreds of years. That is why fabs abate NF3 exhaust and report emissions as Scope 1.

Is NF3 used to etch wafers?

Its main use is cleaning deposition chambers, but it also serves as a fluorine source in some etch processes and in dry native-oxide pre-cleans that combine NF3 and NH3 in a remote plasma.
