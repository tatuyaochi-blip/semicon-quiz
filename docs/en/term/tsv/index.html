What is TSV (through-silicon via)?

An electrode that passes through the silicon substrate. It connects stacked chips vertically over a short distance.

Japanese name: TSV(シリコン貫通電極)

Field: Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets)

How a TSV is fabricated

A through-silicon via (TSV) starts as a deep hole etched into the silicon, typically by deep reactive ion etching using the Bosch process, which alternates SF6 etching with C4F8 sidewall passivation to reach aspect ratios around 10. The sidewalls are lined with an insulating oxide, a diffusion barrier and a copper seed layer, and the hole is filled with copper by electroplating. Excess copper is removed by CMP. Finally the wafer is ground from the back, while temporarily bonded to a glass or silicon carrier, until the via tips are exposed. Void-free filling of the deep hole is the hardest step.

Typical dimensions

TSV diameters range from a few micrometers to over ten micrometers, and the silicon they pass through is thinned to tens of micrometers; HBM DRAM dies, for example, are thinned to around 30 µm so that 8 to 16 of them fit within a fixed stack height. Because the path through the die is so short, a TSV has far less resistance and capacitance than a wire bond loop, and thousands of them can be placed under one die. That density of short vertical links is what makes wide, low-energy interfaces like HBM possible.

Stress and the keep-out zone

Copper expands with temperature about six times as much as silicon (roughly 17 versus 2.6 ppm/K). After the hot steps of fabrication, the mismatch leaves residual stress in the silicon around each TSV, and stress changes carrier mobility, so nearby transistors drift in drive current. Designers therefore reserve a keep-out zone of several micrometers around every TSV in which no sensitive devices are placed. Copper can also expand upward out of the via during annealing, which must be controlled so it does not damage the wiring above.

TSV versus other vertical links

Wire bonding connects stacked dies around their edges with long loops and is limited to the perimeter. TSVs connect through the die body, which is why they serve 3D DRAM stacks, silicon interposers and logic-on-memory stacks. Monolithic (sequential) 3D integration goes further: it builds a second transistor layer directly on the first and links them with vias only tens of nanometers wide, more than ten thousand times denser than TSV die stacking, but upper-tier processing must stay below about 500 °C.

TSVs are formed by deep etching, such as the Bosch process that alternates etching and passivation deposition, to create holes with aspect ratios around 10 in silicon. Insulator, barrier and seed layers are deposited on the sidewalls, and the holes are filled with copper or similar metal by plating. The wafer is then thinned from the back to expose the via tips. Filling deep holes to the bottom without voids is the difficult part.

FAQ

What does TSV stand for in semiconductors?

TSV stands for through-silicon via, a copper-filled (or other metal-filled) hole that runs through the thickness of a silicon die or interposer to connect its front and back electrically. It is the vertical wiring used in stacked memory such as HBM and in silicon interposers.

Why do TSVs need a keep-out zone?

Copper and silicon have very different thermal expansion coefficients, so the silicon around a TSV ends up mechanically stressed after processing. That stress shifts the mobility and current of nearby transistors, so sensitive devices are kept several micrometers away.

What is the difference between a TSV and a wire bond?

A wire bond is a thin wire looped from a pad on the die edge to the package, while a TSV passes straight through the silicon. TSVs are far shorter and can be placed across the whole die area, giving many more connections with lower resistance and capacitance.
