What is HBT (heterojunction bipolar transistor)?

A bipolar transistor using materials with different band gaps for the emitter and base. Types include SiGe and GaAs-based devices, used in high-frequency amplifiers.

Japanese name: HBT(ヘテロ接合バイポーラトランジスタ)

Field: Bipolar transistors

What the heterojunction buys

In an ordinary homojunction bipolar transistor, emitter efficiency requires the emitter to be much more heavily doped than the base, otherwise too many holes flow back from base into emitter. That forces a lightly doped base, which has high resistance and must not be made too thin. In a heterojunction bipolar transistor (HBT), the emitter has a wider band gap than the base, and the band offset blocks hole back-injection by an exponential factor. The base can then be heavily doped and very thin, lowering base resistance and transit time and raising the transition frequency fT and maximum oscillation frequency fmax.

SiGe HBTs

A silicon-germanium (SiGe) HBT keeps a silicon emitter and collector but grows a SiGe base, tens of nanometres thick and doped with boron during low-temperature epitaxy. Grading germanium from near zero at the emitter side to about 20 to 30% at the collector side narrows the band gap progressively, creating a built-in field that speeds electrons across the base. A small amount of carbon limits boron diffusion so the base stays thin. Because it is built on silicon, a SiGe HBT can be integrated with CMOS in BiCMOS processes, with fT reaching hundreds of GHz.

III-V HBTs

Compound-semiconductor HBTs use emitters such as InGaP or AlGaAs on a GaAs base, or layer stacks on indium phosphide (InP). GaAs HBTs combine good breakdown voltage, high current density and a semi-insulating substrate, which suits them to the power amplifiers in mobile handsets. InP HBTs reach the highest frequencies and are used in very high-speed optical-communication circuits and millimetre-wave research. All are built from epitaxial layer stacks, so base thickness, and thus speed, is set by growth control rather than lithography.

HBT vs. HEMT and MOSFET

HBT current flows vertically through layers whose thickness is defined by epitaxy, giving high transconductance and current density from a modest footprint. Turn-on voltage is set by band gap, so devices across a wafer match well, and low-frequency 1/f noise is lower than in field-effect transistors, which helps oscillator phase noise. High-electron-mobility transistors (HEMTs) conduct laterally in a two-dimensional electron gas and typically win on noise figure for low-noise receivers. Silicon CMOS wins on integration density and cost but gives lower breakdown voltage at high frequency.

Answer: B. Thanks to a heterojunction with a SiGe base, it can operate up to high frequencies

A SiGe HBT is a heterojunction bipolar transistor with a narrow-bandgap SiGe base. The band gap difference between emitter and base suppresses hole back-injection, so the base can be heavily doped and thin without losing injection efficiency, and grading the Ge content creates a field in the base that accelerates electrons. With fT reaching hundreds of GHz on a silicon process, it is used in smartphone RF circuits, optical communications and automotive radar.

Answer: A. Epitaxial growth (sometimes grading the Ge concentration with depth)

The base of a SiGe HBT is formed by low-temperature epitaxy of a SiGe layer tens of nm thick, doped with boron during growth. Grading Ge from 0% at the emitter side to 20–30% at the collector side tilts the band gap, creating a built-in field that accelerates electrons and shortens transit time. Adding a little carbon suppresses boron diffusion to keep the base thin.

FAQ

Why is SiGe used in the base of an HBT?

SiGe has a narrower band gap than silicon, so the Si emitter to SiGe base heterojunction suppresses hole back-injection and allows a heavily doped, thin base. Grading the germanium content also adds an accelerating field, raising speed while staying compatible with silicon processing.

What is the difference between an HBT and a conventional BJT?

A conventional BJT uses the same material throughout, so emitter efficiency relies on doping ratios. An HBT uses a wider-gap emitter, decoupling emitter efficiency from base doping and enabling much higher frequency operation.

Where are GaAs HBTs used?

Mainly in RF power amplifiers for mobile phones and other wireless equipment, where their power density, breakdown voltage and efficiency at gigahertz frequencies are valuable.
