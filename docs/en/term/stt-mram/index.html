What is STT-MRAM?

MRAM that writes by flipping the magnetization of the MTJ's free layer using the spin-transfer torque of a spin-polarized current. It is in volume production as embedded non-volatile memory.

Japanese name: STT-MRAM

Field: Emerging memories

The magnetic tunnel junction cell

Each bit is a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ): two ferromagnetic layers, typically CoFeB, separated by an MgO tunnel barrier about a nanometer thick. The reference layer's magnetization is fixed, while the free layer's can point either way. When the two are parallel, electrons tunnel easily and resistance is low; when antiparallel, resistance is higher, and the relative difference is the tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) ratio. A cell usually pairs one access transistor with one MTJ, built in the back-end interconnect layers, and is read by comparing its resistance with a reference.

How spin-transfer torque writes

Electrons flowing from the reference layer into the free layer are spin-polarized by the reference layer, and they transfer angular momentum to the free layer, pulling its magnetization parallel. Reversing the current direction drives it antiparallel, because electrons whose spin opposes the reference layer are reflected back into the free layer, so the access transistor must pass current both ways. Earlier MRAM switched bits with magnetic fields from nearby write lines, and the needed current grew as cells shrank. With spin-transfer torque, the write current scales down roughly with junction area, which is why the approach became mainstream.

Perpendicular MTJs and retention

Retention depends on the energy barrier between the two magnetization states relative to thermal energy, kT. In-plane MTJs rely on an elongated cell shape for that barrier, which weakens as cells shrink. Perpendicular MTJs use the perpendicular magnetic anisotropy that arises at the CoFeB/MgO interface, so even small round cells stay thermally stable while switching at lower current. There is still a trade-off: a higher barrier gives longer retention but generally needs more write current, so devices are tuned differently for code storage and for cache-like use.

Write errors and read disturb

Thermal fluctuation makes switching stochastic, so a pulse of a given current and width occasionally fails to flip the bit. Raising current lowers this write error rate but stresses the thin MgO barrier toward breakdown, so designs combine current and pulse-width margins, verify-and-rewrite and error-correcting code (ECC). Reading also passes current through the junction, which exerts torque, so read current must stay well below the switching current to avoid read disturb, yet large enough for a fast, reliable sense.

Answer: C. By reversing the magnetization with spin-transfer torque from a spin-polarized current

In STT-MRAM, current flows through the MTJ, and electrons spin-polarized by the reference layer transfer angular momentum to the free layer (spin-transfer torque), flipping its magnetization; the current direction determines 0 or 1. Early field-written MRAM needed more current as cells shrank, but STT write current scales down with cell area, so it suits scaling and is now mainstream.

Why have MTJs with magnetization perpendicular to the film plane (perpendicular MTJs) become mainstream in recent STT-MRAM?

  1. ABecause no magnetic materials are needed to manufacture them
  2. BBecause they maintain thermal stability more easily when scaled and allow lower write currents
  3. CBecause light can be used for reading

Answer: B. Because they maintain thermal stability more easily when scaled and allow lower write currents

In-plane MTJs rely on cell shape, such as an ellipse, to stabilize magnetization, so stability is lost as they shrink. Perpendicular MTJs use perpendicular magnetic anisotropy arising at interfaces such as CoFeB/MgO, keeping even small round cells thermally stable while lowering write current. This materials advance made volume production of STT-MRAM possible in the late 2010s.

FAQ

How is data read in STT-MRAM?

A small current is passed through the MTJ and its resistance is compared with a reference: parallel magnetization gives low resistance and antiparallel gives high resistance. The read current is kept well below the write current to avoid flipping the bit.

Why do STT-MRAM cells use perpendicular MTJs?

Perpendicular anisotropy from the CoFeB/MgO interface keeps small, round cells thermally stable without relying on cell shape, and it allows lower switching current. In-plane cells lose stability as they shrink.

Is STT-MRAM non-volatile?

Yes. Data is stored as the magnetization direction of the free layer, which is retained without power, so no refresh is needed as it is in DRAM.
