What is Crystal orientation?

The direction of crystal planes and axes, expressed with Miller indices such as (100). The (100) surface is standard for silicon wafers used for MOSFETs.

Japanese name: 結晶方位

Field: Silicon wafers

Reading Miller indices

Crystal planes and directions are named with Miller indices. Parentheses, as in (100), denote a specific plane; braces, as in {111}, denote the family of equivalent planes; square brackets, as in [110], a specific direction; and angle brackets, as in <110>, the family of equivalent directions. In a cubic crystal such as silicon, the direction [hkl] is perpendicular to the plane (hkl). A wafer is described by its surface plane, so a (100) wafer has the (100) plane as its top surface, with in-plane directions marked by a notch or flat.

Why CMOS uses (100)

Most CMOS wafers are (100). That surface has a lower density of available bonds per unit area than (111), giving an interface-state density at the Si/SiO2 interface roughly one tenth as large, which stabilizes threshold voltage and improves MOS behavior. Electron mobility in the inversion layer is also highest on (100). The (111) orientation, which oxidizes faster, was once common for bipolar devices. Hole mobility, by contrast, is higher on (110) surfaces, one reason channel orientation is a design variable in advanced transistors with vertical sidewalls.

Notches, flats and cleaving

Larger wafers carry a small V-shaped notch on the edge marking a reference direction such as <110>, so tools can align patterns to the crystal; wafers of 150 mm and below used a straight orientation flat instead. Silicon cleaves most easily along {111} planes, which need the least energy to break. On a (100) wafer, fractures tend to run along {110} planes perpendicular to the surface, giving straight cracks at right angles that reveal the in-plane orientation and are used for cross-section samples.

Orientation-dependent processing

Many processes run at different rates on different planes. Thermal oxidation is faster on (111) than on (100), by up to about 1.5 times for thin oxides, which matters in FinFETs whose tops and sidewalls expose different planes; ISSG (in-situ steam generation) and radical oxidation are used because their growth depends less on orientation. Anisotropic wet etchants such as KOH attack {111} planes very slowly, so on a (100) wafer they produce V-grooves and pits bounded by {111} sidewalls at about 54.7° to the surface, a staple of MEMS fabrication.

How does the thermal oxidation rate of silicon depend on crystal orientation?

  1. A(100) oxidizes faster than (111)
  2. BOxidation rate is the same regardless of orientation
  3. C(111) oxidizes faster than (100)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. (111) oxidizes faster than (100)

Under identical conditions, (111) surfaces oxidize faster than (100), with the difference largest for thin oxides (about 1.5×), because they have higher atomic density and more bonds available for reaction. In structures like FinFETs, whose top and sidewalls have different orientations, oxide thickness would vary by location, so radical oxidation or ALD, which are less orientation-sensitive, are used.

FAQ

Why are most silicon wafers (100) oriented?

The (100) surface gives a much lower interface-state density at the gate oxide interface than (111) and high electron mobility, which yields stable, well-behaved MOSFETs.

What does the notch on a silicon wafer indicate?

It marks a reference crystal direction, typically <110>, so lithography and other tools can align patterns to the crystal. Smaller wafers used a flat ground on the edge for the same purpose.

What is the difference between (100) and {100}?

(100) names one specific plane, while {100} names the whole family of planes equivalent to it by crystal symmetry. Square and angle brackets do the same for directions.
