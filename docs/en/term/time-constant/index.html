What is Time constant?

A quantity describing how fast a circuit such as an RC network responds to a step; for an RC circuit τ = RC. The response reaches about 63% of its final value after τ and has nearly settled after about 5τ.

Where the 63% comes from

When a step voltage charges a capacitor through a resistor, the capacitor voltage follows V(1 − e^(−t/τ)) with τ = RC. At t = τ the exponent is −1, so the voltage reaches 1 − 1/e, about 63% of its final value. It reaches about 86% at 2τ, 95% at 3τ, 98% at 4τ and over 99% at 5τ, which is why 5τ is treated as settled. Discharge mirrors this, falling to about 37% after one time constant. If the initial slope continued unchanged, the voltage would reach its final value in exactly τ.

Time constants beyond RC

Any first-order system has a time constant. For a resistor and inductor, τ = L/R, and the current rather than the voltage rises exponentially. Thermal systems behave the same way, with τ equal to thermal resistance times heat capacity, which explains why a chip and its heat sink take seconds or longer to warm up. The time constant also links time and frequency: a first-order low-pass filter has its −3 dB cutoff at 1/(2πτ), and its 10–90% rise time is about 2.2τ.

Typical numbers in practice

With 10 kΩ and 1 µF, τ is 10 ms, a useful scale for reset delays and switch debouncing. An open-drain line with a 10 kΩ pull-up and 100 pF of wiring capacitance has τ = 1 µs. Lowering the pull-up to 1 kΩ makes rising edges ten times faster, but then 3.3 mA flows from a 3.3 V supply whenever the output is low, which can push the low-level voltage above its specified limit if the driver's rated sink current is exceeded.

Settling to a given accuracy

When a precise final value matters, the required number of time constants is ln(1/ε), where ε is the allowed fractional error. Settling to 1% takes about 4.6τ, and settling to half a least-significant bit of an N-bit converter takes (N+1)·ln2 time constants, roughly nine for 12 bits. This is why sample-and-hold acquisition times, reference-capacitor charging and filter delays are all specified as multiples of an RC product, and why a higher source resistance directly lengthens the time an analog-to-digital converter must wait.

  1. A10 ms

  3. C1 ms

Answer: A. 10 ms

The time constant of an RC circuit is τ = RC: 1×10³ Ω × 10×10⁻⁶ F = 1×10⁻² s = 10 ms. It is the time for the capacitor voltage to reach about 63% of its final value after a step input, and after 5τ (50 ms here) the voltage is essentially final (over 99%). Since ohms times farads gives seconds, the units provide a check on the calculation.

What is the time constant of an RL circuit with a coil of inductance L = 10 mH in series with a resistor R = 100 Ω?

  1. A1 s
  2. B100 µs
  3. C1 ms
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 100 µs

The time constant of an RL circuit is τ = L/R: 10×10⁻³ H/100 Ω = 1×10⁻⁴ s = 100 µs. When a DC voltage is applied, the current reaches about 63% of its final value (E/R) after τ and is essentially final after 5τ = 500 µs. A larger resistance shortens the time constant—the opposite of an RC circuit (τ = RC), where a larger resistance lengthens it.

FAQ

How do you calculate the time constant of an RC circuit?

Multiply resistance in ohms by capacitance in farads to get τ in seconds. For example, 10 kΩ and 1 µF give 10 ms.

Why is 5 time constants considered fully charged?

After 5τ the remaining error is e^−5, less than 1%, which is negligible for most purposes. Precision circuits may need more, such as about 9τ for 12-bit accuracy.

What is the relationship between time constant and cutoff frequency?

For a first-order RC low-pass filter, the cutoff frequency is 1/(2πτ). A 1 ms time constant gives a cutoff of about 159 Hz.
