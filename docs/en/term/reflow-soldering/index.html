What is Reflow soldering?

Printing solder paste on a circuit board, placing components and heating everything in an oven to melt the solder. It is the standard method for surface mounting.

Japanese name: リフローはんだ付け

The reflow soldering flow

In reflow soldering, solder paste is first printed through a metal stencil onto the electrodes (pads) of the circuit board. Solder paste is fine solder powder mixed with flux. Next, a placement machine sets components on the paste, whose tackiness holds them temporarily. Finally, the board is carried by conveyor through a reflow oven, where it is heated to melt the solder and then cooled to solidify it. Many components are soldered at once, and when both sides of a board are populated, each side is reflowed in turn. Solder balls of BGAs are also connected this way.

The temperature profile

The reflow oven heats the board along a set temperature profile. Preheating first raises the temperature of the board and components gradually, letting the flux act to remove oxide from the electrode surfaces. The temperature is then raised above the solder's melting point to melt it, wet the electrodes and form the joints. Sn-Ag-Cu (SAC) lead-free solder melts at about 217–220 °C, and a peak of about 245–260 °C is a typical guide. Too short a time above the melting point causes poor wetting, while too long a time or too high a peak damages components and boards. Because large and small components heat at different rates, profiles must keep temperature differences across the board small.

Typical defects and component-side precautions

In reflow, the surface tension of molten solder pulls components into the correct position (self-alignment), but various defects can also occur: tombstoning, in which one end of a small chip component lifts; bridging, in which solder joins adjacent electrodes; voids inside joints; and head-in-pillow, in which a BGA ball and the paste fail to merge. Large packages warp when heated, which can cause open connections. Plastic packages that have absorbed moisture can crack as the moisture vaporizes during heating (the popcorn effect), so the time after opening is controlled according to the MSL, and parts are baked before mounting if necessary.

How it differs from flow soldering and other methods

Flow (wave) soldering passes the board over a wave of molten solder and is mainly used for through-hole components whose leads are inserted into holes. Reflow allows the amount of solder to be set precisely by printing and lets small components be mounted densely on both sides of the board, so it is the mainstream method for surface mounting. However, because the whole board is heated, it may not suit heat-sensitive components. For thin chips that warp heavily or for fine-pitch bumps, thermocompression bonding (TCB), which heats and presses chips one at a time while holding them, is used.

Surface tension of molten solder also pulls parts into alignment, and it is the mainstream method for surface-mount assembly. FAQ

What is the peak temperature in reflow?

For Sn-Ag-Cu lead-free solder, about 245–260 °C is a typical guide. The melting point is about 217–220 °C, so margin is added to melt and wet reliably. With the older Sn-Pb solder (melting point 183 °C), the peak was around 220–235 °C. Actual conditions are set according to the solder paste and component specifications.

What is the difference between reflow and flow soldering?

Reflow prints solder paste, places components and melts the paste by heating in an oven; it is used for surface-mount components. Flow passes the board through a wave of molten solder and is mainly used for through-hole components. Boards with both kinds of component may combine the two.

Why do components stand up during reflow?

This defect is called tombstoning (the Manhattan effect). If the solder at the two ends of a component melts at different times or in different amounts, surface tension on the side that melts first pulls the component and lifts the other end. Smaller, lighter components are more prone to it, and it is prevented by matching the size and routing of the left and right pads and keeping temperatures uniform.
