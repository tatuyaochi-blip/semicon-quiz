What is NBTI (negative bias temperature instability)?

A degradation phenomenon in which the magnitude of a pMOS threshold voltage gradually increases under negative gate voltage at high temperature.

Japanese name: NBTI

NBTI (negative bias temperature instability) appears in 5 practice questions across 2 topics, including Reliability, MOSFET.

Field: Reliability

How NBTI damages a pMOS transistor

NBTI (negative bias temperature instability) occurs when a pMOS gate is held negative relative to its channel, so the channel is inverted with holes, at elevated temperature. Holes at the Si/SiO2 interface help break Si–H bonds left from hydrogen passivation, creating interface traps, while positive charge also builds up in the gate dielectric. Both effects make the threshold voltage more negative, so its magnitude grows, and drive current and transconductance fall. No drain current is needed: a pMOS simply sitting with its gate low and its source at VDD is under NBTI stress.

Slow drift with partial recovery

NBTI shift grows over time roughly as a power law with a small exponent, so most of the change appears early and then continues slowly over years of operation. When the stress is removed, part of the shift recovers, starting within microseconds, as some trapped charge escapes. This makes measurement tricky, because a slow measurement after stress underestimates the damage, and it explains why AC stress, where the gate toggles, degrades a device less than constant DC stress. Nitrogen added to gate oxides to block boron penetration is known to make NBTI worse.

NBTI vs. PBTI and HCI

PBTI (positive bias temperature instability) is the nMOS counterpart: positive gate bias and heat trap electrons at defects in high-k layers such as HfO2, raising the nMOS threshold. It was minor with SiO2 gates but became significant after high-k/metal gate adoption. Hot carrier injection (HCI) is different in kind: it needs current flow and a strong lateral field near the drain, where energetic carriers damage the interface or get injected into the dielectric. NBTI, by contrast, is driven by the vertical gate field and temperature alone.

What designers do about it

Because NBTI slowly raises pMOS threshold voltage, logic paths get slower over the product lifetime. Designers use aging-aware timing libraries and add guardband so that a chip still meets its frequency target after years at worst-case voltage and temperature. Circuits that hold a pMOS in a static on-state are most exposed, such as SRAM cells storing the same value for long periods, where shifted pull-up devices degrade read stability and noise margin. Reliability engineers characterize NBTI with accelerated stress at higher voltage and temperature and extrapolate to use conditions.

Answer: C. GIDL (gate-induced drain leakage)

Even when a transistor is off, a large gate-to-drain voltage bends the bands sharply near the drain edge of the gate, and band-to-band tunneling causes current to flow—GIDL, which notably grows as the gate is driven more negative. It is a problem in devices requiring ultra-low leakage such as DRAM cell transistors and is controlled by engineering the drain-edge doping and field. DIBL is threshold lowering by drain voltage, and NBTI is a pMOS degradation mechanism.

Answer: A. NBTI

NBTI (negative bias temperature instability) arises when negative gate bias and high temperature on a pMOS break Si–H bonds at the Si/SiO2 interface, creating interface traps, while positive charge accumulates in the dielectric, increasing the threshold voltage magnitude. Circuits slow gradually over years, so designers build in margin for the degradation. Partial recovery when stress is removed is another characteristic.

FAQ

What does NBTI stand for?

NBTI stands for negative bias temperature instability, a wear-out mechanism in which a pMOS transistor under negative gate bias and high temperature gradually develops a larger threshold voltage magnitude.

What is the difference between NBTI and hot carrier injection?

NBTI is caused by the vertical gate field and temperature and occurs even with no current flowing, mainly in pMOS. Hot carrier injection requires current and a strong lateral field near the drain, where high-energy carriers damage the gate dielectric or interface.

Does NBTI damage recover?

Partly. When the negative gate bias is removed, some of the threshold shift relaxes quickly, but a permanent component remains and accumulates over the lifetime of the device.
