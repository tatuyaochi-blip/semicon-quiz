What is Wire bonding?

A method of connecting the chip's bond pads to a lead frame or substrate with thin wires of gold, copper or similar metals.

Japanese name: ワイヤボンディング

Wire bonding appears in 12 practice questions across 4 topics, including Packaging and assembly, Interconnect processes, Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets).

Field: Packaging and assembly

How ball bonding works

In thermosonic ball bonding, the wire passes through a fine ceramic tube called a capillary. An electric spark melts the wire tip into a ball about twice the wire diameter, which is pressed onto the chip's bond pad with heat (roughly 150–200 °C), force and ultrasonic vibration to form the first bond. The capillary then draws a loop to the lead frame or substrate, where it presses the wire flat into a stitch, the second bond, and tears it off ready for the next ball. Because a ball has no orientation, wires can run in any direction.

Wire materials and sizes

Bonding wires are typically 15–50 µm in diameter, thinner than a human hair, to match pads only tens of micrometers across. Gold was the traditional choice, but copper has largely displaced it on cost and also conducts electricity and heat better. Copper oxidizes and is harder, so balls are formed under nitrogen–hydrogen gas, palladium-coated wire is common, and parameters are tuned to avoid damaging pads. Power devices use thick aluminum wire of about 100–500 µm, flat ribbons or copper clips, usually joined by wedge bonding, which presses the wire flat from the side.

What happens under the pad

Bond pads are usually aluminum about 1 µm thick, even on chips with copper interconnects, because aluminum's thin native oxide protects it and it bonds reliably. Excess force or ultrasound, or a hard copper wire, can crack the dielectric or silicon beneath the pad, a defect called cratering, which is a particular concern over mechanically weak low-k films. Probe marks left by wafer test must not spoil the bonding area. Over long high-temperature service, gold–aluminum intermetallics grow at the ball bond and can form voids if the joint is poorly controlled.

Wire bonding vs. flip-chip

Wire bonding remains the most widely used chip interconnect by unit volume because the equipment is inexpensive and routing is flexible. Its limits are that connections sit only around the die edge and each wire adds resistance and inductance, a rule of thumb being about 1 nH per millimeter. Flip-chip places bumps across the whole die face for more I/O and shorter paths. Stacked packages for NAND flash and DRAM still wire-bond staircase-offset dies, sometimes more than 16, while very high bandwidth stacks use TSVs or hybrid bonding.

Which metal has been used in many ICs for the top-level bond pads where wire bonding is done?

  1. ATungsten
  2. BTitanium
  3. CAluminum
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Aluminum

Even with copper interconnects inside, the top-level bond pads have widely used aluminum about 1 µm thick. Copper surfaces oxidize and corrode progressively, whereas aluminum forms a dense native oxide a few nm thick that protects it, and it has a long track record of ultrasonic bonding with gold and copper wire. A diffusion barrier separates copper and aluminum. For flip-chip, bumps are formed on the pads.

What is the design practice called in which active devices and wiring are placed directly beneath the bond pads used for wire bonding and the like?

  1. AScribe line
  2. BCUP (circuit under pad)
  3. CDummy fill
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. CUP (circuit under pad)

Circuits were traditionally kept out from under bond pads so bonding impact and force would not crack the layers beneath. Pads are large—tens of µm to about 100 µm square—and numerous, consuming a significant share of die area. Circuit-under-pad (CUP) reinforces the layers below so circuitry can occupy that space, shrinking die area by several to over ten percent. With fragile low-k films, ensuring the structure withstands bonding and probe-needle stress is the challenge.

FAQ

Why is copper wire replacing gold wire in wire bonding?

Copper is far cheaper than gold and has better electrical and thermal conductivity. Its drawbacks, easier oxidation and greater hardness, are handled with forming gas during ball formation, palladium-coated wire and carefully tuned bonding conditions.

What is the difference between ball bonding and wedge bonding?

Ball bonding melts the wire tip into a ball and bonds it with heat, force and ultrasound, so the wire can leave in any direction; it is used with gold and copper wire. Wedge bonding presses the side of the wire flat with a wedge tool, fixes the wire direction, and is common with aluminum wire and ribbons.

Which end of the wire is bonded to the chip?

Normally the ball (first bond) goes on the chip pad because it is compact, and the stitch (second bond) goes on the lead or substrate. The order is sometimes reversed to achieve very low loops in stacked-die packages.
