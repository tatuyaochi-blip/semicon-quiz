What is Ashing?

Removing photoresist that is no longer needed after etching or implantation by oxidizing it to ash, typically with an oxygen plasma.

Japanese name: アッシング

Ashing appears in 7 practice questions across 4 topics, including Ion implantation, Etching, CMP and cleaning.

Field: Etching

How an asher removes resist

Photoresist is an organic polymer of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. In an oxygen plasma, atomic oxygen radicals attack it and convert it into volatile CO, CO2 and H2O, which are pumped away; the name comes from this burning of the resist, as if to ash. Heating the wafer speeds the reaction. Many ashers are downstream (remote) designs in which the plasma is generated away from the wafer, so ions recombine before arriving and only radicals reach the surface, avoiding ion damage and charging of sensitive devices. Ashing is used after the resist has served as an etch or implant mask.

Stripping resist after high-dose implants

High-dose ion implantation drives hydrogen out of the resist surface and carbonizes it into a hard crust tens of nanometres thick, while the resist underneath stays soft. If the wafer is ashed hot immediately, gas released from the soft layer bursts through the crust, an effect called popping, scattering residue and particles. Post-implant strips therefore start at low temperature and ramp up, or remove the crust first with hydrogen-based plasma chemistry. The resist used for implant masking must also be thick enough to stop the ions, several times the projected range.

Ashing near low-k dielectrics

Oxygen plasma is a problem in copper interconnect stacks. SiOC low-k films owe their low dielectric constant to Si-CH3 (methyl) groups, and oxygen radicals strip these into Si-OH, which absorbs moisture and can raise the dielectric constant from about 3.0 to above 4. Porous films are damaged deeper because radicals penetrate through the pores. Reducing chemistries based on hydrogen replace oxygen in these steps, and silylation treatments and post-process dehydration bakes are used to repair or limit damage.

Ashing and wet cleaning together

Ashing removes the organic bulk of the resist but not everything. Inorganic residues, metals introduced during implant or etch, and sidewall polymer from dry etching remain and are removed by a wet clean that follows. SPM (sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide, often about 4:1 at 120–150 °C), nicknamed piranha, oxidizes remaining organics. Chlorine-bearing etch residue must be removed promptly because it corrodes aluminium wiring. Ash-and-clean sequences are repeated throughout the flow, so their efficiency and gentleness affect both yield and cost.

FAQ

Why is it called ashing?

The process burns the organic resist in an oxygen plasma, much like reducing material to ash, converting it into gases such as CO2 and H2O that are pumped away.

What is the difference between ashing and wet resist stripping?

Ashing removes resist dry, using plasma radicals, while wet stripping dissolves or oxidizes it with liquid chemistries such as SPM or organic strippers. In practice the two are often combined: ash first, then wet-clean the residue.

Why are downstream ashers used?

A downstream asher generates the plasma away from the wafer so only neutral radicals reach it. This avoids ion bombardment and charging damage to transistors and sensitive films.
