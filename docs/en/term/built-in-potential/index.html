What is Built-in potential?

The potential difference across the depletion layer of a PN junction even with no external voltage applied. About 0.6 to 0.9 V in silicon, it forms the barrier that stops further carrier diffusion.

Japanese name: 内蔵電位

Field: PN junctions and diodes

Why a built-in potential forms

Before contact, the Fermi level of n-type silicon sits near the conduction band and that of p-type silicon near the valence band. When the two are joined, electrons diffuse from the n side into the p side and holes the other way, leaving behind fixed ionized donors (positive) and acceptors (negative). This depletion-region charge creates an electric field that pushes carriers back, and equilibrium is reached when drift exactly cancels diffusion and the Fermi levels line up. The resulting band bending, divided by the electron charge q, is the built-in potential Vbi.

How doping sets its value

For a nondegenerate junction, Vbi = (kT/q)·ln(NA·ND / ni²), where NA and ND are the acceptor and donor concentrations and ni is the intrinsic carrier concentration. With kT/q of about 26 mV at room temperature, each tenfold increase in doping adds only about 60 mV, so silicon junctions typically fall between about 0.6 and 0.9 V; NA = ND = 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ gives roughly 0.83 V. Wider-band-gap materials have much smaller ni and hence larger Vbi. Because both Fermi levels stay inside the gap, Vbi stays below the band gap in volts unless both sides are degenerately doped.

Bias, depletion width and field

Forward bias, with the p side positive, opposes the built-in potential and lowers the barrier to Vbi − V, so injected current rises exponentially. Reverse bias adds to it, and for a one-sided abrupt junction the depletion width is W = √(2ε(Vbi + V)/qN), where N is the lighter doping. The field peaks at the metallurgical junction, and the area under the triangular field profile equals Vbi + V. Since junction capacitance is εA/W, a plot of 1/C² versus reverse voltage is a straight line whose slope gives N and whose voltage intercept gives Vbi.

Why a voltmeter cannot read it

Connecting a voltmeter across an unbiased diode reads zero, because the metal–semiconductor contacts at each terminal have their own contact potentials that exactly cancel Vbi around the loop; otherwise an equilibrium device could deliver power. Vbi also differs from the diode forward voltage of roughly 0.6–0.7 V quoted for silicon, which is the bias at which current becomes appreciable and depends on current level and temperature. The same Fermi-level alignment that produces Vbi in a PN junction produces the flat-band voltage in a MOS structure and the barrier in a Schottky contact.

Answer: C. It is roughly proportional to the square root of V

For a one-sided abrupt junction, the depletion width is W = √(2ε(Vbi + V)/qN), so for large V it is nearly proportional to √V. The junction capacitance therefore falls as 1/√V, and plotting 1/C² against V gives a straight line whose slope yields the doping N of the lightly doped side and whose intercept yields the built-in potential—the basis of C-V profiling. Lower doping lets the depletion region extend further at the same voltage.

FAQ

Can you measure a PN junction's built-in potential with a voltmeter?

No. The contact potentials at the metal leads cancel it, so an unbiased diode reads zero. Vbi is usually inferred from capacitance–voltage measurements instead.

How does doping affect the built-in potential?

Higher doping on either side raises Vbi, but only logarithmically: a tenfold doping increase adds about 60 mV at room temperature.

Is built-in potential the same as the diode forward voltage?

No. They are related, but the forward voltage is the applied bias needed for a practical current and is usually somewhat lower than Vbi, varying with current and temperature.
