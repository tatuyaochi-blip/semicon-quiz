What is Phosphine (PH3)?

A gas compound of phosphorus and hydrogen used to introduce n-type dopant (phosphorus) into silicon. It is extremely toxic.

Japanese name: ホスフィン(PH3)

Field: Process materials (gases and chemicals)

Where phosphine is used

Phosphine (PH3) supplies phosphorus, the most common n-type dopant for silicon. In ion implantation it is cracked and ionized in the ion source, and the mass analyzer selects P⁺ ions. In CVD and epitaxy it is flowed alongside the silicon source for in-situ doping, producing conductive polysilicon for electrodes or phosphorus-doped silicon (Si:P) source/drain regions with concentrations on the order of 10²¹ cm⁻³. It is also used to deposit phosphosilicate glass, and in compound semiconductor growth it is the phosphorus source for materials such as indium phosphide.

Why it is supplied diluted

Only tiny amounts of dopant are needed, so pure phosphine would require flows too small for a mass flow controller to meter accurately. Diluting it to a few percent or to ppm levels in hydrogen or nitrogen lets the required dose be delivered at controllable flows. Dilution also lowers the concentration released in a leak, reducing both toxic and fire risk. For implanters, phosphine is often stored adsorbed on a porous solid in sub-atmospheric containers, which tend to draw air in rather than leak gas out if a valve or fitting fails.

Hazards and controls

Phosphine is extremely toxic, with occupational exposure limits around 0.05 ppm, and it is also flammable. Fabs therefore keep cylinders in exhausted gas cabinets, run it through double-contained lines, and install continuous leak detectors tied to emergency shutoff valves. Exhaust from process tools passes through abatement systems that burn, scrub or adsorb unreacted hydride before release. Sub-atmospheric adsorbent sources reduce the chance of a large release, but they do not change the toxicity of the gas, so detection and exhaust are still required.

Phosphine versus other dopant sources

Arsine (AsH3) is the other hydride n-type source; arsenic is heavier and diffuses more slowly than phosphorus, which suits shallow junctions. Phosphorus oxychloride (POCl3) is a liquid source used in furnace diffusion, where it forms phosphorus-rich glass on the wafer surface and is still common for crystalline silicon solar cell emitters. For the p-type dopant boron, diborane (B2H6) is the usual in-situ doping gas and boron trifluoride (BF3) the usual implant source gas.

Answer: B. They are extremely toxic and require strict leak control

Phosphine and arsine are so toxic that their exposure limits are 0.05 ppm or lower. They are handled with cylinder cabinets, leak detectors, emergency shutoff valves and abatement systems. Sub-atmospheric containers, in which the gas is adsorbed on a porous material so that it tends not to escape in a leak, are also widely used. Answer: B. Because only tiny amounts are needed, dilution makes the flow easier to control precisely and lowers toxicity and reactivity risks

Dopant quantities are tiny, so pure gas would require flows too small for a mass flow controller to control precisely. Diluting to a few percent or ppm levels in hydrogen or nitrogen lets the required amount be delivered accurately at manageable flows. Dilution also lowers concentrations in a leak, reducing toxicity and ignition risk. Pure diborane slowly decomposes even at room temperature, so dilution aids stability too.

FAQ

Why is phosphine so dangerous?

It is highly toxic at very low concentrations, with exposure limits around 0.05 ppm, and it is flammable. A leak can harm people before it is noticed, so it is handled only with gas cabinets, leak detectors, automatic shutoff and exhaust abatement.

Is phosphine an n-type or p-type dopant source?

It is an n-type source: phosphorus has five valence electrons, one more than silicon, so each substitutional phosphorus atom donates an electron. The common p-type counterparts are diborane and boron trifluoride.

What is the difference between phosphine and POCl3 doping?

Phosphine is a gas used in ion implanters and for in-situ doping during CVD or epitaxy. POCl3 is a liquid source used in diffusion furnaces, where it forms a phosphorus glass on the wafer from which phosphorus diffuses into the silicon.
