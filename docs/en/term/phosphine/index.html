What is Phosphine (PH3)?

A gas compound of phosphorus and hydrogen used to introduce n-type dopant (phosphorus) into silicon. It is extremely toxic.

Japanese name: ホスフィン(PH3)

Which correctly describes phosphine (PH3) and arsine (AsH3), used to introduce n-type dopants?

  1. AThey are harmless and need no special control
  2. BThey are extremely toxic and require strict leak control
  3. CThey are nonflammable and odorless and safe to release into air
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. They are extremely toxic and require strict leak control

Phosphine and arsine are so toxic that their exposure limits are 0.05 ppm or lower. They are handled with cylinder cabinets, leak detectors, emergency shutoff valves and abatement systems. Sub-atmospheric containers, in which the gas is adsorbed on a porous material so that it tends not to escape in a leak, are also widely used. They are neither harmless nor nonflammable; phosphine is also flammable.

Why are dopant gases such as diborane and phosphine often supplied diluted to low concentrations in hydrogen, nitrogen or similar?

  1. ATo change the gas's color
  2. BBecause only tiny amounts are needed, dilution makes the flow easier to control precisely and lowers toxicity and reactivity risks
  3. CTo strengthen the odor
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Because only tiny amounts are needed, dilution makes the flow easier to control precisely and lowers toxicity and reactivity risks

Dopant quantities are tiny, so pure gas would require flows too small for a mass flow controller to control precisely. Diluting to a few percent or ppm levels in hydrogen or nitrogen lets the required amount be delivered accurately at manageable flows. Dilution also lowers concentrations in a leak, reducing toxicity and ignition risk. Pure diborane slowly decomposes even at room temperature, so dilution aids stability too.
