What is Cutoff region?

The state of a bipolar transistor in which both junctions are reverse-biased and almost no collector current flows. It corresponds to a switch in the off state.

Japanese name: 遮断領域

Cutoff region appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Bipolar transistors.

Field: Bipolar transistors

Bias conditions in cutoff

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has four operating regions set by its two junctions. In the forward-active region the base-emitter junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction reverse biased; in saturation both are forward biased; in cutoff both are reverse biased. In practice a silicon transistor is effectively in cutoff whenever VBE is below about 0.5 V, because forward current is then negligible. Only junction leakage, on the order of nanoamperes, reaches the collector, so the collector-emitter path behaves like an open switch.

Cutoff as the off state of a switch

In switching circuits a BJT alternates between cutoff (off) and saturation (on). In cutoff almost no collector current flows, so there is no drop across the load resistor and VCE rises to nearly the supply voltage; power dissipation is close to zero. If the base is left floating, collector-base leakage flows into the base and is amplified by the current gain, so the transistor may not turn off fully, especially when hot, since leakage rises steeply with temperature. A resistor between base and emitter diverts this leakage and guarantees cutoff.

Entering and leaving cutoff

Switching speed depends on how fast the transistor crosses between regions. Turning on from cutoff requires charging the junction capacitances until VBE reaches about 0.6–0.7 V before collector current rises, a delay time. Turning off from saturation is slower, because excess minority charge stored in the base must be removed before the transistor can return to cutoff (the storage time). Circuits that keep the transistor out of deep saturation, such as Schottky-clamped transistors, shorten this turn-off delay.

Cutoff in other contexts

For a MOSFET, the comparable off state is VGS below the threshold voltage, although subthreshold current still flows and is usually larger than BJT cutoff leakage in scaled devices. Note that the word saturation means different things for the two devices: BJT saturation is the fully on switch state, whereas MOSFET saturation corresponds to the BJT's active region. The cutoff region is also unrelated to the cutoff frequency (fT), which is the frequency at which current gain falls to one.

Answer: C. Both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions are reverse biased

In cutoff, both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions are reverse-biased (or VBE is below about 0.5 V, so almost no current flows), and only leakage current (around nA) reaches the collector—the switch is off. If the base is left floating, leakage can be amplified so the device is not fully off, so a resistor between base and emitter is standard practice to ensure turn-off.

FAQ

What are the conditions for a BJT to be in the cutoff region?

Both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions are reverse biased, or in practice VBE is below about 0.5 V for silicon. Collector current is then limited to leakage of around nanoamperes.

Why put a resistor between base and emitter?

With a floating base, collector-base leakage flows through the base-emitter junction and is amplified by the current gain, so the transistor may conduct slightly. The resistor shunts that leakage away so the transistor stays firmly in cutoff.

Is the cutoff region related to the cutoff frequency?

No. The cutoff region is a DC bias state where the transistor is off, while the cutoff frequency (fT) is the frequency at which the transistor's small-signal current gain drops to one.
