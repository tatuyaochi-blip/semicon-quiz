What is Slew rate?

The maximum rate at which an op-amp's output voltage can change, expressed in V/µs. If it is insufficient for large, high-frequency signals, the waveform distorts.

Japanese name: スルーレート

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

What limits the output speed

In a typical two-stage op-amp, a fast, large input step overloads the input differential pair, which then steers its entire tail current I to one side. That current, the most the stage can deliver, charges the compensation capacitor Cc, so the output can ramp no faster than SR = I/Cc. Instead of following the input with a smooth exponential, the output moves as a straight-line ramp until the error shrinks and linear operation resumes. Rising and falling slew rates can differ, and datasheets give slew rate in V/µs under stated gain and load conditions.

Full-power bandwidth

A sine wave Vp·sin(2πft) has a maximum slope of 2πf·Vp, at its zero crossings. If that slope exceeds the slew rate, the output cannot keep up and turns into a triangle-like wave. The highest undistorted frequency, called the full-power bandwidth, is therefore f = SR/(2πVp). An op-amp with 0.5 V/µs delivering a 10 V peak sine is limited to about 8 kHz; forgetting the 2π gives an incorrect 50 kHz. Halving the amplitude doubles the usable frequency, so slew limits bite hardest on large signals.

Slew rate vs. gain-bandwidth product

The gain-bandwidth product (GBW) describes small-signal, linear behavior, while slew rate is a large-signal, nonlinear limit, and the two must be checked separately. Suppose an amplifier with a GBW of 1 MHz runs at a gain of 10: its small-signal bandwidth is about 100 kHz, but a 10 V peak output at 100 kHz needs a slew rate of at least 2π × 100 kHz × 10 V ≈ 6.3 V/µs. If the part offers only 0.5 V/µs, large signals distort long before the small-signal bandwidth is reached.

Design levers and typical values

Because GBW = gm1/(2πCc) and SR = I/Cc, the two are linked through the ratio of tail current to input-pair transconductance. A bipolar input pair gives a high gm per unit current, so it tends to have a low slew rate for its bandwidth; emitter degeneration or MOS input pairs with larger overdrive raise slew rate at the same GBW. Typical values range from below 1 V/µs in low-power general-purpose op-amps to well over 1,000 V/µs in high-speed amplifiers, which often use current-feedback or class-AB input structures.

Answer: A. The maximum rate at which the output voltage can change

Slew rate is the maximum rate at which the output voltage can change, expressed in V/µs, set mainly by how fast limited internal current can charge the compensation capacitor. The highest frequency for an undistorted sine of amplitude Vp is f = SR/(2πVp); with SR = 0.5 V/µs and Vp = 10 V that is only about 8 kHz, beyond which the output turns triangular. It limits large-signal bandwidth independently of small-signal GBW.

Answer: A. About 8 kHz

The maximum slope of a sine wave Vp sin(2πft) is 2πf × Vp. If this exceeds the slew rate, the output cannot keep up and distorts into a triangle-like wave. f = SR/(2π × Vp) = 0.5 × 10⁶ V/s ÷ (2π × 10 V) ≈ 8 kHz. This is called the full-power bandwidth, and it falls as amplitude increases. Note that even if the gain-bandwidth product (GBW) is sufficient, bandwidth for large-amplitude signals is limited by the slew rate. About 50 kHz is the value obtained by forgetting to multiply by 2π.

FAQ

How do I calculate the slew rate needed for a sine wave?

Multiply 2π by the frequency and by the peak output voltage: SR ≥ 2πf·Vp. A 10 V peak sine at 20 kHz needs at least about 1.3 V/µs, plus some margin.

What does slew rate distortion look like?

A sine wave becomes triangular because the output can only change at a fixed maximum rate, and step responses show straight-line ramps instead of smooth exponential settling.

Is slew rate the same as bandwidth?

No. Bandwidth (set by GBW) limits small signals linearly, while slew rate limits how fast large signals can swing; a part can have enough GBW and still distort large signals.
