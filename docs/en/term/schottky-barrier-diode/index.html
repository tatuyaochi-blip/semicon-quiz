What is Schottky barrier diode?

A diode that uses a metal-semiconductor junction (Schottky junction). It has a low forward voltage and switches fast because there is no minority-carrier storage.

Japanese name: ショットキーバリアダイオード

Field: PN junctions and diodes

How the barrier forms

When a metal contacts a lightly doped semiconductor, usually n-type, electrons flow until the Fermi levels align, leaving a depletion region in the semiconductor and an energy barrier at the interface called the Schottky barrier. Ideally its height is the metal work function minus the semiconductor electron affinity, but in practice interface states partly pin it, so the measured barrier depends less on the metal than this rule predicts. Forward current flows by thermionic emission of majority carriers over the barrier, rising exponentially with voltage like a pn diode, but with no minority-carrier injection.

Schottky vs. pn diode

Because the barrier is lower than the built-in potential of a silicon pn junction, a Schottky diode conducts at about 0.2–0.4 V instead of roughly 0.7 V. Since current is carried by majority carriers only, no minority charge is stored, so reverse recovery is almost absent and switching is fast. The price is reverse leakage that is much larger than a pn diode's and rises steeply with temperature. A lower barrier gives lower forward voltage but higher leakage, so barrier height is a design trade-off. Silicon Schottky diodes are practical only up to about 200 V.

Schottky contact vs. ohmic contact

The same metal can form either a rectifying or an ohmic contact depending on doping. On lightly doped silicon the depletion region is wide and carriers must go over the barrier, giving diode behavior. On heavily doped silicon the depletion region becomes so thin that electrons tunnel straight through, and the contact behaves like a low resistance. This is why transistor source, drain and contact regions are heavily doped, and why a Schottky diode needs a lightly doped drift layer under the metal, which also sets its breakdown voltage.

Where you will meet it

Silicon Schottky diodes are standard rectifiers in low-voltage switching power supplies, where their low forward drop and fast recovery cut losses. SiC (silicon carbide) Schottky diodes rated roughly 600–1700 V serve as freewheeling diodes in inverters and power-factor-correction circuits; because they have almost no reverse-recovery current, they also reduce the turn-on loss of the complementary switch. A Schottky diode across a bipolar transistor's base-collector junction keeps it out of deep saturation, the trick behind the 74S and 74LS Schottky TTL logic families.

Answer: B. It uses a metal-semiconductor junction, with a low forward voltage and fast switching

A Schottky barrier diode uses a metal-semiconductor (Schottky) junction and operates with majority carriers only. With no minority-carrier storage it has almost no reverse recovery and switches fast, and its forward voltage of about 0.2–0.4 V is lower than a PN junction's, so it is used for rectification in switching power supplies. Answer: B. To keep the transistor out of deep saturation, reducing stored charge and speeding up switching

A Schottky diode conducts at about 0.3 V, lower than a PN junction (about 0.7 V). It therefore diverts excess base current before the base-collector junction becomes deeply forward-biased, keeping the transistor out of deep saturation. Less minority charge is stored in the base, so turn-off is faster; this was used in the 74S and 74LS (Schottky TTL) families for speed and lower power. The trade-off is a slightly higher VCE(sat), about 0.4 V.

FAQ

Why does a Schottky diode have higher reverse leakage than a pn diode?

Its barrier is lower than a pn junction's built-in potential, and reverse current is thermionic emission over that barrier, which is exponentially sensitive to barrier height and temperature.

Does a Schottky diode have reverse recovery time?

Practically none from stored charge, because it conducts with majority carriers only. What remains is a small capacitive current from charging the junction capacitance.

Can Schottky diodes be used at high voltage?

Silicon Schottky diodes are limited to about 200 V, but silicon carbide Schottky diodes are commercially used from about 600 V to 1700 V.
