What is Microlens?

A tiny lens placed over each pixel of an image sensor. It gathers light and directs it efficiently onto the photodiode, raising sensitivity.

Japanese name: マイクロレンズ

Microlens appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Image sensors and optical devices.

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Focusing light onto the photodiode

Only part of each image-sensor pixel is a photodiode that converts light to charge; the rest is occupied by transistors, wiring, isolation between pixels and so on. A microlens is a convex lens a few µm across placed over each pixel, gathering the light entering the pixel and directing it onto the photodiode. This reduces light lost by falling outside the light-sensitive area, raising the effective fill factor and sensitivity. Above the pixel, from the bottom up, sit the wiring layers (in a front-side illuminated sensor), the color filter and the microlens, which is also called an on-chip lens.

How microlenses are made

Microlenses are made all at once on the wafer using semiconductor processes. In a typical method, a transparent resin is patterned by lithography into small islands, one per pixel, then heated and melted so that surface tension rounds it into a lens shape (thermal reflow). In another approach this shape serves as a mask and is transferred by etching into the film below to tailor the lens material and profile. The smaller the gap between neighboring lenses, the more light is collected, so gapless microlenses with almost no gaps are made. An antireflective coating may also be applied to the surface.

Oblique light and pixel scaling

Light from the camera lens enters nearly straight at the center of the sensor but obliquely toward the edges. Left uncorrected, the focused light misses the photodiode, darkening the periphery or spilling into neighboring pixels and mixing colors. Microlenses are therefore shifted progressively toward the center near the edges to compensate (a countermeasure to shading, also called pupil correction). As pixels shrink to around 1 µm, the distance between lens and photodiode becomes relatively large, so backside illumination (BSI) is used to shorten it, and deep trench isolation (DTI) between pixels suppresses light leakage.

Phase-detection AF and related components

Microlenses are also used for autofocus. Placing two photodiodes under one microlens lets them receive separately the light passing through the left and right sides of the camera lens, and the shift between the two images reveals how far out of focus the image is (on-sensor phase-detection AF). A microlens only gathers light: color filters separate colors, IR-cut filters block infrared, and optical low-pass filters blur overly fine detail to suppress moiré. Microlens arrays, in which microlenses are laid out in two dimensions, are also used in optical components other than image sensors, such as LCD projectors and wavefront sensors.

FAQ

What are microlenses for?

They gather the light entering each pixel and direct it efficiently onto the photodiode, which occupies only part of the pixel. This reduces light lost outside the light-sensitive area and raises sensitivity.

Why are microlenses shifted near the edges of the sensor?

Because light from the camera lens arrives obliquely near the edges of the sensor. Shifting the microlenses toward the center lets them focus even oblique light onto the photodiodes, preventing a darkened periphery and color mixing.

Do backside-illuminated sensors still need microlenses?

Yes. In a backside-illuminated sensor the wiring layers are out of the light path, so the light-receiving area is larger, but there are still isolation structures between pixels. Focusing light onto the center of each pixel with a microlens raises sensitivity and suppresses leakage into neighboring pixels.
