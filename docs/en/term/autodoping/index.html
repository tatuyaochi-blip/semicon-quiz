What is Autodoping?

Unintended incorporation into a growing epitaxial layer of dopant from the substrate or elsewhere via the gas phase. It is a problem when growing lightly doped layers on heavily doped substrates.

Japanese name: オートドーピング

Autodoping appears in 4 practice questions across 2 topics, including Epitaxial growth, Silicon wafers.

Field: Epitaxial growth

How dopant travels through the gas

During silicon epitaxy at high temperature in hydrogen, dopant atoms evaporate from exposed heavily doped surfaces: the substrate front before it is covered, its back side and edges, and deposits on the susceptor. These atoms enter the gas boundary layer above the wafer and are incorporated into the growing film along with the intended dopant. The effect is strongest at the start of growth, so the epi layer shows a doping tail near the interface and its overall doping can drift from target.

Autodoping vs. out-diffusion

Out-diffusion moves substrate dopant into the epi layer through the solid by thermal diffusion, producing a graded transition a short distance from the interface that grows with thermal budget. Autodoping moves dopant through the gas phase and can raise doping far from the interface, sometimes throughout a thin layer. Both blur the junction between substrate and epi and effectively thin the lightly doped region, but they are fought with different measures.

Countermeasures

The back side of a heavily doped wafer is commonly sealed with an oxide or polysilicon film so it cannot release dopant. Growing at reduced pressure, several to tens of kPa, speeds gas diffusion so evaporated dopant does not linger near the surface. Purging after the pre-growth hydrogen bake, or depositing a thin undoped cap layer first and purging before continuing, also helps. Lower growth temperatures reduce evaporation, at the cost of growth rate.

Where it matters

Lightly doped epi on heavily doped substrates is standard for vertical power MOSFETs and diodes, which use n+ substrates of about 0.001–0.01 Ω·cm, and for CMOS wafers built on p+ substrates. In both, the epi doping sets breakdown voltage or device behavior, so autodoping directly shifts electrical results. Bipolar processes with patterned buried layers also suffer lateral autodoping, where dopant from a buried region spreads sideways into neighboring areas.

Answer: C. As substrates for growing lightly doped epitaxial layers, and as the drain-side substrate of vertical power devices

Heavily doped, low-resistivity n⁺ wafers (about 0.001–0.01 Ω·cm) serve as substrates for epitaxial wafers with lightly doped epi layers on top. In vertical power MOSFETs and diodes, current flows down through the substrate, so low substrate resistance reduces on-resistance. Arsenic, antimony and phosphorus are chosen based on segregation and evaporation behavior, and suppressing out-diffusion into the epi layer (autodoping) is a challenge.

Answer: A. Autodoping

Autodoping occurs when dopant evaporates from a heavily doped substrate—its back side or edges included—and is reincorporated through the gas phase into the growing layer. Strongest early in growth, it shifts epi doping from target and smears the transition at the interface. Countermeasures include sealing the backside with oxide or polysilicon, growing at reduced pressure and purging before growth.

FAQ

What causes autodoping in epitaxial growth?

Dopant evaporates from heavily doped surfaces such as the substrate back side, edges or susceptor at growth temperature, travels through the gas and is reincorporated into the growing layer.

Why is backside sealing used on epitaxial substrates?

An oxide or polysilicon film on the back of a heavily doped wafer blocks dopant evaporation from that surface, which is one of the largest sources of autodoping.

Is autodoping the same as out-diffusion?

No. Autodoping transfers dopant through the gas phase during growth, while out-diffusion is solid-state diffusion from the substrate across the interface during high-temperature steps.
