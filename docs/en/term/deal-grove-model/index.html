What is Deal-Grove model?

A model of thermal oxidation in which the oxidant diffuses through the oxide and reacts with silicon at the Si/SiO2 interface. Thickness grows linearly with time for thin oxides and with the square root of time for thick ones.

Japanese name: Deal-Groveモデル

Three fluxes in series

The Deal-Grove model, published in 1965, treats thermal oxidation as three steps in series: oxidant moves from the gas to the oxide surface, diffuses through the existing oxide, and reacts with silicon at the Si/SiO2 interface. In steady state the three fluxes are equal, and dividing the flux by the number of oxidant molecules incorporated per unit volume of oxide gives the growth rate. Solving this yields x² + Ax = B(t + τ), where x is oxide thickness, B is the parabolic rate constant, B/A is the linear rate constant and τ accounts for any oxide present at the start.

Linear and parabolic regimes

When the oxide is thin, oxidant crosses it easily and the interface reaction limits growth, so x ≈ (B/A)t: thickness grows linearly with time. When the oxide is thick, diffusion through it limits growth, so x² ≈ Bt and thickness grows with the square root of time; doubling the thickness then takes about four times as long. The crossover occurs near a thickness of A/2, from tens of nanometers to about 100 nm for dry oxidation depending on temperature. Both constants rise steeply with temperature and roughly in proportion to oxidant partial pressure.

What the model explains

Because new oxide forms at the interface, the interface is always freshly made, and silicon is consumed: about 0.44 of the final oxide thickness comes from silicon below the original surface. Wet oxidation in steam is much faster than dry oxidation in oxygen, largely because water dissolves in SiO2 at far higher concentration, which is why thick isolation or masking oxides are grown wet and at high pressure, while denser dry oxides were preferred for gates. The linear constant depends on crystal orientation, with (111) silicon oxidizing faster than (100), while the parabolic constant does not.

Where it breaks down

For dry oxidation below a few tens of nanometers, real growth is faster than the model predicts. Proposed explanations include additional oxidant transport mechanisms in very thin films and stress effects at the interface. The original model handles this by assuming an effective initial thickness, roughly 20 to 25 nm for dry oxygen, but gate oxides only a few nanometers thick still require empirical calibration. Heavy doping, chlorine additions and two-dimensional geometries such as corners, where stress slows growth, also need corrections.

The Deal-Grove model (1965) treats oxidation as oxidant entering the oxide surface from the gas, diffusing through the film and reacting with silicon at the interface to form new oxide. Oxide therefore grows at the interface, which is always freshly formed, and growth slows as the diffusion path lengthens. As the oxide thickens, diffusion through it becomes limiting and thickness follows the square root of time (rate constant B). FAQ

What are the linear and parabolic rate constants in the Deal-Grove model?

B/A is the linear rate constant, governing thin oxides where the interface reaction limits growth. B is the parabolic rate constant, governing thick oxides where diffusion through the oxide limits growth.

Why is wet oxidation faster than dry oxidation?

Water vapor dissolves in SiO2 at a much higher concentration than oxygen, so far more oxidant reaches the interface. Both rate constants are therefore larger for wet oxidation.

How much silicon is consumed during thermal oxidation?

About 0.44 of the grown oxide thickness, so growing 100 nm of SiO2 consumes roughly 44 nm of silicon.
