What is Negative feedback?

Returning part of the output to the input with opposite polarity. Gain drops, but it becomes stable, and distortion and bandwidth improve.

Japanese name: 負帰還

Negative feedback appears in 4 practice questions across 2 topics, including Analog and electronic circuits, Bipolar transistors.

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

How negative feedback works

An amplifier with open-loop gain A has a fraction β of its output fed back and subtracted from the input. The closed-loop gain becomes A/(1 + Aβ), and when the loop gain Aβ is much larger than 1 this reduces to about 1/β. Gain is then set by passive parts, such as a resistor ratio, rather than by transistor or op-amp gain, which drifts with temperature, supply voltage and process. The amplifier itself only sees the small error left after subtraction, which is why the two inputs of an op-amp with feedback sit at almost the same voltage (the virtual short).

What you get in exchange for gain

The factor (1 + loop gain) is the exchange rate. Gain drops by that factor, and in return sensitivity to changes in A, nonlinear distortion and, when the output voltage is sampled, output impedance all shrink by roughly the same factor. Bandwidth widens: for an amplifier dominated by a single pole, the gain-bandwidth product stays nearly constant, so lowering the closed-loop gain extends the bandwidth in proportion. Depending on whether the feedback is mixed in series or in shunt at the input, input impedance is raised or lowered by the same factor.

Negative vs. positive feedback

Feedback is negative only as long as the loop does not add too much phase shift. If the phase lag around the loop reaches 180 degrees at a frequency where loop gain is still 1 or more, subtraction turns into addition and the circuit oscillates. Designers therefore check phase margin, usually aiming for 45 to 60 degrees or more. Positive feedback is used on purpose where snap action is wanted: comparators with hysteresis, Schmitt triggers, latches and oscillators. Because those circuits do not settle in a linear state, the virtual-short assumption does not apply to them.

Where you will meet it

Op-amp amplifiers, filters and voltage regulators all rely on an overall feedback loop. Local feedback is just as common: in a discrete bipolar stage, an emitter resistor raises the emitter voltage when collector current increases, which lowers VBE and pulls the current back, stabilizing the bias point against temperature and hFE spread. A bypass capacitor across that resistor removes the feedback at signal frequencies to restore AC gain. Source degeneration in MOSFET stages and phase-locked loops use the same principle.

  2. BGain becomes infinite

Answer: A. Gain is reduced, but gain stability improves, bandwidth widens and distortion decreases

Negative feedback subtracts part of the output from the input, reducing gain. In return, closed-loop gain becomes about 1/β (the inverse of the feedback fraction), largely independent of device variation and temperature; bandwidth widens; and distortion and output impedance shrink by a factor of (1 + loop gain). Op-amp circuits are built on this. At frequencies where the phase shifts enough to make feedback positive, however, the loop oscillates, so phase margin must be secured.

In an ideal op-amp circuit with negative feedback, what is the property called by which the two input terminals have almost the same voltage?

  1. ASlew rate
  2. BOffset
  3. CVirtual short
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Virtual short

When an op-amp with huge open-loop gain A has negative feedback, the output settles at a finite value, so the voltage between its inputs is Vout/A—essentially zero. The inputs look shorted without being connected, hence "virtual short"; with the non-inverting input grounded, the inverting input sits at 0 V (virtual ground). This is the foundation of op-amp analysis, but it fails when the output saturates or in positive-feedback circuits such as comparators.

FAQ

Why does negative feedback reduce amplifier gain?

Part of the output is subtracted from the input, so the amplifier only amplifies a small error signal. The closed-loop gain becomes A/(1 + Aβ), which approaches 1/β when the loop gain is large.

Can negative feedback make an amplifier oscillate?

Yes. If the phase lag around the loop reaches 180 degrees while the loop gain is still at least 1, the feedback becomes positive and the circuit oscillates; adequate phase margin prevents this.

What is loop gain in a feedback amplifier?

Loop gain is the product Aβ, the gain a signal experiences going once around the feedback loop. The larger it is, the more closely the closed-loop gain approaches 1/β and the more distortion and drift are suppressed.
