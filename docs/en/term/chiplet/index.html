What is Chiplet?

A small chip made for a specific function. Several chiplets are connected inside one package to build a larger system.

Japanese name: チップレット

Field: Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets)

Chip terms in the news · In the news: March 2022 – April 2026

In a nutshell

Chiplets are a way of building a product from small chips split up by function and connected inside one package, instead of one large chip. Because they help with yield and cost, they are used in AI processors and PC CPUs.

Why it is in the news

  • March 2022 Ten companies (AMD, Arm, ASE, Google Cloud, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Qualcomm, Samsung and TSMC) announced UCIe 1.0, a standard for connecting chiplets to each other.
  • August 5, 2025 The UCIe industry consortium released UCIe 3.0, raising the data rate to up to 64 GT/s (twice that of UCIe 2.0).
  • January 2026 At CES 2026, Intel announced Core Ultra Series 3 (code name Panther Lake), which combines a CPU tile made on Intel 18A with other tiles made on Intel 3 and TSMC N3E and N6.
  • April 2026 Rapidus opened Rapidus Chiplet Solutions in Chitose, which handles the design and manufacture of chiplets and packages.

Scaling alone no longer delivers easy performance gains, and the larger a chip gets, the harder yield and cost become. That has drawn attention to chiplets, which combine chips made on different processes, and to the advanced packaging that supports them.

Why split a system into chiplets

Yield falls steeply with die area, because the chance that a die contains no killer defect drops roughly exponentially as area grows. In a simple Poisson model with 0.1 defects per cm², an 8 cm² die yields about 45%, while a 2 cm² die yields about 82%, and only defective small dies are discarded. Chiplets also allow each function to use the best process, leading-edge nodes for compute logic and mature nodes for I/O and analog, and they allow one design to be reused across several products. They also get around the reticle limit on maximum die size.

How chiplets are connected

The simplest approach places dies side by side on an organic package substrate, with relatively coarse wiring. For higher density, 2.5D integration routes signals through a silicon interposer or small embedded silicon bridges, using micro-bumps with pitches of a few tens of micrometers. 3D stacking places dies on top of each other, and hybrid bonding pushes connection pitch below roughly 10 µm. Finer pitch allows more wires per millimeter of die edge, raising bandwidth density in Gb/s/mm, while short in-package links can cut energy per bit by about an order of magnitude compared with board-level links.

Standard interfaces: UCIe

Early chiplet products used proprietary die-to-die links, so all dies came from one company. UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express), announced in 2022 by a group of semiconductor companies, aims to make chiplets from different suppliers and processes interoperable. It defines the physical layer, including bump layout and signaling for both standard organic packages and advanced packages, a die-to-die adapter layer, and the mapping of protocols such as PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express) and CXL (Compute Express Link) onto the link.

Costs and trade-offs vs. a monolithic die

Chiplets are not automatically cheaper or faster. Each die-to-die interface consumes area and power, crossing between dies adds latency compared with on-die wiring, and advanced packaging adds cost and its own assembly yield loss. Each chiplet must be thoroughly tested before assembly, as known good die (KGD), because one bad die scraps the whole package. Heat from stacked or closely packed dies is harder to remove, and the design and verification flow must cover the package. Chiplets pay off mainly for large, high-volume or highly varied product families.

How it differs from existing technology

A conventional monolithic SoC builds every function on a single piece of silicon. With chiplets, the CPU, GPU, I/O and so on become separate chips, and each can use the process that suits it. Small chips are less likely to hit a defect, so yield improves, but there is a trade-off: the power and latency of the die-to-die wiring and the complexity of the package increase.

Answer: C. The best process can be chosen for each function, small chips raise yield, and designs are easier to reuse

Chiplets let each function use its best process, such as leading-edge nodes for performance-critical logic and mature nodes for I/O and analog. Small dies yield better, and the same chiplet can be reused across multiple products in different combinations. Die-to-die interfaces and packaging add cost, however, so chiplets are not always cheaper or faster, and design work is still required.

FAQ

What is the difference between a chiplet and an SoC?

A monolithic system on chip (SoC) puts all functions on one die, whereas a chiplet design splits them across several dies connected inside one package, which can improve yield and allow different processes per function.

What is UCIe?

UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express) is an open die-to-die interconnect standard announced in 2022, defining physical, adapter and protocol layers so chiplets from different companies can be combined in one package.

Why do chiplets improve yield?

Smaller dies are less likely to contain a defect, and dies are tested before assembly, so a defect scraps only one small die instead of a large monolithic chip.
