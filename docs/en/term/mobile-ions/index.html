What is Mobile ions?

Ions such as sodium and potassium that can move within the gate oxide. They drift with field and temperature and shift the threshold voltage. Because they can come from sources such as human sweat, they are tightly controlled.

Which ions move and why

Mobile ions are alkali metal ions, chiefly sodium (Na+) and potassium (K+), that can migrate through silicon dioxide because they are small and only weakly bound in the oxide network. At room temperature they move slowly, but at moderately elevated temperatures, around 200 °C, and under an electric field they drift readily across a gate oxide. Common sources are human sweat and skin, process chemicals, tools, quartzware and furnace components, which is why the semiconductor industry treats alkali metals as critical contaminants.

How mobile ions shift the threshold voltage

A charge in the gate oxide shifts the flat-band voltage, and therefore the threshold voltage, by roughly the charge divided by the oxide capacitance, weighted by how close the charge sits to the silicon. Ions near the gate electrode have little effect, but under positive gate bias positive ions drift toward the Si/SiO2 interface, where they have full effect and lower the nMOS threshold voltage. Reversing the bias pulls them back, so the shift is reversible and depends on the bias and temperature history, the signature that distinguishes mobile ions from fixed oxide charge.

Measuring mobile ion contamination

The standard check is a bias-temperature stress test on a MOS capacitor. The capacitor is heated, commonly to about 200 °C or more, with a positive gate bias applied, then cooled under bias and measured with a capacitance-voltage (C–V) sweep; the procedure is repeated with negative bias. The difference in flat-band voltage between the two states gives the mobile ion charge per area. Monitor wafers processed through furnaces and other tools are tested this way to detect contamination sources early.

How the industry brought them under control

Mobile ions were the main cause of unstable MOS transistors in the 1960s, and several countermeasures made MOS technology practical. Cleanroom discipline, gloves and high-purity chemicals limit sources, and metal-free chemicals such as TMAH replace sodium hydroxide as photoresist developer. Adding a chlorine source such as HCl during thermal oxidation removes sodium as volatile chlorides or immobilizes it in the oxide. Phosphosilicate glass (PSG) layers getter sodium, and silicon nitride films act as barriers in passivation layers.

FAQ

Why is sodium contamination bad for MOS transistors?

Sodium ions move through the gate oxide under electric fields and heat, and when they accumulate near the silicon interface their positive charge shifts the threshold voltage, making devices unstable.

What is the difference between mobile ions and fixed oxide charge?

Mobile ions can drift through the oxide, so the threshold shift they cause changes with bias and temperature and can be reversed. Fixed oxide charge stays near the interface and gives a constant shift.

Why is HCl added during gate oxidation?

Chlorine binds alkali and metal contaminants, removing them as volatile chlorides or immobilizing them in the oxide, which reduces mobile ion effects and improves oxide reliability.
