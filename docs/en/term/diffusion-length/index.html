What is Diffusion length?

The average distance minority carriers diffuse before recombining, given by L = √(Dτ) from the diffusion coefficient D and lifetime τ. It strongly affects the efficiency of solar cells and photodetectors.

Japanese name: 拡散長

Diffusion length appears in 3 practice questions across 3 topics, including Semiconductor physics, Bipolar transistors, Oxidation and diffusion.

Field: Semiconductor physics

Where L = √(Dτ) comes from

When excess minority carriers are injected at one boundary of a neutral region, for example at the edge of a forward-biased pn junction or where light is absorbed, they spread out by diffusion while recombining at a rate set by their lifetime τ. Solving the steady-state continuity equation gives an exponential decay, Δn(x) = Δn(0)·exp(−x/L), with L = √(Dτ). The diffusion length is thus the distance over which the excess carrier density falls to 1/e of its starting value. The units check out: √(cm²/s × s) gives centimeters.

Typical values

The diffusion coefficient follows from the mobility through the Einstein relation, D = μkT/q. Electrons in lightly doped silicon have D of about 35 cm²/s, holes about 12 cm²/s. With D = 36 cm²/s and τ = 10 µs, L = √(3.6 × 10⁻⁴ cm²) ≈ 190 µm. Lifetime varies enormously, from nanoseconds in heavily doped or contaminated material to milliseconds in very pure silicon, so L ranges from below a micrometer to millimeters. Heavy doping shortens L twice over, since both the mobility and the lifetime fall.

Solar cells and photodetectors

A light-generated carrier contributes current only if it reaches the junction's electric field before recombining. Carriers generated within roughly one diffusion length of the depletion region are collected efficiently, while those created deeper are mostly lost. Long-wavelength light is absorbed deep in silicon, so red and infrared response depends strongly on L. A diffusion length longer than the absorber thickness helps collect nearly all carriers. Metal impurities and crystal defects that create recombination centers cut the lifetime and hence L, which is why wafer purity matters so much for solar cells.

Not the same as dopant diffusion length

The same square-root form appears in process engineering as √(Dt), the characteristic distance dopant atoms move during a thermal step of time t. That D describes atoms, not carriers, and is many orders of magnitude smaller: about 2 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s for boron in silicon at 1000 °C, giving about 0.1 µm in an hour. In bipolar transistors the carrier diffusion length matters again: the base is made much thinner than L so that few injected carriers recombine before reaching the collector, which keeps the current gain high.

Answer: B. L = √(D × τ)

The diffusion length is roughly how far a minority carrier diffuses before recombining: L = √(Dτ). For example, D = 36 cm²/s and τ = 10 µs give L = √(3.6×10⁻⁴) ≈ 190 µm. Checking units, √(cm²/s × s) = cm, so this is the only option that yields a length. In solar cells, a diffusion length longer than the cell thickness helps collect light-generated carriers; in bipolar transistors the base width is made much shorter than the diffusion length.

FAQ

What is the formula for minority carrier diffusion length?

L = √(Dτ), where D is the minority carrier diffusion coefficient and τ is the minority carrier lifetime. For example, D = 36 cm²/s and τ = 10 µs give about 190 µm.

Why does diffusion length matter for solar cell efficiency?

Only carriers that diffuse to the junction before recombining produce current. A longer diffusion length means carriers generated deep in the cell are still collected, raising the photocurrent.

Does higher doping increase or decrease diffusion length?

It decreases it. Heavier doping lowers mobility, and therefore D, and also shortens the minority carrier lifetime through faster recombination.
