What is Plasma?

A state in which a gas is ionized into a mixture of electrons, ions and radicals (reactive neutrals). It is used in many processes, including dry etching, plasma CVD and ashing.

Japanese name: プラズマ

Plasma appears in 54 practice questions across 14 topics, including Etching, Deposition (CVD and PVD), Process materials (gases and chemicals).

Field: Etching

What is inside a process plasma

Process plasmas are weakly ionized: only a small fraction of the gas molecules are ionized, while the rest are neutral molecules and radicals. An applied radio-frequency or microwave field accelerates the light electrons to energies of a few electronvolts, and their collisions break up and ionize the gas, while the heavy ions and neutrals stay relatively cool. This non-equilibrium state lets chemistry that would otherwise need hundreds of degrees run on a cool wafer. Between the glowing plasma and any surface forms a thin sheath whose electric field accelerates positive ions toward the wafer.

Radicals vs. ions

The two active ingredients do different jobs. Radicals are electrically neutral, chemically reactive fragments that drift in all directions and react isotropically. Ions are accelerated across the sheath and strike the wafer nearly vertically, adding physical energy and directionality. Reactive ion etching combines them to get vertical profiles, and the Bosch process for deep silicon etching takes this further by alternating SF6 etch steps with C4F8 passivation steps every few seconds; ions clear the passivation only at the bottom, giving trenches with aspect ratios above 20 and small sidewall ripples called scallops.

Where you will meet it

Plasma appears throughout the fab. Plasma CVD (PECVD) deposits films at about 300 to 400 °C instead of the 600 to 800 °C of thermal CVD, and high-density plasma CVD combines deposition with ion sputtering to fill narrow gaps. Magnetron sputtering confines electrons with magnets to sustain a dense argon plasma at about 0.1 to 1 Pa. Oxygen plasma ashing strips photoresist. Plasma nitridation and radical oxidation modify thin dielectrics at low temperature, plasma doping implants dopants at sub-keV energies, and ion implanters extract their ions from a plasma source.

Side effects to manage

Plasmas also cause problems. Charge collecting on isolated gates or resist can stress gate oxides only a few nanometres thick; in ion implantation a plasma flood gun supplies low-energy electrons to neutralize this charge. Energetic ion bombardment can damage crystals, and PECVD films usually contain hydrogen from the precursors, so their quality is below that of thermal CVD films. Plasma density and gas flow must be uniform across the wafer to keep etch and deposition rates even. Note that the plasma dispersion effect in silicon photonics refers to free carriers in a solid, not to a gas discharge.

Answer: C. Plasma nitridation

Plasma nitridation introduces active nitrogen from a nitrogen plasma into the upper part of an oxide at relatively low temperatures (a few hundred °C), forming SiON with about 10–20% nitrogen. Because nitrogen concentrates near the top surface, it blocks boron penetration and raises permittivity to thin the EOT without degrading Si/SiO2 interface properties such as mobility. Thermal nitridation (NO or N2O anneals) instead piles nitrogen at the interface.

Answer: B. High doses at low energy are easy, and 3D structures can be doped relatively uniformly

Plasma doping exposes the whole wafer at once rather than scanning a beam, delivering high doses of 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻² quickly even at energies below 1 keV. Ions and radicals arrive from many angles, making it easier to dope the sidewalls of 3D structures such as FinFET fins. Lacking mass analysis, however, it admits unwanted species, and dose measurement and control are more difficult.

FAQ

Why is plasma used in semiconductor manufacturing?

Energetic electrons in a plasma create reactive radicals and ions without heating the wafer, enabling etching, deposition and surface treatment at low temperature. Ions accelerated by the sheath also make vertical, anisotropic etching possible.

What is the difference between radicals and ions in a plasma?

Radicals are neutral, highly reactive species that move randomly and etch isotropically, while ions carry charge, are accelerated by the sheath field and hit the wafer nearly vertically. Dry etching balances the two to control profile and selectivity.

What is plasma damage?

It is degradation caused by the plasma itself, mainly charge buildup that stresses thin gate oxides and lattice damage from ion bombardment. Process tuning, charge neutralization and layout rules are used to limit it.
