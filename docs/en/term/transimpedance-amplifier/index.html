What is Transimpedance amplifier?

An amplifier that converts an input current to a voltage. The current enters the op-amp's inverting input and a feedback resistor turns it into a voltage. It is used to read photodiode signals.

Japanese name: トランスインピーダンスアンプ

Field: Analog and electronic circuits

How the circuit converts current to voltage

A transimpedance amplifier (TIA) feeds the input current into the inverting input of an op-amp whose non-inverting input is grounded. Negative feedback keeps the inverting input at a virtual ground, so the current has nowhere to go except through the feedback resistor Rf, and the output becomes −Iin × Rf. With 1 µA and 1 MΩ the output is 1 V. The gain is therefore measured in ohms, or volts per ampere, which is why it is called a transimpedance. A photodiode connected here sees almost 0 V across it regardless of light level.

TIA vs. a simple load resistor

A photodiode current could be turned into a voltage with just a resistor to ground, but then the diode voltage changes with the signal, and the diode's junction capacitance must charge through that resistor. A large resistor for high gain then means a slow RC response and a light-dependent operating point. In a TIA the op-amp holds the diode voltage nearly constant, so the effective input impedance is roughly Rf divided by the amplifier's open-loop gain. That decoupling of gain from input impedance is what improves both speed and linearity.

Gain, bandwidth and stability

Larger Rf gives more output per microamp and lowers the resistor's relative thermal-noise contribution, but it also slows the circuit. The photodiode and op-amp input capacitance combine with Rf to put a zero in the feedback network's noise gain, which can cause peaking or oscillation. A small capacitor Cf across Rf restores phase margin and sets the bandwidth near 1/(2π Rf Cf). A common first estimate for the achievable bandwidth is the square root of the op-amp gain-bandwidth product divided by 2π Rf times the total input capacitance.

Where you will meet it

Every optical receiver starts with a TIA directly after the photodiode. A received power of about 0.1 mW yields roughly 0.1 mA, too small for the decision circuit, so the TIA converts and amplifies it while providing bandwidths of tens of gigahertz with low noise. It is placed right beside the photodiode to minimize interconnect capacitance, and is usually followed by limiting or variable-gain stages. Slower TIAs with megohm-range feedback resistors read ambient-light sensors, laboratory photodetectors and CT-scanner detector channels.

FAQ

Why is a capacitor placed across the feedback resistor in a transimpedance amplifier?

Photodiode and amplifier input capacitance create a phase shift that can make the loop ring or oscillate. The feedback capacitor adds compensation and also sets the circuit's bandwidth.

What are the units of transimpedance gain?

Ohms, since gain is output voltage divided by input current. A 100 kΩ TIA produces 0.1 V for every microamp of input current.

Why use a TIA instead of a resistor to read a photodiode?

The TIA keeps the photodiode at a nearly fixed voltage, so its capacitance is not repeatedly charged and the response stays fast and linear even with high gain.
