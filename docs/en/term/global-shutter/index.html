What is Global shutter?

An electronic shutter method that exposes all pixels at the same time. Unlike a rolling shutter, which reads row by row, it does not distort images of moving objects.

Japanese name: グローバルシャッター

Field: Image sensors and optical devices

Global versus rolling shutter

The rolling shutter used in many CMOS image sensors resets, exposes and reads the pixels one row at a time. Every row is exposed for the same length of time, but each row starts at a different moment, so the top and bottom of the frame are captured several to tens of milliseconds apart. As a result, fast-moving objects appear slanted, the image wobbles when the camera pans, and flickering fluorescent or LED lights show up as bands. A global shutter starts and ends exposure for all pixels at the same time, so the whole frame shows a single instant and these motion distortions do not occur.

How a global shutter works

Even if all pixels finish exposure together, they still have to be read out row by row. A global shutter pixel therefore transfers the photodiode's charge to an in-pixel storage element at the end of exposure, all pixels at once, and holds it there until its row is read. Storage can be in the charge domain, keeping the charge itself in a dedicated memory node or the floating diffusion, or in the voltage domain, converting it to a voltage held on in-pixel capacitors. Some designs can run the next exposure while the stored frame is being read. Either way, the pixel needs more transistors and storage elements than a rolling-shutter pixel.

Image quality challenges and remedies

The storage element is shielded from light, but light entering at an angle and charge generated in the substrate leak in slightly and add a false signal while the pixel waits to be read. How small this leakage is gets evaluated as parasitic light sensitivity (PLS) or shutter efficiency. Noise also tends to rise from dark current in the storage node and in designs where correlated double sampling, which cancels reset noise, is hard to apply, and the storage element takes area from the photodiode, reducing sensitivity and full-well capacity. Remedies include light-shield and doping designs that reduce leakage, and stacked sensors that move storage capacitors and circuits to a lower chip to preserve photodiode area.

Where global shutters are used

Global shutters are used wherever moving things must be captured accurately: industrial inspection cameras (machine vision) imaging products on conveyors, barcode readers, position estimation for robots and drones, and motion tracking in AR/VR devices. In vehicles they are sometimes used for driver monitoring, which images the driver with short pulses of infrared LED light; aligning every pixel's exposure with a short light pulse is another advantage. Most digital cameras have combined a rolling shutter with a mechanical shutter, but in 2023 a full-frame mirrorless camera with a global shutter sensor was announced.

FAQ

What is the difference between a global shutter and a rolling shutter?

A global shutter exposes all pixels at the same time, so the whole image captures a single instant. A rolling shutter exposes each row at a slightly different time, so fast-moving objects can appear slanted. On the other hand, rolling-shutter pixels are simpler and often have an edge in sensitivity and noise.

How does a global shutter work?

All pixels are reset together to begin exposure, and at the end of exposure every pixel's photodiode charge is transferred at once to a light-shielded storage element inside the pixel. The stored signals are then read out row by row. Exposing simultaneously but reading sequentially yields an image without motion distortion.

What are the drawbacks of a global shutter?

Because each pixel needs a storage element and extra transistors, the light-collecting area shrinks for a given pixel size, which tends to lower sensitivity and dynamic range, and pixels tend to be larger. Parasitic light leaking into storage and higher noise are further challenges. These sensors also tend to cost more.
