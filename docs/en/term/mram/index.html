What is MRAM?

Non-volatile memory that stores data as the difference in resistance caused by the magnetization direction of a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ).

Japanese name: MRAM

MRAM appears in 19 practice questions across 2 topics, including Emerging memories, SRAM and flash memory.

Field: Emerging memories

How a magnetic tunnel junction stores a bit

The storage element is a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ): a fixed reference layer and a switchable free layer, both ferromagnetic, separated by an insulating tunnel barrier about 1 nm thick. When the two magnetizations are parallel, electrons tunnel easily and resistance is low; antiparallel gives high resistance. This tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) distinguishes 0 from 1. Crystalline MgO barriers give TMR ratios of several hundred percent, far above the tens of percent of amorphous Al2O3. Reading passes a small current and compares against a reference; it does not disturb the magnetization, so no write-back is needed.

Writing with spin-transfer torque

In STT-MRAM, write current flows through the MTJ itself. Electrons spin-polarized by the reference layer transfer angular momentum to the free layer and flip it; the current direction selects the state. Unlike early MRAM written by magnetic fields from nearby wires, the STT write current shrinks with cell area, which makes scaling possible. The limit is thermal stability: the energy barrier is anisotropy times free-layer volume, and its ratio to kT, called Δ, must be roughly 60 or more for ten-year retention. Raising Δ raises write current, so perpendicular magnetization materials are used to balance the two.

Embedded MRAM in logic chips

MTJs are built between interconnect layers in the back end of line (BEOL), after the transistors are finished, with only a few extra masks. Embedded flash, by contrast, needs ten or more additional masks for floating gates and becomes impractical beyond about 28 nm or with high-k/metal gates. This makes MRAM a leading replacement for embedded flash in microcontrollers and automotive chips at 22 nm and below. Integration challenges include surviving BEOL thermal steps of about 400 °C and patterning magnetic films without metal redeposition, typically using ion-beam etching.

MRAM vs. other memories

Compared with flash, MRAM writes in a few to tens of nanoseconds without an erase step and endures over 10¹⁰ cycles, versus thousands to tens of thousands for NAND. Unlike DRAM and SRAM it is non-volatile. Its weaknesses are density and read margin: cells are larger than NAND cells and cannot be stacked in 3D, so cost per bit is far higher, and a 2 to 3 times resistance ratio gives smaller read margins than DRAM or flash. FeRAM stores data in ferroelectric polarization instead, and phase-change memory in crystalline versus amorphous resistance.

Answer: A. Through differences in resistance due to the magnetization direction of a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ)

MRAM uses a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ): two magnetic layers sandwiching an insulating layer about 1 nm thick. Parallel magnetizations give low resistance and antiparallel give high resistance (tunnel magnetoresistance), distinguishing 0 from 1, and since magnetization persists without power the memory is non-volatile. Near-unlimited endurance and high speed are its strengths. Crystalline-versus-amorphous resistance is phase-change memory, and stored charge is DRAM.

Answer: C. It can be rewritten far more times, and writes are fast

MRAM stores data in magnetization, so unlike flash—whose oxide wears—it endures far more cycles (over 10¹⁰), writes in a few to tens of ns, and needs no erase step. It is being adopted as embedded memory in microcontrollers and automotive devices and for frequent data logging. Its larger cells, which cannot be stacked, leave it far behind NAND flash in capacity and cost per bit.

FAQ

Is MRAM volatile or non-volatile?

MRAM is non-volatile: data is held in the magnetization direction of the free layer, which persists without power, typically for ten years or more. It combines this with fast writes and very high endurance.

What is the difference between STT-MRAM and toggle MRAM?

Toggle or field-written MRAM flips the free layer with magnetic fields from current in nearby wires, which needs more current as cells shrink. STT-MRAM passes spin-polarized current directly through the MTJ, so write current scales down with cell size.

Why is MRAM replacing embedded flash?

Embedded flash adds many process steps and is hard to integrate beyond about 28 nm or with high-k/metal gates. MRAM is built in the interconnect layers with only a few extra masks and offers faster writes and higher endurance.
