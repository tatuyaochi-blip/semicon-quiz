What is AEC-Q100?

The qualification standard for reliability testing of automotive integrated circuits, defined by the Automotive Electronics Council (AEC). It has operating temperature grades: Grade 1 is −40 to 125 °C and Grade 0 is −40 to 150 °C. Discrete semiconductors are covered by AEC-Q101 and passive components by AEC-Q200.

What the qualification involves

AEC-Q100 is a set of stress tests an integrated circuit must pass before it is accepted for automotive use. Test groups cover accelerated lifetime, package integrity, temperature cycling, humidity, electrostatic discharge (ESD) and latch-up, among others. The best-known item is high temperature operating life (HTOL): parts run at around 125 °C and at least nominal voltage, with 77 units from each of three lots operated for 1,000 hours and zero failures allowed. Temperature and voltage acceleration let those hours stand in for years of field use.

Temperature grades

Each part is qualified to an operating temperature grade defined by ambient temperature. Grade 0 is −40 to 150 °C, Grade 1 is −40 to 125 °C, Grade 2 is −40 to 105 °C and Grade 3 is −40 to 85 °C; the smaller the number, the hotter the upper limit. Because the grade refers to ambient, self-heating means the junction runs hotter still, so thermal design must budget for that rise. Parts are tested hot and cold, since leakage grows at high temperature and timing shifts at low temperature.

AEC-Q100 vs. Q101 and Q200

The Automotive Electronics Council splits its documents by component type because failure mechanisms differ. AEC-Q100 covers integrated circuits such as microcontrollers and power-management ICs. AEC-Q101 covers discrete semiconductors such as diodes, power MOSFETs and IGBTs. AEC-Q200 covers passive components such as resistors, capacitors, inductors and crystal resonators. None of these is a functional safety standard: they demonstrate that a part survives automotive stress, while ISO 26262 governs how safety requirements, safety mechanisms and failure-rate metrics are handled.

Change control after qualification

Qualification applies to a specific design, process, fab and package. Moving production to another fab, changing materials or modifying the package can alter reliability even if datasheet values are unchanged. Suppliers therefore issue a Product Change Notification (PCN) in advance, describing the change and its expected impact, repeat the relevant reliability tests, and switch only after customer agreement. AEC-Q100 includes guidance on which tests to repeat for each type of change. This discipline supports the very low defect rates, aimed at ppb levels, that automotive customers expect.

The AEC standards are divided by component type. Integrated circuits such as microcontrollers and power ICs fall under AEC-Q100; discrete semiconductors such as diodes, power MOSFETs and IGBTs fall under AEC-Q101; and passive components such as resistors, capacitors, inductors and crystal resonators fall under AEC-Q200. Because the components differ in structure and failure behavior, the test items and conditions also differ. For example, automotive multilayer ceramic capacitors use AEC-Q200-compliant types.

FAQ

What is the temperature range of AEC-Q100 Grade 1?

Grade 1 covers an ambient temperature of −40 to 125 °C. Grade 0 extends to 150 °C, Grade 2 to 105 °C and Grade 3 to 85 °C, all starting at −40 °C.

Is AEC-Q100 a certification?

Not in the sense of a certificate issued by an outside body. The supplier runs the specified stress tests and declares the part qualified, providing the data to customers who may review it.

What is the difference between AEC-Q100 and AEC-Q101?

AEC-Q100 is for integrated circuits, while AEC-Q101 is for discrete semiconductors such as diodes, MOSFETs and IGBTs. Their test lists and conditions differ because the devices fail in different ways.
