What is Resonant circuit?

A circuit that behaves in a special way at the frequency at which the reactances of a coil and a capacitor cancel (the resonant frequency f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))). At series resonance the impedance drops to its minimum R and the current peaks; at parallel resonance the impedance peaks and the source current is at its minimum. The sharpness of resonance is expressed by the Q factor, and the bandwidth is f0/Q. Resonant circuits are used in filters, oscillators and radio tuning circuits.

Series and parallel resonance

The impedance of a series RLC circuit is Z = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC)); its imaginary part becomes 0 at ω0 = 1/√(LC), where the current V/R peaks. In a parallel RLC circuit, the imaginary part of the admittance Y = 1/R + j(ωC − 1/(ωL)) becomes 0 and the impedance peaks. With L = 1 mH and C = 10 nF, f0 ≈ 50.3 kHz in both cases.

Q factor and magnification

For series resonance Q = (1/R)√(L/C), and for parallel resonance Q = R√(C/L). At series resonance the voltages across L and C are Q times the source voltage, and at parallel resonance the currents in L and C are Q times the source current. A small source can produce large voltages or currents, but care is needed not to exceed the voltage or current ratings of the parts.

Where they are used

A radio tuning circuit uses LC resonance to pick out only the signal at the desired frequency. LC oscillators and crystal oscillators use the sharpness of resonance to produce a stable frequency. On the other hand, when the parasitic inductance and capacitance of power wiring and components on a printed circuit board form an unintended resonance, noise and voltage fluctuations grow at particular frequencies.

Which is closest to the resonant frequency of a series resonant circuit with L = 1 mH and C = 10 nF?

  1. AAbout 50 kHz
  2. BAbout 316 kHz
  3. CAbout 5 kHz
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. About 50 kHz

The resonant frequency is f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)). Since LC = 1×10⁻³ × 10×10⁻⁹ = 1×10⁻¹¹, √(LC) ≈ 3.16×10⁻⁶ s and f0 ≈ 1/(2π × 3.16×10⁻⁶) ≈ 50.3 kHz. At this frequency the inductor and capacitor reactances are equal, both √(L/C) ≈ 316 Ω. The resonant frequency is inversely proportional to the square root of LC, so making C 100 times larger lowers it to 1/10.

Which is closest to the bandwidth (the difference between the two frequencies at which the current falls to 1/√2 of its resonant value) of a series RLC resonant circuit with R = 10 Ω, L = 1 mH and C = 10 nF?

  1. AAbout 1.6 kHz
  2. BAbout 10 kHz
  3. CAbout 50 kHz
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. About 1.6 kHz

The resonant frequency is f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)) ≈ 50.3 kHz and the Q factor is Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = (1/10) × √(1×10⁻³/(10×10⁻⁹)) ≈ 316/10 ≈ 31.6. The bandwidth is BW = f0/Q ≈ 50.3 kHz/31.6 ≈ 1.59 kHz. For a series RLC circuit it can also be found directly as BW = R/(2πL) = 10/(2π × 1×10⁻³) ≈ 1.59 kHz. A smaller resistance narrows the bandwidth and sharpens frequency selectivity.

FAQ

Why does the current not become infinite at the resonant frequency?

Because the resistance R remains even when the reactances cancel at series resonance. The current is set by V/R, and the smaller R is, the higher the Q and the sharper the current peak. The winding resistance of real coils and the losses of capacitors also act as R.

Does no current flow in L and C at parallel resonance?

It does. The currents in L and C are equal and opposite, so they cancel as seen from the source, but a current Q times the source current circulates between L and C. Energy is exchanged back and forth between the magnetic field of the coil and the electric field of the capacitor.
