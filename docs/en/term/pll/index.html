What is PLL (phase-locked loop)?

A circuit that detects the phase difference from a reference clock and controls an oscillator to produce a clock locked to the reference, for example at an integer multiple of its frequency. It is used to generate CPU clocks and synthesize radio frequencies.

Japanese name: PLL(位相同期回路)

How a PLL locks

A typical PLL contains a phase-frequency detector (PFD), a charge pump, a loop filter, a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) and a feedback divider. The PFD compares the reference clock with the VCO output divided by N and emits up or down pulses proportional to their phase difference. The charge pump turns these into current, which the loop filter integrates into the VCO control voltage. Negative feedback adjusts the VCO until the divided output matches the reference in phase, at which point the output frequency is exactly N times the reference, for example multi-GHz from a 25 MHz crystal.

Integer-N vs. fractional-N

In an integer-N PLL the output can move only in steps equal to the reference frequency, so fine channel spacing forces a low reference and therefore a narrow, slow loop. A fractional-N PLL switches the divide ratio between neighboring integers, typically under control of a delta-sigma modulator, so the average ratio can be a fraction. This gives fine frequency resolution with a high reference frequency. The cost is quantization noise and fractional spurs that the loop filter and careful design must suppress.

Bandwidth, jitter and VCO choice

Loop bandwidth sets a key trade-off. Within the bandwidth, output phase noise follows the reference multiplied by N, which raises it by 20·log10(N) dB; outside, it follows the free-running VCO. A wider loop locks faster and suppresses VCO noise better, but for stability the bandwidth is normally kept well below the reference frequency, often a tenth or less. Ring-oscillator VCOs are compact and tune over a wide range but are noisy; LC-tank VCOs give much lower phase noise at the cost of inductor area.

PLL vs. DLL and where they appear

PLLs generate processor and memory-interface clocks, the local oscillators of radio transceivers, the at-speed test clocks applied after scan loading, and the recovered clock in clock and data recovery (CDR) circuits of serial links. MEMS oscillators also pair a silicon resonator with a PLL to produce programmable frequencies. A delay-locked loop (DLL) is the closest relative: instead of an oscillator it adjusts a delay line to align a clock's phase. Because it does not oscillate, a DLL accumulates no jitter, but it cannot easily multiply frequency.

In smartphone radio circuits, the transceiver including the LNA, mixer and PLL is often integrated on a CMOS chip, while compound semiconductors such as GaAs HBTs are often used for the transmit PA. Which is the most appropriate main reason?

  1. ACMOS transistors do not operate at high frequencies in the GHz range
  2. BScaled CMOS transistors have low breakdown voltages and have difficulty delivering large output power efficiently, whereas GaAs HBTs excel in breakdown voltage and in output and efficiency at high frequencies
  3. CCompound semiconductors are cheaper to manufacture and easier to mass-produce on large wafers
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Scaled CMOS transistors have low breakdown voltages and have difficulty delivering large output power efficiently, whereas GaAs HBTs excel in breakdown voltage and in output and efficiency at high frequencies

Scaled CMOS can integrate many transistors cheaply and handle GHz signals, so it suits combining the LNA, mixer, PLL and even baseband processing on one chip. A PA, on the other hand, must deliver power of several hundred mW with high efficiency and linearity, and scaled CMOS with its low breakdown voltage has difficulty supporting large voltage swings, putting it at a disadvantage in efficiency and output. For this reason, GaAs HBTs are widely used for smartphone PAs, and GaN HEMTs, which are even better in breakdown voltage and heat dissipation, for high-power PAs such as those in base stations. Compound semiconductor wafers are generally smaller and more expensive than silicon, so they are used only where needed.

FAQ

How does a PLL multiply frequency?

A divider in the feedback path divides the VCO output by N before it is compared with the reference. When the loop locks, the divided signal equals the reference frequency, so the VCO runs at N times the reference.

What is the difference between a PLL and a DLL?

A PLL controls an oscillator and can synthesize new frequencies, while a DLL controls a delay line to shift the phase of an existing clock. DLLs are simpler and do not accumulate jitter but generally cannot multiply frequency.

What is a fractional-N PLL?

It is a PLL whose divider alternates between integer ratios so the average ratio is fractional. This allows fine frequency steps without lowering the reference frequency, at the cost of extra spurs and noise.
