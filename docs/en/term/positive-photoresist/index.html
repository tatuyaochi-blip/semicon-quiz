What is Positive photoresist?

A photoresist whose exposed areas dissolve and are removed during development. One whose exposed areas remain is a negative resist.

Japanese name: ポジ型フォトレジスト

Positive photoresist appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Lithography.

Field: Lithography

Why exposed areas dissolve

A positive photoresist is one whose exposed areas change chemically, become soluble in developer and are removed during development. The areas the mask shielded from light remain, so the mask's light and dark pattern is copied as is, as a positive. The classic positive resist for g-line and i-line mixes an alkali-soluble novolak resin with a diazonaphthoquinone (DNQ) photoactive compound that inhibits dissolution. Light converts DNQ into a carboxylic acid that instead promotes dissolution, creating a large difference in dissolution rate between exposed and unexposed areas.

Chemically amplified positive resists

For KrF (248 nm) and ArF (193 nm) exposure, novolak resin absorbs the light too strongly, so chemically amplified resists are used. The soluble groups of the resin are capped with protecting groups, and acid generated from a photoacid generator (PAG) during exposure acts as a catalyst during the post-exposure bake (PEB), removing protecting groups one after another. Deprotected areas become alkali-soluble and are developed in a 2.38% TMAH solution. One photon drives many reactions, giving high sensitivity, but if airborne bases such as ammonia neutralize the acid, defects such as T-topping, where the top of a pattern overhangs, result.

Compared with negative resists

A negative resist works the other way: exposed areas become less soluble, for example through crosslinking, and remain after development. Early negative resists swelled in organic-solvent developers and fine patterns tended to collapse, so positive resists, with their superior resolution, became mainstream. Which is better still depends on the pattern. For narrow trenches and small holes, negative-tone development (NTD), which develops a positive chemically amplified resist in an organic solvent to dissolve the unexposed areas, is also used. The "type" of the resist material and the "tone" of the developed image should be considered separately.

Process considerations

Pattern dimensions in a positive resist depend on dose, focus, PEB temperature and time, and development conditions. Increasing the dose widens the dissolved area, so remaining resist lines get narrower and opened trenches and holes get wider. Standing waves, formed by interference between incident light and light reflected from the wafer, make pattern sidewalls wavy, so antireflective coatings and acid diffusion during PEB are used to smooth them. Because the developed resist becomes the mask for subsequent etching or ion implantation, etch resistance and film thickness are key design factors alongside resolution and sensitivity.

Answer: B. Exposed areas dissolve and are removed during development

In a positive resist, exposed areas become soluble in developer and are removed, leaving the pattern where the mask blocked light. Representative types are novolak resins for i-line and chemically amplified resists for KrF and ArF, developed in alkaline TMAH solution. Their superior resolution makes them the mainstream. Negative resists keep the exposed areas, analogous to photographic negatives and positives.

FAQ

What is the difference between positive and negative photoresist?

In a positive resist, exposed areas dissolve and are removed during development, leaving the areas the mask shielded. In a negative resist, exposed areas become less soluble and remain, and unexposed areas are removed. To make the same pattern, the light and dark areas of the mask are reversed.

Why are positive resists mainstream?

Early negative resists swelled in organic-solvent developers, causing patterns to collapse, and were unsuited to scaling. Positive resists can be developed in aqueous alkaline solution with little swelling and offer superior resolution, so they became mainstream, from novolak resists for i-line to chemically amplified resists for KrF and ArF.

What developer is used for positive resists?

Typically a 2.38% aqueous solution of TMAH (tetramethylammonium hydroxide), which dissolves the areas made alkali-soluble by exposure. There is also negative-tone development, which develops a positive chemically amplified resist in an organic solvent so that, conversely, the unexposed areas dissolve.
