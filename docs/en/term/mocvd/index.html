What is MOCVD (metal-organic chemical vapor deposition)?

A method that grows crystals using metal-organic compounds and source gases such as ammonia. It is widely used to grow compound semiconductors such as GaN and GaAs.

Japanese name: MOCVD(有機金属気相成長)

Field: Epitaxial growth

How MOCVD grows crystals

Metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD), also called MOVPE (metal-organic vapor phase epitaxy), carries group-III metal-organic precursors such as trimethylgallium (TMGa), trimethylaluminum and trimethylindium to a heated substrate together with group-V hydride sources such as ammonia, arsine or phosphine. The molecules decompose at the hot surface and the atoms arrange themselves epitaxially, following the substrate's crystal structure. Metal-organics are held in temperature-controlled bubblers through which hydrogen or nitrogen flows, so bubbler temperature and carrier flow set the delivery rate. Switching gases changes composition at the nanometer scale, enabling quantum wells and heterostructures.

Key growth parameters

Substrate temperature, reactor pressure, growth rate and the V/III ratio, the molar ratio of group-V to group-III precursor, define the process. GaN growth uses V/III ratios of hundreds to thousands, because ammonia cracks inefficiently and nitrogen tends to desorb from the surface at high temperature. The ratio influences surface smoothness, the balance of lateral versus vertical growth, carbon incorporation from the methyl groups and point-defect levels. Production reactors hold dozens of wafers per run, which, together with uniformity and composition control, makes MOCVD the workhorse for compound-semiconductor devices.

The p-type GaN problem

Magnesium is the acceptor for p-type GaN, but layers grown by MOCVD come out highly resistive. Hydrogen from ammonia and the carrier gas bonds to Mg, forming Mg–H complexes that passivate the acceptors. In 1989 Hiroshi Amano and colleagues activated p-type conduction using low-energy electron-beam irradiation, and in 1992 Shuji Nakamura and colleagues showed that annealing at about 700 °C in nitrogen drives the hydrogen out, a method suited to mass production. This understanding opened the way to practical blue LEDs and laser diodes.

MOCVD vs. MBE, LPE and HVPE

Molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) evaporates sources into ultra-high vacuum around 10⁻⁸ Pa, with shutters and in-situ reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) for atomic-layer control, but at about 1 µm per hour it suits research, high-performance HEMTs and quantum devices. Liquid-phase epitaxy (LPE) grows near equilibrium from a metal solution, giving excellent crystals but poor thin-layer control. Hydride vapor phase epitaxy (HVPE) grows GaN tens of times faster, for thick layers and freestanding substrates. MOCVD sits between: thin, abrupt layers with high throughput, though pyrophoric metal-organics and toxic hydrides require careful handling.

Answer: C. Epitaxial growth of compound semiconductors such as GaN

MOCVD reacts metal-organic precursors such as trimethylgallium with hydride gases such as ammonia on a heated substrate to grow compound-semiconductor crystals. Switching gases changes composition on a nanometer scale, and reactors process dozens of wafers per run, giving high throughput. It is widely used for GaN blue and white LEDs, laser diodes, RF and power devices, and solar cells.

FAQ

What is the difference between MOCVD and MBE?

MOCVD grows crystals from chemical precursors flowing over a heated substrate and offers high throughput for production. MBE uses atomic or molecular beams in ultra-high vacuum, giving atomic-layer control and in-situ monitoring but slower growth.

Why is the V/III ratio so high in GaN MOCVD?

Ammonia decomposes inefficiently and nitrogen easily leaves the GaN surface at growth temperature, so a large excess of ammonia is needed to keep the surface nitrogen-rich and stoichiometric.

Why are MOCVD precursors like trimethylgallium hazardous?

Trimethylgallium and similar metal-organics ignite spontaneously in air and react violently with water. The hydride gases used alongside them, such as arsine and phosphine, are also highly toxic.
