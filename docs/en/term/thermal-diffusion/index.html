What is Thermal diffusion?

A doping method that diffuses impurity atoms into a semiconductor at high temperature. They spread from regions of high concentration to low.

Japanese name: 熱拡散

Thermal diffusion appears in 6 practice questions across 3 topics, including Oxidation and diffusion, Ion implantation, Epitaxial growth.

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

How dopants spread

At high temperature, dopant atoms in silicon hop from site to site and drift from high to low concentration, following Fick's laws. The diffusion coefficient D rises steeply with temperature in Arrhenius fashion, so process temperature, typically on the order of 900–1200 °C for classic diffusion, dominates the result, and the characteristic distance scales as the square root of D × t. The same physics continues during every later high-temperature step, which is why total thermal budget is tracked in modern processes to keep junctions from moving.

Predeposition and drive-in

Classic diffusion used two steps. Predeposition holds the surface concentration constant, roughly at the dopant's solid solubility, and introduces a shallow layer whose profile is a complementary error function (erfc) and whose dose grows with time. Drive-in then removes the source and heats the wafer so the fixed dose spreads deeper as a Gaussian profile, with surface concentration falling. Combining the two lets surface concentration and junction depth be chosen fairly independently, although real high-concentration diffusion departs from these ideal shapes.

Sources and masks

Dopants are supplied from gases such as phosphine or diborane, or from liquids carried by bubbling nitrogen through them. Phosphorus oxychloride (POCl3) mixed with oxygen forms a phosphorus-rich glass on the wafer from which phosphorus diffuses, and boron tribromide (BBr3) serves the same role for boron. Furnaces process hundreds of wafers per batch at low cost. Because the temperatures would destroy photoresist, the areas to be protected are masked with silicon dioxide, in which common dopants diffuse much more slowly than in silicon.

Diffusion vs. implantation and epitaxy

Ion implantation replaced diffusion for most IC doping because it runs near room temperature, can use resist masks, controls dose to about ±1% by counting beam current and sets depth independently through energy. It damages the lattice, however, so an anneal follows, and that anneal is itself a short diffusion step. Diffusion always yields profiles peaking at the surface, whereas in-situ doping during epitaxy can create abrupt or buried layers. Thermal diffusion survives in solar-cell emitters and deep junctions in power devices.

Answer: B. It is easier to create abrupt changes in concentration with depth

Thermal diffusion spreads dopants inward from the surface, so it can only produce profiles that peak at the surface and fall off gradually. Epitaxy changes doping layer by layer by switching gas flows, enabling transitions within a few nm, buried heavily doped layers, and even inverted profiles with light doping at the surface. It is indispensable for HBT bases and the multilayer structures of power devices.

Answer: C. Drive-in

Thermal diffusion was often done in two steps: predeposition introduces a set dose shallowly while holding surface concentration constant, then drive-in stops the supply and heats the wafer to spread that fixed dose to the desired depth and profile. Today dopants are usually introduced by ion implantation, which controls dose precisely, followed by anneals for activation and profile shaping, though diffusion remains in use for solar cells and deep junctions in power devices.

FAQ

What is the difference between thermal diffusion and ion implantation?

Diffusion introduces dopants from the surface at high temperature, coupling surface concentration and depth, while implantation fires accelerated ions in at low temperature with independently controlled dose and depth. Implantation then needs an anneal to repair damage and activate the dopants.

Why is a predeposition profile erfc and a drive-in profile Gaussian?

Predeposition solves the diffusion equation with a fixed surface concentration, giving an erfc shape. Drive-in solves it with a fixed total dose and no further supply, giving a Gaussian that broadens with time.

Is thermal diffusion still used?

Yes, for crystalline-silicon solar-cell emitters using POCl3 and for deep junctions in power devices. In CMOS, dopant diffusion during anneals is still a key effect that thermal budgets must control.
