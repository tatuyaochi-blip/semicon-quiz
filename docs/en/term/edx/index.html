What is EDX (energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy)?

An analysis method that measures the energies of characteristic X-rays emitted under an electron beam to identify which elements are present and how much. It is used with SEM or TEM.

Field: Metrology and analysis

How characteristic X-rays are produced and counted

The primary electron beam, typically 5 to 30 keV in an SEM, knocks an inner-shell electron out of an atom. An outer-shell electron drops into the vacancy and the energy difference leaves as an X-ray photon whose energy is fixed by the element, for example about 1.74 keV for the silicon K-alpha line. A solid-state detector, today usually a silicon drift detector (SDD), turns each photon into a charge pulse proportional to its energy. A pulse-height analyzer sorts the pulses into a spectrum of counts versus energy: peak positions identify the elements, and corrected peak areas give their concentrations.

Spatial resolution: SEM versus TEM

In a bulk sample the electrons scatter into a pear-shaped interaction volume that is often a micrometer or more across, so an SEM-based analysis reports the average composition of a region much larger than the beam spot. Lowering the beam energy shrinks this volume but may no longer excite the higher-energy lines of heavier elements. In a TEM or scanning TEM the specimen is a thin lamella, usually well under 100 nm thick and often cut with a focused ion beam (FIB), so the beam hardly spreads and elemental maps with nanometer-scale resolution become possible, which is essential for analyzing advanced device structures.

Limits you need to know

The detection limit is around 0.1 wt%, so EDX sees major constituents, contamination particles and film stoichiometry but not dopants at device-level concentrations. Light elements such as boron, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen emit low-energy X-rays that are easily absorbed, so their quantification is poor and boron is especially difficult. The energy resolution of an SDD is roughly 130 eV, so some lines overlap; a well-known case in chip analysis is silicon K-alpha and the tungsten M line near 1.77 keV. Insulating samples can charge up, and sensitive films can be damaged by the beam.

EDX versus SIMS and other analysis methods

EDX is quick, largely non-destructive and shows composition exactly where the microscope is looking, which makes it the first choice for identifying particles and failure sites. Secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) sputters the sample with primary ions and mass-analyzes the ejected ions, reaching ppm to ppb sensitivity and giving depth profiles, but it consumes the sample. Wavelength-dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) resolves overlapping peaks better but is slower. Spreading resistance profiling measures electrically active carriers, and infrared absorption (FTIR) measures interstitial oxygen in silicon, neither of which EDX can do.

Answer: B. The kinds and amounts of elements in the sample

When an electron beam ejects an inner-shell electron, an outer electron dropping into the vacancy emits a characteristic X-ray whose energy is unique to the element. EDX analyzes these energies to identify elements and their amounts. Because it reveals composition right where the microscope is looking, it is widely used to analyze particles and failure sites. Its detection limit is about 0.1 wt%, so trace impurities require techniques such as SIMS.

FAQ

What is the difference between EDX and EDS?

They are the same technique. EDX (energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy) and EDS (energy-dispersive spectroscopy) are used interchangeably, and some texts write XEDS.

Can EDX measure dopant concentration in silicon?

No. Typical dopant levels are far below the roughly 0.1 wt% detection limit of EDX. Use SIMS for the atomic concentration profile and electrical methods such as spreading resistance profiling for the active carrier concentration.

Is EDX analysis destructive?

The measurement itself usually leaves the sample intact, although the beam can charge insulators or damage sensitive films. TEM-based EDX, however, requires cutting a thin lamella, which destroys that part of the device.
