What is Chemically amplified resist?

A resist in which acid generated by exposure catalyzes a chain of reactions during the post-exposure bake (PEB). It needs little exposure dose and is widely used from KrF onward, but is sensitive to basic contamination.

Japanese name: 化学増幅型レジスト

Field: Lithography

How chemical amplification works

A chemically amplified resist (CAR) contains a polymer whose alkali-soluble groups are blocked by acid-labile protecting groups, plus a photoacid generator (PAG). Exposure produces only a small amount of acid. During the post-exposure bake (PEB), typically around 90–130 °C, each acid molecule catalytically removes protecting groups again and again, tens to hundreds of times, without being consumed. In a positive-tone CAR the deprotected regions become soluble in aqueous TMAH developer. One absorbed photon therefore drives many chemical events, which is the amplification.

Why KrF, ArF and EUV need it

Older i-line resists based on novolak and a diazonaphthoquinone sensitizer produce at most one photochemical event per absorbed photon, and novolak absorbs too strongly at deep-UV wavelengths. Excimer laser and EUV sources deliver comparatively little usable dose, so throughput depends on resist sensitivity. Chemical amplification, a concept developed at IBM in the 1980s, made practical doses possible and became standard from KrF (248 nm) onward. ArF resists use different, transparent polymer platforms, but the acid-catalyzed mechanism is the same.

Acid diffusion and base contamination

Because the image is formed by acid moving during PEB, diffusion blurs the latent image and roughens line edges, so PEB temperature and time are tightly controlled and base quenchers are added to limit how far acid travels. The same sensitivity makes CARs vulnerable to airborne bases such as amines. Contaminants neutralize acid at the resist surface, leaving an insoluble skin that develops into T-shaped profiles, called T-topping, and the effect worsens with delay between exposure and PEB. Lithography tracks therefore use chemical filters to remove amines.

CAR vs. non-amplified resists

The trade-off is often summarized as resolution, line-edge roughness and sensitivity: improving one tends to worsen another, a tension that is especially visible in EUV. A positive CAR can also be developed in an organic solvent that dissolves the unexposed regions instead, called negative-tone development (NTD), which gives better image contrast for small contact holes and narrow trenches. Non-amplified resists, such as novolak for i-line, have no acid diffusion step and are simpler to control but need far higher doses at short wavelengths.

Answer: C. Negative-tone development (NTD)

Negative-tone development uses a positive chemically amplified resist but develops it in an organic solvent (such as butyl acetate) that dissolves the unexposed areas, leaving the exposed ones. For narrow trenches and small holes, keeping mask bright areas small (dark-field) gives better image contrast, so NTD is used with ArF immersion for contact holes and wiring trenches. Reduced resist swelling is another advantage.

FAQ

What is a photoacid generator in a chemically amplified resist?

A photoacid generator (PAG) is a compound that releases a strong acid when it absorbs exposure light; that acid then catalyzes the deprotection reaction during the post-exposure bake.

Why is the post-exposure bake so critical for chemically amplified resists?

The amplification reaction and acid diffusion both happen during PEB, so small changes in its temperature or time change the dose response and the printed linewidth.

What causes T-topping in photoresist?

T-topping occurs when airborne bases such as amines neutralize the photogenerated acid at the top of a chemically amplified resist, leaving an insoluble surface layer that overhangs the developed pattern.
