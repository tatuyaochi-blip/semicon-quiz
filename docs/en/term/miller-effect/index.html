What is Miller effect?

The effect by which a capacitance between the input and output of an inverting amplifier appears (1 + gain) times larger from the input. It degrades the high-frequency response of common-emitter and common-source stages.

Japanese name: ミラー効果

Miller effect appears in 6 practice questions across 4 topics, including Bipolar transistors, MOSFET, Analog and electronic circuits.

Field: Bipolar transistors

Why the capacitance looks larger

Consider a capacitor C connected between the input and output of an inverting amplifier with voltage gain −Av. When the input rises by v, the output falls by Av·v, so the voltage across the capacitor changes by (1 + Av)·v. The input must therefore supply (1 + Av) times the charge it would for a grounded capacitor, and the capacitor appears at the input as C(1 + Av). Seen from the output the same capacitor looks roughly like C(1 + 1/Av), close to C itself. This equivalent input capacitance is often called the Miller capacitance.

Effect on amplifier bandwidth

In a common-emitter stage the base-collector capacitance Cμ is multiplied this way; in a common-source stage it is the gate-drain capacitance Cgd, which includes gate-to-drain overlap capacitance. With Cμ = 2 pF and a gain of 100, the input sees about 200 pF. Together with the source resistance Rs it forms a low-pass pole near 1/(2π·Rs·C_Miller), which often sets the upper bandwidth of the stage. Higher gain therefore directly costs bandwidth, which is why high-gain inverting stages driven from high-impedance sources are slow.

Ways to suppress it

The classic remedy is the cascode: a common-base (or common-gate) transistor stacked on the input device holds the input transistor's collector or drain at a nearly constant voltage. The input device then has a voltage gain of only about one, so its feedback capacitance is barely multiplied, while the overall stage keeps high gain. A common-base stage alone also avoids the problem because its base is AC-grounded. Lowering the driving source resistance raises the pole frequency, and choosing devices with small feedback capacitance helps as well.

When the Miller effect is useful

The same multiplication is exploited on purpose in op-amp frequency compensation. A small capacitor placed across a high-gain inverting stage behaves like a large one, creating a dominant pole at a low frequency so that the gain falls below unity before other poles add much phase shift. This keeps the amplifier stable even as a unity-gain follower without needing a physically large capacitor on chip. In power MOSFET switching, the Miller effect also explains the plateau in gate voltage while the drain voltage swings and Cgd is being charged.

Answer: A. The base-collector capacitance appears, from the input, as a much larger capacitance in proportion to the voltage gain

In a common-emitter stage the collector swings strongly opposite to the base, so the base-collector capacitance Cμ appears about (1 + |Av|) times larger from the input. With Cμ = 2 pF and a gain of 100 it looks like about 200 pF, forming a low-pass filter with the source resistance and narrowing the bandwidth. Remedies include a cascode, which stacks a common-base stage to take the gain at the collector side, and lowering the source resistance.

FAQ

What is Miller capacitance?

Miller capacitance is the enlarged input capacitance, about C(1 + |Av|), that a capacitor bridging the input and output of an inverting amplifier presents to the signal source. For transistors the bridging capacitor is usually Cμ (bipolar) or Cgd (MOSFET).

Why does a cascode amplifier reduce the Miller effect?

In a cascode the input transistor drives the low impedance of a common-base or common-gate device, so its own voltage gain is about one. The feedback capacitance is then multiplied only by about two instead of by the full stage gain.

Does the Miller effect occur in non-inverting amplifiers?

The large multiplication requires an inverting gain; with a follower whose gain is near +1, the voltage across the bridging capacitor barely changes and its effective input capacitance actually shrinks. That is why emitter and source followers have good high-frequency behavior.
