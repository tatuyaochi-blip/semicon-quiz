What is Skin effect?

The concentration of high-frequency current near the surface of a conductor, which raises its effective resistance. The higher the frequency, the thinner the surface layer carrying the current.

Japanese name: 表皮効果

Skin effect appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Silicon photonics, I/O Interfaces & Buses.

Field: Interconnect processes

Why current crowds to the surface

An alternating current produces a changing magnetic field inside the conductor itself. That changing field induces eddy currents that oppose the current flow near the center and reinforce it near the surface. The net result is that current density falls off roughly exponentially with depth from the surface. The characteristic depth, where density has dropped to about 37% (1/e) of its surface value, is the skin depth δ = √(ρ / (π f μ)), with ρ the resistivity, f the frequency and μ the permeability. At DC there is no skin effect and current uses the whole cross-section.

Typical numbers for copper

For copper, skin depth is roughly 66 µm divided by the square root of frequency in MHz: about 8.5 mm at 60 Hz, about 66 µm at 1 MHz, about 2 µm at 1 GHz and about 0.66 µm at 10 GHz. Once δ is much smaller than the conductor's thickness, the effective resistance rises in proportion to √f. At multi-gigahertz frequencies the current also follows the microscopic roughness of copper foil surfaces, adding further loss, which is why smooth low-profile copper is used in high-speed boards.

Skin effect vs. dielectric loss

Signal loss on a board trace or cable has two main frequency-dependent parts. Conductor loss from the skin effect grows roughly as √f, while dielectric loss, set by the loss tangent of the insulating material, grows roughly in proportion to f and tends to dominate at the highest frequencies. Both make a faster signal lose more over the same length. The resulting inter-symbol interference must be recovered by equalizers, low-loss laminate materials, or retimers that receive and retransmit a clean signal partway along the channel.

Where you will meet it

Skin effect limits the reach of high-speed copper links in servers and data centers, one reason optical fiber, with loss of only about 0.2–0.35 dB/km almost independent of modulation rate, takes over for rack-scale and longer distances. On chips, ordinary interconnect is thinner than the skin depth at typical clock frequencies, but thick top-metal layers used for RF inductors and transmission lines at multi-gigahertz frequencies see raised resistance and lower quality factor. A related effect, proximity effect, pushes current to one side when neighboring conductors carry current.

For high-speed links of a few m or more, such as those spanning racks, what is the main reason optical fiber has an advantage over copper cables and board traces?

  1. ALight travels more than 10 times faster than electrical signals in copper, nearly eliminating latency
  2. BOptical fiber also conducts electricity, so it can deliver power to equipment along with the signal
  3. CFiber loss barely depends on signal speed and is small, about 0.5 dB or less even over 1 km
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Fiber loss barely depends on signal speed and is small, about 0.5 dB or less even over 1 km

In copper wiring, skin effect and dielectric loss make loss increase with signal frequency, so higher speeds shorten reach. The equalizers and retimers used to compensate increase power consumption. In contrast, optical fiber loss is almost independent of modulation speed and small—about 0.35 dB/km at 1.31 µm and about 0.2 dB/km at 1.55 µm in single-mode fiber—and fiber is thin, light and immune to electromagnetic noise. Electrical signals in cables and boards also travel at about half to 80% of the speed of light, not much different from light in fiber (about two-thirds the speed of light).

Why does raising serial link speed make it harder to send signals over board traces of the same length, sometimes requiring retimers along the way?

  1. ATrace loss grows with frequency, so faster signals are attenuated more over the same distance
  2. BFast signals travel through traces faster than light
  3. CFaster signals make trace resistance approach zero and draw too much current
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Trace loss grows with frequency, so faster signals are attenuated more over the same distance

In traces, conductor loss from the skin effect and dielectric loss from the board material both grow with frequency. Doubling the speed weakens the signal much more over the same length, beyond what the receiver's equalizer can recover. So designers use low-loss board materials or insert retimers that receive the signal and retransmit a clean copy.

FAQ

What is the skin depth of copper at 1 GHz?

About 2 µm. It scales with 1/√f, so it is about 0.66 µm at 10 GHz and about 66 µm at 1 MHz.

Does skin effect happen with DC current?

No. Skin effect needs a time-varying current to induce opposing eddy currents, so DC current spreads uniformly over the conductor cross-section.

Why does trace loss increase at higher data rates?

Skin effect raises conductor resistance roughly as the square root of frequency and dielectric loss rises roughly linearly with frequency, so faster signals with higher-frequency content are attenuated more.
