What is Weibull distribution?

A probability distribution widely used to analyze lifetime data. Its shape parameter can represent early-failure, random-failure and wear-out behavior.

Japanese name: ワイブル分布

Weibull distribution appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Reliability.

Field: Reliability

Formula and parameters

The Weibull distribution gives the fraction failed by time t (cumulative failure probability) as F(t) = 1 − exp[−(t/η)^m]. Here m is the shape parameter (also written β) and η is the scale parameter. At t = η, F = 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632, so η is the time by which about 63.2% of the population has failed, also called the characteristic life. The failure rate (hazard function) is λ(t) = (m/η)(t/η)^(m−1), and the value of m determines how it changes with time. There is also a three-parameter form that adds a location parameter representing the time before any failures can occur.

Shape parameter and failure types

When the shape parameter m is less than 1, the failure rate falls over time, representing early failures from manufacturing defects. When m equals 1, the failure rate is constant and the distribution is identical to the exponential distribution, representing random failures. When m is greater than 1, the failure rate rises over time, representing wear-out. Around m = 3.5, the shape of the distribution approaches a normal distribution. Being able to represent early, random and wear-out failures just by changing the shape parameter is a major strength of the Weibull distribution. A single Weibull distribution, however, has a failure rate that only falls, stays constant or only rises, so describing the whole bathtub curve requires fitting each period separately or combining several distributions. A larger m means failure times are more tightly clustered, so it also serves as a measure of spread.

Weibull plot analysis

Rearranging F(t) = 1 − exp[−(t/η)^m] gives ln(−ln(1 − F)) = m·ln t − m·ln η, which is a straight line when ln t is plotted on the horizontal axis and ln(−ln(1 − F)) on the vertical axis. This is the Weibull plot (Weibull probability paper), and the slope of the line is the shape parameter m. Failure times are sorted in ascending order, and the cumulative failure probability of the i-th failure is estimated with a median-rank approximation such as (i − 0.3)/(n + 0.4) before plotting. When the data include tests stopped before every unit failed, maximum likelihood estimation is generally used. A bent plot may indicate a mix of failure modes.

Use in semiconductor reliability

Time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) of gate dielectrics follows weakest-link behavior, failing first at the weakest spot in the film, so it is often described by a Weibull distribution. Lifetime then scales with area to the power −1/m: for example, with m = 1.5, a 1000-fold increase in area cuts lifetime to 1/100. This relation converts results from small test structures to the total gate area of a product. Thinner dielectrics tend to have smaller m. The Weibull distribution is also used for analyses such as thermal fatigue of solder joints, but electromigration lifetimes are often described with a lognormal distribution, so it is important to choose the distribution that fits the failure mechanism.

Answer: A. Weibull distribution

In the Weibull distribution, a shape parameter m below 1 describes infant mortality with decreasing failure rate, m = 1 a constant random-failure rate, and m above 1 wear-out with increasing rate. Its strength is that the same family of distributions covers each of the three bathtub periods just by changing m. The failure rate of a single Weibull distribution only rises or falls monotonically, however, so the whole bathtub curve is modeled by combining distributions with different m. Failure data plotted on Weibull probability paper form a straight line whose slope is m. A uniform distribution gives equally likely values, and a binomial distribution counts successes.

FAQ

What happens when the shape parameter m is 1?

The failure rate becomes constant over time and the Weibull distribution is identical to the exponential distribution. It represents failures in the random-failure period, and the mean time to failure (MTTF) then equals the scale parameter η.

What does the scale parameter η represent?

It is the time by which about 63.2% of the population has failed, also called the characteristic life. Substituting t = η into the formula gives F = 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632 regardless of the shape parameter.

When should you use a Weibull versus a lognormal distribution?

The Weibull distribution is commonly used for dielectric breakdown, where failure is set by the weakest spot, and when you want to describe how the failure rate changes over time. Degradation driven by many accumulating factors, such as diffusion and chemical reactions, often fits a lognormal distribution, which is widely used for electromigration lifetimes. In practice, the data are plotted on each type of probability paper to check the fit as well.
