What is Pellicle?

A thin membrane stretched a short distance above a photomask. It keeps particles off the mask surface, and particles landing on the pellicle are out of focus and do not print.

Japanese name: ペリクル

Pellicle appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Lithography, Process materials (gases and chemicals).

Field: Lithography

Why photomasks need protection

A photomask, or reticle, is imaged onto every exposure field of every wafer it prints. A single particle on its patterned surface therefore becomes a repeating defect that can kill the same die location across thousands of wafers. Masks are expensive, and cleaning them repeatedly risks damaging the pattern. The pellicle solves this by keeping particles off the patterned surface entirely, so that contamination arriving during handling, storage and exposure lands somewhere harmless.

How the defocus principle works

The pellicle is a thin transparent membrane, under 1 µm thick for deep-ultraviolet tools, stretched on a frame about 6 mm from the patterned surface. The projection lens is focused on the pattern, and its depth of focus at the mask side is a tiny fraction of a millimeter. A particle on the pellicle is therefore millimeters out of focus; its image is so blurred that it only slightly dims a wide area instead of printing as a sharp defect. The membrane must transmit the exposure light with minimal loss and stay stable under prolonged irradiation.

Why EUV pellicles are difficult

Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light at 13.5 nm is strongly absorbed by every material, so an EUV pellicle must be only tens of nanometers thick, made from materials such as polysilicon or carbon nanotube films, and still transmits only around 90% per pass. Because EUV masks are reflective, light crosses the pellicle twice, compounding the loss. The membrane also absorbs part of the high source power and heats up, so it must survive high temperatures while remaining mechanically intact across the full mask area.

Pellicle vs. other protective layers

The pellicle protects the mask, not the wafer. In ArF immersion lithography, a topcoat over the photoresist keeps immersion water from leaching resist components, and a bottom antireflective coating (BARC) under the resist controls reflections; neither has anything to do with mask contamination. A pellicle also cannot fix defects already present in the mask pattern or particles trapped under it during mounting, so masks are inspected before the pellicle is attached.

A particle on a mask prints as a defect in every field of every wafer exposed with it. A pellicle is a transparent film under 1 µm thick stretched on a frame about 6 mm above the mask; particles landing on it are far out of focus and do not print. For EUV, ultrathin films tens of nm thick—polysilicon or carbon nanotubes—transmitting about 90% of EUV light and withstanding high temperatures have been developed.

FAQ

What is a pellicle in lithography?

A pellicle is a thin transparent membrane mounted on a frame a few millimeters above a photomask's pattern. Particles land on the membrane instead of the mask, where they are out of focus and do not print.

Why is an EUV pellicle hard to make?

All materials absorb EUV strongly, so the membrane must be only tens of nanometers thick yet span the whole mask, survive heating from absorbed power, and still transmit around 90% of the light on each pass.

Does a pellicle prevent all mask defects?

No. It only keeps new particles off the patterned surface after mounting; pattern defects, or particles trapped underneath when the pellicle is attached, still print.
