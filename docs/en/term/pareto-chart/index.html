What is Pareto chart?

A chart that lists items such as defect types as bars in descending order of count, with a line for the cumulative share. It shows which items to tackle first.

Japanese name: パレート図

Field: Yield and quality control

How a Pareto chart is built

Start by tallying occurrences per category over a fixed period, for example defect types found at inspection or failure bins at wafer test. Plot the categories as bars in descending order, largest on the left, with small categories often grouped into an "other" bar at the far right. Then overlay a line showing the cumulative percentage against a second axis from 0 to 100%. The bars show which category is biggest; the line shows how many categories you must address to cover a given share of the problem.

The 80/20 pattern

The chart is named after the Pareto principle, the empirical observation that a few causes often account for most of the effect. In defect data it is common for the top two or three categories to make up 70–80% of the total, which tells an engineering team where limited effort pays off first. It is a tendency, not a law. A flat Pareto chart, where no category dominates, is itself informative: it suggests a broad, systemic cause rather than one dominant failure mode.

Using it in yield improvement

Counting alone can mislead, so bars are often weighted by impact, such as yield loss, scrap cost or the fraction of defects that actually kill a die. A frequent but harmless defect may then drop below a rarer killer defect. Engineers also stratify: the top bar is broken down into a second-level Pareto chart by tool, chamber, layer or product to narrow the cause. After a fix, a new chart over the same period length shows whether the target bar shrank and what has become the next priority.

Pareto chart vs. other quality tools

A Pareto chart is one of the classic seven basic quality-control tools, and each answers a different question. A histogram shows the distribution of a continuous measurement in ordered bins, while a Pareto chart sorts discrete categories by size. A scatter plot reveals correlation between two variables, a control chart tracks stability over time, a wafer map shows where failures occur on the wafer, and a shmoo plot shows pass/fail across supply voltage and frequency. Pareto analysis decides which problem to attack first; the others help explain it.

FAQ

What is the difference between a Pareto chart and a histogram?

A histogram groups a continuous variable into ordered bins to show its distribution, so bar order follows the value axis. A Pareto chart plots discrete categories sorted from largest to smallest and adds a cumulative-percentage line.

Why does a Pareto chart have a cumulative line?

The cumulative percentage line shows how much of the total is covered by the top categories combined. It lets you read off, for example, that fixing the first three causes would address about 75% of all defects.

What is the Pareto principle?

It is the observation, often summarized as the 80/20 rule, that a small number of causes produce most of the effects. The exact ratio varies and should be checked with data rather than assumed.
