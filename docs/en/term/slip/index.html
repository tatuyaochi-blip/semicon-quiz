What is Slip?

A defect in which crystal planes shift and dislocations form under stress from temperature differences or the wafer's own weight during high-temperature processing of large wafers. It can cause problems such as overlay errors.

Japanese name: スリップ

Field: Silicon wafers

How slip forms

Silicon is brittle at room temperature but becomes much softer at the temperatures used for oxidation, diffusion and annealing. If shear stress in the wafer exceeds the critical value at that temperature, dislocations are created and glide on {111} planes, permanently shifting one part of the crystal relative to another. The stress comes from temperature differences across the wafer, for example when the edge heats or cools faster than the centre during a rapid ramp, and from the wafer's own weight concentrated at the points where it rests on a boat or support pins.

Why large wafers are more vulnerable

Gravitational stress rises with wafer diameter because a larger, heavier disk spans a greater distance between supports, and radial temperature gradients are harder to suppress across a bigger area. Slip therefore typically starts near edge contact points and appears as families of straight lines; on a (100) wafer they run along the two perpendicular ⟨110⟩ directions, where the {111} slip planes intersect the surface. X-ray topography and surface inspection are used to detect it. Rapid thermal processing, with its fast ramps, is a common place to meet slip problems.

Consequences for devices and lithography

Dislocations crossing active regions provide paths for junction leakage and act as recombination centres, especially once metal impurities collect on them, so dies cut by slip lines often fail. The plastic deformation that accompanies slip also distorts the wafer in plane and out of plane. That distortion cannot be removed by later steps, so patterns printed afterwards no longer line up with earlier layers, producing overlay errors that the scanner can only partly correct.

How slip is prevented

Process engineers limit ramp-up and ramp-down rates, design furnace boats and RTP susceptors that spread support loads and keep temperature uniform, and position support points where the combined thermal and gravitational stress is lowest. Wafer material also matters. Interstitial oxygen in Czochralski (CZ) silicon gathers on dislocations and pins them, so CZ wafers resist slip better than low-oxygen float-zone (FZ) wafers, one reason CZ is used for large-diameter IC production. Excessive oxygen precipitation, however, can make wafers more prone to warpage.

Answer: A. Stress from temperature differences within the wafer or its own weight shifts crystal planes and introduces dislocations

At high temperature silicon weakens greatly, so stress from temperature gradients during rapid heating or cooling, or from the wafer's own weight at support points, can make crystal planes ({111}) slip, creating dislocations (slip). It often starts near edge support pins and causes leakage, yield loss and lithography overlay errors. It is prevented by limiting ramp rates and optimizing support methods and geometry.

FAQ

What is slip in a silicon wafer?

Slip is plastic deformation in which dislocations form and glide along {111} crystal planes when thermal or gravitational stress exceeds silicon's strength at high temperature. It shows up as straight slip lines, usually starting near the wafer edge or support points.

Why are CZ wafers more resistant to slip than FZ wafers?

CZ silicon contains interstitial oxygen from the quartz crucible, and these oxygen atoms pin dislocations so they move less easily. Low-oxygen FZ silicon lacks this pinning and slips more readily during thermal processing.

What is the difference between slip and warp?

Slip is a crystallographic defect, the creation and motion of dislocations along slip planes. Warp is a measure of the wafer's overall out-of-plane shape; slip can cause warpage, but warp can also come from film stress without any slip.
