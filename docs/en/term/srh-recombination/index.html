What is SRH recombination (Shockley-Read-Hall recombination)?

Recombination of electrons and holes through deep levels (traps) in the band gap. It mainly determines minority-carrier lifetime in silicon and is accelerated by metal contamination and crystal defects.

Japanese name: SRH再結合(ショックレー・リード・ホール再結合)

How a trap mediates recombination

A deep level in the band gap can capture an electron from the conduction band and then capture a hole from the valence band, annihilating the pair in two steps; emission of a trapped carrier back to its band is the competing process. The net rate is U = (np − ni²) / [τp(n + n1) + τn(p + p1)], where n1 and p1 describe trap position. Traps near midgap make n1 and p1 smallest and are therefore the most effective. The released energy goes mainly into lattice vibrations, not light.

Lifetime and its killers

The characteristic capture lifetime is τ = 1/(σ·vth·Nt), where σ is the capture cross-section, vth the thermal velocity and Nt the trap density, so lifetime falls in inverse proportion to trap concentration. Heavy metals such as iron and gold and crystal defects such as dislocations are typical culprits. In silicon, an indirect-gap semiconductor where radiative recombination is weak, SRH recombination dominates at moderate carrier densities and sets minority-carrier lifetime, which is commonly measured by photoconductance decay techniques.

Lifetime control on purpose

Fast switching devices sometimes need short lifetime. Fast-recovery diodes and IGBTs introduce recombination centers deliberately, by platinum or gold diffusion or by electron or proton irradiation, so that stored minority carriers disappear quickly at turn-off. This reduces reverse recovery time and switching loss, but it also raises forward voltage drop and leakage, so the lifetime is tuned as a compromise between conduction loss and switching loss.

SRH generation and leakage

When np is below ni², as in a reverse-biased depletion region, the same traps run in reverse and generate electron-hole pairs. This generation current is the main source of reverse leakage in silicon pn junctions at room temperature and of dark current in image sensors. It scales with ni, which goes as exp(−Eg/2kT), so its activation energy is about half the band gap, whereas diffusion current, which goes as ni², has an activation energy close to the full gap and takes over at high temperature.

Answer: A. Impurities that create deep levels, such as gold, and crystal defects

Heavy metals such as gold and iron, and crystal defects, create deep levels near midgap that act as recombination centers mediating electron-hole recombination (SRH recombination). Levels closer to midgap promote recombination most strongly, so lifetime becomes short. Conversely, fast-recovery diodes and IGBTs intentionally shorten lifetime by platinum diffusion or electron/proton irradiation so that stored minority carriers disappear quickly. Cryogenic temperature or vacuum are not typical lifetime killers.

  2. BRadiative recombination

Answer: A. SRH (Shockley-Read-Hall) recombination

SRH recombination is the process in which deep levels (traps) from metal impurities or crystal defects capture an electron and a hole in turn, causing them to recombine. Traps near midgap are the most effective. In indirect-gap semiconductors such as silicon it is the dominant recombination process and sets minority-carrier lifetime. SRH generation within depletion regions is also the main source of PN-junction reverse leakage and image-sensor dark current.

FAQ

Why are midgap traps the most effective recombination centers?

A midgap level can capture both electrons and holes at comparable rates and rarely re-emits them thermally, so captured carriers tend to recombine rather than escape back to their band.

What is the difference between SRH, radiative and Auger recombination?

SRH goes through trap levels and dominates in silicon at moderate densities; radiative recombination emits a photon and is strong in direct-gap materials; Auger recombination gives the energy to a third carrier and dominates at very high carrier density.

What shortens minority-carrier lifetime in silicon?

Deep-level impurities such as iron and gold and crystal defects, which act as SRH recombination centers; lifetime falls roughly in inverse proportion to their density.
