What is Acceptor?

An impurity that accepts electrons from a semiconductor and creates holes. For silicon, group III elements such as boron (B) act as acceptors and make p-type semiconductors.

Japanese name: アクセプタ

Acceptor appears in 11 practice questions across 5 topics, including Semiconductor physics, Compound semiconductors, MOSFET.

Field: Semiconductor physics

How an acceptor creates a hole

Silicon atoms share four valence electrons with their neighbours. A group III atom such as boron substitutes for silicon but has only three, leaving one bond incomplete. With very little energy, an electron from the valence band fills that bond, so the boron becomes a fixed negative ion and a mobile hole appears in the valence band. Boron's acceptor level lies about 0.045 eV above the valence band, small compared with room-temperature thermal energy, so nearly all acceptors are ionized and the hole concentration roughly equals the acceptor concentration. At very low temperatures holes freeze back onto the acceptors.

Acceptors vs. donors

Donors are the mirror image: group V atoms such as phosphorus and arsenic have one extra electron, sit about 0.045–0.054 eV below the conduction band and become fixed positive ions. If both types are present they compensate, and the net doping is their difference. Because the product of electron and hole concentrations stays at ni² in equilibrium, a p-type region has very few electrons, its minority carriers. In compound semiconductors the role can depend on the site: silicon in GaAs is a donor on a gallium site but an acceptor on an arsenic site, so it is called amphoteric.

Acceptors in silicon devices

In a pn junction, the depletion region on the p side holds uncompensated negative acceptor ions, which set the built-in field and junction capacitance. In an nMOSFET, a more heavily doped p-type channel holds more acceptor charge in depletion and has a larger Fermi potential, raising the threshold voltage, which is how channel implants tune Vth. Boron is light and diffuses quickly, so shallow p+ junctions use low-energy or BF2 implants. Heavier indium diffuses slowly and has been used for steep retrograde and halo profiles, but its deeper level of about 0.16 eV leaves it only partly ionized.

Deep acceptors in GaN

Wide-bandgap materials make acceptors harder to use. In GaN the standard acceptor, magnesium, sits about 0.2 eV above the valence band, so only a few percent ionize at room temperature: Mg concentrations above 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ yield only 10¹⁷–10¹⁸ holes per cm³. During MOCVD growth, hydrogen also bonds to Mg and passivates it, leaving the layer highly resistive. Electron-beam irradiation, and later annealing at about 700 °C in nitrogen, were found to activate the acceptors, the step that made practical blue LEDs and lasers possible.

Answer: A. Just below the bottom of the conduction band (about 0.045 eV below)

The phosphorus donor level lies only about 0.045 eV below the conduction band, so even room-temperature thermal energy (about 0.026 eV) ionizes nearly all donors. Arsenic is about 0.054 eV, and the boron acceptor level is about 0.045 eV above the valence band. These shallow levels are weakly bound because the host's dielectric constant screens them, so they can be estimated with a hydrogen-atom model. Deep levels near midgap come from impurities such as gold and act as recombination centers.

How many valence (outermost-shell) electrons does a silicon atom have?

  1. A5
  2. B4
  3. C3
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 4

Silicon is a group IV (group 14) element with four valence electrons, and in the crystal each atom shares one electron with each of four neighbors to form covalent bonds. Because every valence electron is tied up in bonding, a pure crystal conducts very little. Adding a group V atom (such as phosphorus) with one extra valence electron creates a donor, and a group III atom (such as boron) with one fewer creates an acceptor—the basis of doping.

FAQ

What is the difference between an acceptor and a donor?

An acceptor (group III in silicon, such as boron) takes an electron from the valence band, leaving a hole and making the material p-type. A donor (group V, such as phosphorus) gives an electron to the conduction band, making it n-type.

Why is boron used as the acceptor in silicon?

Boron has a shallow level about 0.045 eV above the valence band, so it is almost fully ionized at room temperature, and it is highly soluble in silicon. Its drawback is fast diffusion, which makes very shallow junctions harder to form.

Why is p-type GaN hard to make?

Its magnesium acceptor is deep, about 0.2 eV, so only a few percent ionize, and hydrogen from growth passivates Mg until an anneal or electron-beam treatment activates it.
