What is Control chart?

A chart that plots process measurements over time against a center line and control limits (usually ±3σ) to judge whether the process is stable. Points outside the limits or runs on one side signal problems.

Japanese name: 管理図

Anatomy of a control chart

A control chart plots a statistic, such as an individual measurement, a subgroup mean or a defect count, in time order against a center line at the process average. The upper and lower control limits sit three standard deviations of that plotted statistic away from the center; for subgroup means, this is the process standard deviation divided by the square root of the subgroup size. When the process is stable and roughly normal, only about 0.27% of points fall outside the limits by chance, so a point beyond them is strong evidence that something has changed.

Run and trend rules

Points inside the limits can still signal trouble when their pattern is unlikely to be random. Common rule sets, such as the Western Electric or Nelson rules, flag a long run on one side of the center line, a steady upward or downward trend, or several points clustered near one limit. Nine consecutive points on the same side of the center, for example, occur by chance less than 1% of the time in a stable process, so they suggest a shift in the mean before any product goes out of specification. Each added rule also raises the false-alarm rate.

Choosing the right chart

Variables such as film thickness or line width use an X-bar and R chart, which tracks subgroup mean and range on separate panels to catch both shifts in center and changes in spread. Larger subgroups use standard deviation instead of range, and processes with one measurement per lot use an individuals and moving-range chart. Attribute data use a p chart for fraction defective, whose limits vary with sample size, an np chart for defective counts, and c or u charts for defect counts. EWMA and CUSUM charts detect small, gradual shifts faster.

Setting and maintaining limits

Limits are first calculated in a setup phase from roughly 20 to 25 subgroups of a process believed to be stable, after removing points with known special causes; the chart is then used for ongoing monitoring. Control limits must not be confused with specification limits: a process can be stable yet produce out-of-spec parts, or be within spec while drifting out of control. Specification limits for individual parts also should not be drawn on a chart of subgroup means. Limits are recalculated after intentional process changes.

In a control chart, which is an example that should be judged abnormal even though all points are within the control limits?

  1. APoints scattered randomly on both sides of the center line
  2. BAll points moving up and down inside the control limits
  3. CMany consecutive points on one side of the center line

Answer: C. Many consecutive points on one side of the center line

Even within control limits, patterns such as many consecutive points on one side of the centerline (a run) or steadily rising or falling points (a trend) are unlikely to be chance and signal that the process mean is starting to shift. For example, the chance of nine points in a row on the same side is below 1%. Such rules catch problems before out-of-spec product appears. Points scattering randomly around the centerline indicate a normal state.

Which control chart is used to control the fraction defective?

  1. AX̄-R chart
  2. Bp chart
  3. CScatter diagram
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. p chart

A p chart plots the fraction defective for each sample, such as a lot, making it an attribute control chart. Its control limits widen or narrow as sample size changes. Related charts include the np chart for defective counts and the c chart for defect counts. For variables such as film thickness or dimensions, X̄-R charts monitor mean and spread, while a scatter plot examines correlation.

FAQ

Why are control limits set at three sigma?

Three-sigma limits balance sensitivity against false alarms: a stable process puts only about 0.27% of points outside them by chance, while real shifts of meaningful size are detected quickly.

When should I use a p chart instead of an X-bar R chart?

Use a p chart when each item is classified as good or defective and you track the fraction defective. Use an X-bar and R chart when you have continuous measurements, such as thickness or dimensions, taken in subgroups.

Can a process be in control but out of specification?

Yes. Being in control means the process varies only randomly around a stable mean; if that spread is wider than the specification, or the mean is off target, it will still produce defects consistently.
