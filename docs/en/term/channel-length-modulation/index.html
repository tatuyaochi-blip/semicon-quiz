What is Channel-length modulation?

In a saturated MOSFET, raising the drain voltage moves the pinch-off point toward the source, shortening the effective channel and slightly increasing drain current. It makes the output resistance finite.

Japanese name: チャネル長変調

Channel-length modulation appears in 2 practice questions across 1 topic, including MOSFET.

Field: MOSFET

Why drain current keeps rising in saturation

When VDS exceeds VGS − Vth, the inversion layer vanishes at the drain end of the channel, a condition called pinch-off. Further increases in VDS are absorbed by a short depletion region between the pinch-off point and the drain, and that region widens as VDS grows. The pinch-off point therefore moves toward the source, leaving a conducting channel of effective length L − ΔL. Since drain current is inversely proportional to channel length and the voltage across the remaining channel stays near VGS − Vth, the current rises slightly instead of staying perfectly flat.

The λ model and output resistance

Channel-length modulation is usually modeled by adding a factor to the square-law equation: ID = (1/2)μCox(W/L)(VGS − Vth)²(1 + λVDS), where λ is the channel-length modulation coefficient. The slope of ID versus VDS is about λID, so the small-signal output resistance is ro ≈ 1/(λID). Because ΔL is a larger fraction of a short channel, λ is roughly inversely proportional to L, and doubling L roughly doubles ro. In short-channel devices, drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL) adds to the output conductance as well.

Why it matters for analog design

The intrinsic gain of a single transistor is gm·ro, and with gm = 2ID/(VGS − Vth) this becomes 2/(λ(VGS − Vth)). In highly scaled transistors it is only about 10–20, which is not enough for many amplifiers. Designers respond by using longer-than-minimum channel lengths in analog blocks, stacking cascode devices to multiply output resistance, and using multiple gain stages. Channel-length modulation also degrades current mirrors: a mismatch in drain voltage between the reference and output devices produces a current error proportional to λ.

Channel-length modulation vs. the Early effect

Bipolar transistors show an analogous effect called the Early effect, or base-width modulation. A higher collector-emitter voltage widens the collector-base depletion region, narrowing the neutral base and slightly increasing collector current. Its strength is expressed by the Early voltage VA, the point where extrapolated output curves meet the voltage axis, giving ro ≈ VA/IC. For MOSFETs the equivalent quantity is 1/λ, sometimes also called an Early voltage. Both effects tilt the otherwise flat output characteristics and limit the voltage gain available from a single device.

Answer: A. ro ≈ 1 / (λ × ID)

Including channel-length modulation, saturation drain current rises slightly with VDS as ID(1 + λVDS), and the inverse of this slope is the output resistance ro ≈ 1/(λID). λ is roughly inversely proportional to channel length, so doubling L about doubles ro. The intrinsic gain gm·ro is only about 10–20 in highly scaled transistors, so cascodes and multistage designs are used to obtain enough gain.

FAQ

What is lambda (λ) in the MOSFET drain current equation?

λ is the channel-length modulation coefficient, which describes how much saturation drain current rises per volt of VDS through the factor (1 + λVDS). Its inverse behaves like an Early voltage and sets the output resistance ro ≈ 1/(λID).

Why do analog circuits use longer channel lengths?

Channel-length modulation is weaker in long channels because the pinch-off shift is a smaller fraction of L. Longer devices therefore have higher output resistance and intrinsic gain, along with better matching.

What is the difference between channel-length modulation and the Early effect?

Both make output current rise with output voltage and give a finite output resistance. Channel-length modulation shortens the effective channel of a MOSFET, while the Early effect narrows the neutral base of a bipolar transistor.
