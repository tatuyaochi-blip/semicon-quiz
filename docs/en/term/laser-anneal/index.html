What is Laser anneal?

Heating only the near-surface region of a wafer for an extremely short time with laser light. It achieves high activation with almost no diffusion.

Japanese name: レーザーアニール

Laser anneal appears in 4 practice questions across 2 topics, including Ion implantation, Oxidation and diffusion.

Field: Ion implantation

How laser annealing works

Laser annealing is a heat treatment in which laser light is absorbed at the wafer surface, bringing only the near-surface region to a high temperature for a short time. Because the distance heat spreads in depth is roughly proportional to the square root of the heating time, shortening the heating time to the millisecond-to-nanosecond range heats only a shallow region near the surface while most of the wafer stays cool. The surface reaches above 1000 °C, in some conditions near silicon's melting point, but the time at high temperature is so short that implanted dopants can be activated before they diffuse appreciably. This makes shallow, low-resistance junctions possible.

Non-melt and melt annealing

There are two broad approaches. In non-melt annealing, continuous-wave laser light focused into a narrow line is scanned across the wafer, heating each point below the melting point for about a millisecond or less; this is called laser spike annealing, among other names. In melt annealing, nanosecond pulses from an excimer laser or similar melt a very shallow surface layer, which then recrystallizes on cooling using the crystal below as a seed. Melt annealing gives a nearly box-shaped dopant profile and can achieve activation above the equilibrium solid solubility, but that state tends to deactivate during later heat treatments.

Main applications

In logic LSIs, laser annealing is combined with spike annealing to form shallow source/drain junctions and to reach high activation that lowers contact resistance. Because heat does not reach deep, it is also used in steps where underlying layers must not be damaged, such as forming backside layers on thinned wafers for IGBTs and other power devices, or building transistors in an upper tier of a stacked structure. Outside semiconductor manufacturing, excimer laser annealing (ELA), which converts amorphous silicon on glass substrates into polycrystalline silicon, is used to produce low-temperature polysilicon (LTPS) for LCD and OLED displays.

Challenges and comparison with other anneals

One challenge is the pattern effect: reflectivity and absorption vary with the materials and structures on the surface, so peak temperature varies from place to place. It is reduced by choosing the wavelength and angle of incidence or by adding a light-absorbing film. Care is also needed so that stress from rapid heating and cooling does not cause slip or warpage in the wafer. Anneal times get shorter in the order furnace anneal (tens of minutes or more), RTA and spike anneal (around seconds), flash-lamp anneal (around milliseconds) and laser anneal (milliseconds to nanoseconds); the shorter the time, the less diffusion.

Laser annealing heats only the near-surface region with laser light for microseconds to nanoseconds, reaching above 1000 °C (approaching melting in some cases). High activation is achieved before dopants can diffuse appreciably, yielding shallow, low-resistance junctions. FAQ

What is the difference between laser annealing and RTA (rapid thermal annealing)?

RTA heats the whole wafer with lamps for around seconds, while laser annealing heats only the near-surface region for milliseconds to nanoseconds. Because the time at high temperature is far shorter, high activation is achieved with little dopant diffusion, and heat has less effect on underlying layers.

How do melt and non-melt laser annealing differ?

Non-melt annealing activates dopants in the solid state below the melting point, while melt annealing melts a shallow surface layer and lets it recrystallize. Melt annealing gives abrupt profiles and high activation, but the melt depth and changes in surface shape need careful control.

Why is laser annealing also used in display manufacturing?

Glass substrates cannot withstand high temperatures, so an excimer laser momentarily melts only the thin amorphous silicon film and converts it into polycrystalline silicon. This yields low-temperature polysilicon (LTPS) TFTs with high electron mobility, used in high-resolution LCD and OLED displays.
