What is Silicon interposer?

A silicon intermediate substrate that holds chips side by side and connects them densely with fine wiring. It is used to connect GPUs and HBM, among other things.

Field: Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets)

What it is made of

A silicon interposer is a slab of silicon processed with the same copper damascene wiring used for chip interconnect, giving lines and spaces of about 1 µm or finer in several metal layers. It is usually passive, containing wiring but no transistors. Chips such as a processor and memory stacks are attached face down with dense microbumps, and through-silicon vias (TSVs) carry power, ground and external signals down through the interposer to larger bumps on an ordinary package substrate. This arrangement, chips side by side on a shared silicon base, is called 2.5D packaging.

Why GPUs and HBM need it

High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) reaches its bandwidth through width rather than per-pin speed: each stack has a 1,024-bit data bus through the HBM3 generation. HBM3 at 6.4 Gb/s per pin gives about 819 GB/s per stack, and several stacks around a GPU add up to several TB/s. That requires thousands of signal wires only millimeters long, far more than an organic package substrate with lines tens of micrometers wide can route. The interposer's fine wiring makes the connection possible, and short wires also keep energy per bit low.

Size limits and alternatives

An interposer must be larger than all the dies it carries, so large AI packages need interposers several times the area of a single lithography exposure field, built by stitching exposures together; this adds cost and yield risk. Alternatives trade density for cost. Silicon bridges embed small pieces of silicon in the package substrate only under die-to-die edges, as in Intel's EMIB. Organic redistribution-layer (RDL) interposers use polymer dielectric and copper, are cheaper and easier to make large, and better match the substrate's thermal expansion, but offer coarser wiring.

2.5D vs. 3D integration

In 2.5D integration, dies sit side by side on an interposer and communicate laterally; in 3D integration, dies are stacked vertically and connected through TSVs or hybrid bonding. 2.5D keeps every die exposed for cooling and lets dies from different process nodes be mixed easily, while 3D gives the shortest connections and smallest footprint but concentrates heat. The two are often combined: HBM itself is a 3D stack of DRAM dies, and those stacks are placed in 2.5D fashion next to the processor on an interposer, as in TSMC's CoWoS (chip on wafer on substrate).

Answer: B. An intermediate substrate on which several chips are placed side by side and connected at high density with fine wiring

A silicon interposer is a silicon intermediary substrate on which wiring of 1 µm or finer is formed using semiconductor back-end processes. Multiple chips, such as a processor and HBM, sit side by side on it and are connected with wiring far denser than ordinary organic package substrates, whose lines are tens of micrometers wide, can provide. The interposer connects to the package substrate through TSVs. It is unrelated to cooling parts or photomasks.

  1. AWire bonding approach
  2. BSilicon bridge approach
  3. CLead frame approach

Answer: B. Silicon bridge approach

The silicon bridge approach embeds small silicon pieces in the package substrate covering only the connection regions between adjacent dies, forming fine wiring on them for high-density die-to-die links. Eliminating a large interposer under all the dies cuts cost and allows assemblies larger than the lithography field. Intel's EMIB is a representative example. Wire bonds or lead frames cannot reach this density.

FAQ

What is a silicon interposer used for?

It carries several chips side by side and connects them with micrometer-scale wiring, most notably linking a GPU or AI accelerator to multiple HBM stacks with thousands of short wires.

What is the difference between 2.5D and 3D packaging?

2.5D places dies side by side on an interposer or bridge, while 3D stacks dies directly on top of each other using TSVs or hybrid bonding.

Why not use an organic substrate instead of a silicon interposer?

Standard organic substrates have lines tens of micrometers wide and cannot route thousands of HBM signals in the available area; organic RDL interposers and silicon bridges are cheaper alternatives with somewhat lower density.
