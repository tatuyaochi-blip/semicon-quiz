What is FZ method (float zone)?

A method that melts a zone of a polysilicon rod and moves that molten zone along to form a single crystal. With no crucible, oxygen content is low, suiting high-resistivity wafers.

Japanese name: FZ法(フローティングゾーン法)

How the FZ method grows a single crystal

In the FZ (float zone) method, a high-purity polysilicon rod is held vertically and a narrow band of it is melted by an RF induction coil. A seed crystal is attached to the end of the rod, and the coil and rod are then moved slowly relative to each other, so the melted band (the molten zone) travels along the rod and solidifies behind it as a single crystal with the seed's orientation. Surface tension holds the molten zone between the solid parts above and below, so it never touches a crucible or container. The name 'float zone' describes this zone that seems to float in place.

Why it is low in oxygen and high in purity

In the CZ method about 10¹⁷–10¹⁸ cm⁻³ of oxygen dissolved from the quartz crucible enters the crystal, but FZ uses no crucible, so oxygen contamination is orders of magnitude lower. Most impurities also have segregation coefficients below 1, so they prefer to stay in the molten zone rather than the solidifying crystal and are carried toward the end of the rod as the zone moves (the same principle as zone refining). This yields high-purity, high-resistivity crystals, with resistivities above 1000 Ω·cm possible. Dopants are added by flowing a dopant gas around the molten zone or by neutron irradiation.

Why it is used for power devices and detectors

FZ silicon is used for power devices such as IGBTs, thyristors and high-voltage diodes, and for radiation detectors. High breakdown voltage requires a thick, lightly doped drift layer, which calls for wafers with high, uniform resistivity. Low oxygen also avoids resistivity shifts from oxygen-related thermal donors formed during heat treatment. Where uniformity is critical, neutron transmutation doping (NTD), which uses neutrons to convert part of the Si-30 into phosphorus, keeps resistivity variation to a few percent or less.

Differences from CZ and its limits

FZ's weak points are large diameters and volume production. Because only surface tension supports the molten zone, larger diameters become harder, and the practical limit is about 200 mm. The feedstock must be crack-free, uniform polysilicon rods, which raises cost. And without oxygen to pin dislocations, FZ wafers tend to be less resistant to slip and warpage during heat treatment than CZ wafers. Large-diameter LSIs that pass through many high-temperature steps therefore use CZ wafers, while FZ wafers are reserved for applications where high resistivity or purity determines performance.

The float-zone (FZ) method melts a narrow band of a polysilicon rod with RF induction heating and moves the molten zone along the rod to convert it to a single crystal. With no crucible, little oxygen enters, and impurities are swept away in the molten zone, yielding high-purity crystals with resistivities above 1000 Ω·cm. FZ silicon serves high-voltage power devices such as IGBTs and radiation detectors, but CZ scales more easily, with FZ limited to about 200 mm.

FAQ

What is the difference between the FZ and CZ methods?

The CZ method pulls a crystal from silicon melted in a quartz crucible; it scales to 300 mm and supplies most wafers for LSIs. The FZ method uses no crucible and instead melts and moves a zone along a polysilicon rod, giving crystals with very little oxygen and high resistivity, but only up to about 200 mm.

Why is FZ silicon used for power devices?

High-voltage power devices need a thick, lightly doped drift layer, and FZ wafers offer high, uniform resistivity with little oxygen. Combined with neutron transmutation doping, resistivity can be made even more uniform.

What is neutron transmutation doping (NTD)?

A doping method that exposes silicon to neutrons in a nuclear reactor: Si-30, an isotope making up about 3% of silicon, absorbs a neutron to become Si-31, which beta-decays with a half-life of about 2.6 hours into phosphorus. Because neutrons reach the whole crystal evenly, it produces very uniform n-type silicon.
