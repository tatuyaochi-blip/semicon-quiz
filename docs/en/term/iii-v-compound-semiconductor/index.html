What is III-V compound semiconductor?

Semiconductors made of group III elements (Ga, In, Al, etc.) and group V elements (As, P, N, etc.), such as GaAs, InP and GaN.

Japanese name: III-V族化合物半導体

Field: Compound semiconductors

Combining group III and group V elements

III-V compound semiconductors combine group-III elements such as gallium (Ga), indium (In) and aluminum (Al) with group-V elements such as arsenic (As), phosphorus (P), nitrogen (N) and antimony (Sb). Atoms with three and five valence electrons alternate one to one, so the average is four, the same as silicon, and they form covalently bonded crystals. GaAs and InP take the zinc blende structure, in which each atom is surrounded by four atoms of the other element, while GaN mainly takes the hexagonal wurtzite structure. SiC (a IV-IV compound) and ZnSe or CdTe (II-VI compounds) belong to different families.

Engineering the band gap with alloys

A defining feature of III-V materials is that alloys of several elements let the band gap and lattice constant be tuned. By combining three or four elements, as in AlGaAs, InGaAs, InGaAsP and AlGaInP, and adjusting the composition, the wavelength of emitted or detected light can be chosen. The lattice constant varies roughly linearly with composition (Vegard's law), so In0.53Ga0.47As, for example, nearly matches the lattice constant of an InP substrate and can be grown in thick layers with little strain. Heterostructures that stack layers of different composition underpin quantum-well lasers, HEMTs and HBTs.

Strengths silicon lacks, and main uses

Many III-V materials, including GaAs and InP, have a direct band gap, so electrons and holes recombine efficiently and emit light; they are used in LEDs, semiconductor lasers and photodetectors. InP-based materials serve the 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm optical-communication bands, GaAs- and AlGaInP-based materials cover infrared to red, and InGaN covers blue to green. GaAs also has an electron mobility of about 8500 cm²/V·s, about six times that of silicon, which suits high-frequency power amplifiers and HEMTs. GaN has a breakdown field about ten times that of silicon and is used in base-station amplifiers and power devices for power supplies.

Drawbacks compared with silicon

III-V materials also face manufacturing hurdles. Growing large, low-defect single crystals while holding the ratio of two or more elements exactly is difficult, so wafers are typically about 150 mm for GaAs and 100–150 mm for InP, smaller and more expensive than 300 mm silicon. They have no good thermal oxide and low hole mobility, which makes them unsuitable for high-performance CMOS or large digital circuits. Toxic materials such as arsenic and arsine also require careful control. As a result they are combined with silicon LSIs and used where they excel, such as light sources and high-frequency components.

Answer: B. GaN

GaN combines group-III gallium and group-V nitrogen, making it a III-V compound semiconductor. Pairing groups III and V averages four valence electrons per atom, forming covalent crystals similar to silicon; GaAs, InP, AlN and InSb belong to the same family. SiC is a IV-IV compound, and ZnSe, made of group-II zinc and group-VI selenium, is a II-VI compound, as are CdTe (solar cells) and ZnO (transparent electrodes).

FAQ

What are typical III-V compound semiconductors?

Binary examples include GaAs (gallium arsenide), InP (indium phosphide), GaN (gallium nitride), AlN and InSb. Alloys of these, such as AlGaAs, InGaAs, InGaN and AlGaInP, are also included, and they are used in LEDs, lasers and high-frequency transistors.

Is SiC a III-V compound semiconductor?

No. SiC is a IV-IV compound semiconductor made of the group-IV elements silicon and carbon. It is often discussed alongside GaN because both are wide-bandgap semiconductors used in power devices, but it is not a III-V material.

Why is it hard to make light emitters from silicon?

Silicon has an indirect band gap, so electron-hole recombination needs a phonon to make up the momentum difference, and the probability of emitting light is very low. Many III-V materials such as GaAs and InP have direct band gaps and emit light efficiently. That is why even silicon photonic circuits use InP-based or similar lasers as light sources.
