What is Bathtub curve?

A curve showing how the failure rate changes over time. It consists of the early-failure, random-failure and wear-out periods and is shaped like a bathtub.

Japanese name: バスタブ曲線

Bathtub curve appears in 5 practice questions across 4 topics, including Reliability, Testing and inspection, Yield and quality control.

Field: Reliability

The three regions and their causes

Plotted as failure rate against time for a large population of parts, the curve falls, flattens, then rises. Early failures, often called infant mortality, come from latent manufacturing defects such as weak gate oxide spots, particles or marginal bonds, and their rate decreases as the weak parts drop out. The useful-life period has a low, roughly constant rate caused by random events such as electrical overstress. Wear-out begins when intrinsic degradation mechanisms, including electromigration, time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB), hot-carrier injection and bias temperature instability, accumulate and the rate climbs.

The math behind each region

The curve plots the hazard rate: the probability per unit time that a part still working fails now. A constant hazard rate λ gives exponential reliability R(t) = exp(−λt) and a mean time to failure of 1/λ; for semiconductors λ is usually quoted in FIT, failures per 10⁹ device-hours. The Weibull distribution is the usual tool for all three regions: a shape parameter below 1 gives a falling rate, exactly 1 a constant rate, and above 1 a rising rate, so fitting test data to it indicates which region a failure mechanism belongs to.

Burn-in: skipping the infant-mortality region

Burn-in operates parts for hours to tens of hours at elevated temperature, around 125 °C, and above-nominal voltage so that latent defects fail in the factory rather than in the field. Parts that survive are shipped already past the steep early slope. It is costly, needs special sockets and boards, and consumes a small amount of the product's wear-out life, so it is reserved for applications that demand very low early failure rates, such as automotive and server components, and is often supplemented by voltage stress tests during wafer test.

Designing so wear-out comes late

Engineers aim to place the start of wear-out well beyond the intended service life, for example around 15 years or more for automotive electronics, by setting design rules for current density, electric field and temperature with margin. Accelerated life tests at high temperature and voltage then confirm that intrinsic mechanisms stay out of the mission profile. The bathtub curve should not be confused with the yield learning curve, which tracks manufacturing yield improving over months as a process matures, not failures in the field over a product's life.

Answer: A. Early failure period, random failure period, wear-out failure period

The bathtub curve has three periods shaped like a bathtub cross-section: infant mortality, with high failure rates from manufacturing defects early in use; the random-failure period, with low and nearly constant rates; and wear-out, where degradation raises the rate. Infant failures are screened out before shipment by burn-in, and products are designed so wear-out begins after their intended life. Design, manufacturing and shipping are lifecycle stages, not failure-rate periods.

FAQ

What are the three phases of the bathtub curve?

Early failure (infant mortality) with a decreasing rate, the useful-life period with a low and roughly constant rate, and wear-out with an increasing rate.

Why does burn-in reduce early failures?

High temperature and voltage accelerate the failure of parts with latent defects, so they are screened out before shipment and customers receive a population that is already past the infant-mortality region.

Which Weibull shape parameter corresponds to wear-out?

A shape parameter greater than 1 means an increasing failure rate, which is wear-out; less than 1 means infant mortality and exactly 1 means a constant, random failure rate.
