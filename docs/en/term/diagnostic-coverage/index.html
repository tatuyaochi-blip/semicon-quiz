What is Diagnostic coverage?

The fraction of faults that a safety mechanism can detect or control, abbreviated DC. Adding a safety mechanism with 99% diagnostic coverage to a 200 FIT circuit leaves 2 FIT of undetected faults.

Japanese name: 診断カバレッジ

Field: Automotive Semiconductors and Functional Safety

Definition and arithmetic

Diagnostic coverage (DC) is the share of a hardware element's failure rate, or of one failure mode's rate, that a safety mechanism detects or controls in time to prevent a safety goal violation. The uncovered part becomes residual faults: residual rate = failure rate × (1 − DC). A 200 FIT block with 99% coverage leaves 2 FIT; the same block with 90% leaves 20 FIT, ten times more. Detection alone is not enough: the system must also reach a safe state, such as resetting or disabling an output, within the fault tolerant time interval (FTTI).

Diagnostic coverage vs. test fault coverage

The two are easily confused because both are percentages of faults detected. Fault coverage in manufacturing test is the fraction of modeled faults, such as stuck-at faults, that test patterns can catch on a tester before shipment; it screens defective parts. Diagnostic coverage concerns faults that arise during use, in the vehicle, and must be caught on the spot by mechanisms built into the chip or system. Some techniques serve both purposes: logic built-in self-test (LBIST) run at power-up, for example, helps detect latent faults in the field.

How mechanisms achieve high coverage

Coverage depends on both the mechanism and the fault model. Parity detects any single-bit error in a word but misses double-bit errors; single-error-correct, double-error-detect (SECDED) ECC covers more. Dual-core lockstep compares two cores running the same instructions every cycle, giving high coverage for CPU logic, but common-cause faults that hit both cores alike, such as clock or supply disturbances, need extra measures like a cycle offset between cores and separate monitors. Watchdogs and program-flow monitoring typically reach lower coverage because they see only gross misbehavior.

Justifying the claimed number

ISO 26262 lists typical mechanisms and ranks the coverage they can be credited with as low, medium or high, corresponding to about 60, 90 and 99%. A claim must be backed by evidence: analysis of which faults the mechanism can observe, and often fault-injection simulation, where stuck-at and transient faults are injected into the RTL or gate-level netlist and the fraction detected is counted. Separate coverages are tracked for residual faults, which feed the single-point fault metric (SPFM), and for latent faults, which feed the latent fault metric (LFM).

  3. C2 FIT

Answer: C. 2 FIT

Diagnostic coverage (DC) is the proportion of faults that a safety mechanism can detect or control. The remaining failure rate is 200 × (1 − 0.99) = 200 × 0.01 = 2 FIT. 198 FIT is the failure rate of the faults the safety mechanism can detect, and 0.02 FIT is the result of misplacing the decimal point in the multiplication. Fault coverage in manufacturing test represents the proportion of defective parts found before shipment, whereas diagnostic coverage represents the proportion of faults occurring in use that are detected on the spot.

FAQ

How do you calculate residual failure rate from diagnostic coverage?

Multiply the element's relevant failure rate by one minus the diagnostic coverage. For example, 200 FIT with 99% coverage leaves 200 × 0.01 = 2 FIT undetected.

What is high diagnostic coverage in ISO 26262?

High coverage corresponds to about 99%, medium to about 90% and low to about 60%. ASIL D designs generally need high coverage on the blocks that dominate the failure rate.

Is diagnostic coverage the same as fault coverage?

No. Fault coverage measures how many modeled faults manufacturing test patterns detect before shipment, while diagnostic coverage measures how many faults occurring in operation are detected or controlled by on-board safety mechanisms.
