What is Post-quantum cryptography?

Cryptography that bases its security on mathematical problems believed to be hard to solve efficiently even for quantum computers, such as lattice problems and hash functions. It runs on conventional computers and is used to replace RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, which Shor's algorithm can break.

Japanese name: 耐量子計算機暗号

Field: Quantum Computing Hardware

Why it is needed

RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, which are widely used today, would be broken by Shor's algorithm on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. Such quantum computers do not yet exist, but there is the risk of “harvest now, decrypt later”, in which ciphertext recorded today is decrypted in the future, and replacing cryptography takes many years. The migration therefore needs to begin before quantum computers arrive.

Main schemes and standards

In 2024 the US NIST published as standards the lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism ML-KEM (FIPS 203), the lattice-based signature scheme ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and the hash-based signature scheme SLH-DSA (FIPS 205). Their keys and signatures are larger than before: for example, an ML-KEM-768 public key is 1,184 bytes, far larger than the 32 bytes of the elliptic-curve key exchange X25519. An ML-DSA-65 signature is 3,309 bytes.

Hardware and migration

Because the keys and signatures are larger and the computations are different in nature, migration proceeds while checking the impact on embedded devices and smart cards with little memory, packet sizes in communication protocols and so on. Lattice-based schemes speed up polynomial multiplication with the number-theoretic transform (NTT), and acceleration with dedicated circuits is being studied. Implementations also need protection against side-channel attacks, which leak secrets through power consumption or timing. For now, migration proceeds step by step with hybrid schemes that combine conventional cryptography, and designs that make it easy to replace cryptography later (crypto agility) are emphasized.

  2. BCryptography that cannot encrypt or decrypt without using a quantum computer
  3. CCryptography that simply doubles the key length of RSA

Answer: A. Cryptography that runs on conventional computers and bases its security on mathematical problems (such as lattice problems) believed to be hard to solve efficiently even for quantum computers

Post-quantum cryptography replaces factoring and the discrete logarithm problem, which Shor's algorithm can solve, with schemes based on lattice problems, hash functions and the like, and it runs as is on today's computers and network equipment. In 2024 the US NIST published standards including the lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism ML-KEM (FIPS 203) and the signature scheme ML-DSA (FIPS 204). Many of these schemes have larger keys or signatures than RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography, so migration proceeds with attention to the impact on communication protocols, embedded devices and hardware implementations. It is a different technology from quantum key distribution (QKD), which shares keys using quantum properties themselves.

Answer: C. Ciphertext intercepted and stored today may be decrypted by future quantum computers (“harvest now, decrypt later”), and migrating cryptography takes many years

An attacker can record encrypted traffic now and, once quantum computers become practical, break the key exchange and read the contents. Information that must stay secret for a long time therefore needs to be protected with post-quantum cryptography even before quantum computers arrive. Cryptography is also built into operating systems, browsers, network equipment, smart cards and the firmware and hardware of embedded devices, and replacing all of it takes many years. Migration is being carried out step by step with hybrid schemes that combine conventional and post-quantum cryptography.

FAQ

How does it differ from quantum key distribution (QKD)?

Post-quantum cryptography is based on the difficulty of mathematical problems and runs on ordinary computers and networks. Quantum key distribution shares keys using quantum properties of photons and the like and requires dedicated optical communication equipment. They are separate technologies, and post-quantum cryptography is at the center of the replacement effort.

Do symmetric-key ciphers such as AES need to be replaced too?

Against symmetric-key ciphers there is only the quadratic speed-up of Grover's algorithm, so it is thought that no change of scheme is needed and long enough keys are an adequate response. For example, AES-256 is considered to keep sufficient security even against quantum computers.
