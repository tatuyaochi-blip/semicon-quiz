What is FinFET?

A 3D transistor whose channel stands up like a fin and is surrounded by the gate on three sides. It suppresses short-channel effects well.

Japanese name: FinFET

Field: Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA)

Why the gate wraps the fin

In a planar MOSFET the gate controls the channel only from above, and at short gate lengths the drain field reaches under it, causing leakage and threshold shifts known as short-channel effects. A FinFET stands the silicon up as a narrow fin and wraps the gate over both sidewalls and the top, so the gate reaches the whole fin from three sides. The fin must be thin enough for the two sidewall gates to control its center, roughly half to equal the gate length or less, about 5 to 7 nm in advanced nodes. Even thinner fins suffer from quantum confinement, surface scattering and variability.

Effective width and fin quantization

Current flows along the gated perimeter, so the effective channel width of one fin is twice the fin height plus the fin width. A fin 50 nm tall and 6 nm wide gives 2 × 50 + 6 = 106 nm, more than three times the width a planar device gets from a 30 nm fin pitch. Because height and width are fixed by the process, designers change drive strength only by changing the number of fins. This width quantization makes fine current ratios in analog circuits and transistor-strength ratios in SRAM cells harder to tune than with planar devices.

How fins are built and tuned

Fins are etched into silicon and separated by shallow trench isolation, with flowable CVD often used to fill the narrow gaps between them. Process steps must work on three-dimensional surfaces, which favors conformal techniques such as radical oxidation and plasma doping. Raised source and drain regions are grown epitaxially on the fins to lower resistance, using SiGe for pMOS and phosphorus-doped silicon for nMOS, which also strains the channel. On a standard (100) wafer, the fin sidewalls are (110) planes, which favor hole mobility and penalize electrons, narrowing the usual gap between nMOS and pMOS.

From planar to FinFET to GAA

The FinFET was published under that name in 1999 by Chenming Hu and colleagues at UC Berkeley; its forerunner was the DELTA structure reported by Hitachi in 1989. Intel brought it into volume production at its 22 nm generation in 2012 under the name Tri-Gate, and other foundries followed at the 16/14 nm generation, ending the planar era for leading-edge logic. Its successor is the gate-all-around (GAA) nanosheet transistor, which stacks thin horizontal silicon sheets, each surrounded by the gate on all four sides. GAA gives stronger electrostatic control and lets designers set sheet width continuously, removing the fin quantization constraint.

Answer: A. By changing the number of fins, so the width takes discrete values

In a FinFET, fin height and width are fixed by the process, so channel width is adjusted only by the number of fins—a discrete quantity ("width quantization"). This makes it hard to set fine current ratios in analog circuits or tune the strength ratios among SRAM cell transistors, constraining design. Nanosheet GAA devices relax this limitation.

FAQ

What is the difference between a FinFET and a planar MOSFET?

A planar MOSFET's gate sits only on top of a flat channel, while a FinFET's gate wraps three sides of a vertical fin. The stronger gate control suppresses short-channel leakage and the fin height adds current-carrying width without adding footprint.

How do you calculate the effective width of a FinFET?

For one fin, the effective width is twice the fin height plus the fin width, because the gate covers both sidewalls and the top. Total width is that value multiplied by the number of fins.

What is the difference between FinFET and GAA?

A FinFET gate covers three sides of a vertical fin, whereas a gate-all-around transistor surrounds stacked horizontal nanosheets on all sides. GAA offers better electrostatic control and continuously adjustable channel width instead of whole-fin steps.
