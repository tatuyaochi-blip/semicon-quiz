What is IDDQ test?

A test that measures the supply current of a CMOS circuit at rest and flags defects such as bridging faults when it is abnormally high.

Principle of IDDQ testing

IDDQ is the current flowing into the supply of a CMOS circuit (IDD) measured in the quiescent state, when the inputs are not changing. In a fault-free CMOS logic gate in the quiescent state, either the pMOS or the nMOS is always off, so there is no direct current path from supply to ground, and only a tiny leakage current flows. However, a short between wires (a bridge), a gate-oxide defect or a transistor that is stuck on lets current flow from supply to ground even in the quiescent state. IDDQ testing finds defects by measuring this abnormal current.

How the test is run

The test applies a sequence of patterns that put the circuit's internals into several different states and measures the supply current once activity has settled for each. Because defect current flows only when opposite logic values are set on the two sides of the defect, patterns are chosen to activate as many node combinations as possible. Chips whose measured current exceeds a predetermined threshold are judged defective. Waiting for the current to settle and then measuring it accurately takes time, so the number of measured patterns is kept far smaller than in logic tests.

Advantages of IDDQ testing

A major advantage of IDDQ testing is that it finds defects that do not appear at the logic outputs. For example, if a bridge draws current but the circuit's logic values happen to stay correct, a test that only compares outputs sees a good part. Latent defects that work now but may fail in use, such as weak spots in the gate oxide, can also sometimes be caught as increased current. Because it detects many defects with few patterns, it has been used in reliability-focused products to complement stuck-at testing.

Limits from scaling and workarounds

With scaling, the off-state leakage per transistor rose and transistor counts grew, so even fault-free chips draw a large quiescent current. Chip-to-chip variation is also large, and the extra current from a defect gets buried in the normal current, making it hard to separate with a fixed threshold. Workarounds include looking at current differences between patterns (ΔIDDQ), comparing with values from nearby chips on the same wafer, and measuring the supply of each circuit block separately. Even so, application has become difficult, and IDDQ is used in combination with tests such as delay-fault tests.

Answer: A. It can also find defects such as shorts between wires (bridges) that increase the quiescent current while the logic outputs happen to remain correct

In a healthy CMOS circuit at rest, either the pMOS or the nMOS is off, so only leakage current flows from supply to ground. A bridge or gate-oxide defect increases the quiescent current when opposite logic values are set on its two sides, but if the logic value happens to stay correct, a test that compares outputs sees a good chip. The IDDQ test measures this current to find such defects, and it can sometimes also catch latent defects that may fail in use. However, scaling has increased the leakage current of good chips, making it harder to distinguish the increase caused by a defect.

FAQ

What does the Q in IDDQ stand for?

It is the initial of quiescent. IDD denotes the supply current of a CMOS circuit, and IDDQ means the supply current measured in the quiescent state, when the inputs are not changing.

Which defects does IDDQ testing find easily?

Defects that create a current path from supply to ground in the quiescent state, such as shorts between wires (bridges), gate-oxide defects and transistors stuck on. Even bridges that do not show up at logic outputs can sometimes be detected as increased current.

Why has IDDQ testing become harder to use?

Because scaling increased transistor leakage, so even fault-free chips draw a large and variable quiescent current. The extra current from a defect is buried in the normal current and becomes hard to distinguish. It is therefore used with workarounds such as looking at current differences between patterns, and in combination with other tests.
