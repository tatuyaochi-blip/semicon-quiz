What is HVPE (hydride vapor phase epitaxy)?

Vapor-phase growth using gallium chloride, formed by reacting HCl with gallium, together with ammonia. Its very high growth rate is used to grow thick GaN crystals for free-standing substrates.

Japanese name: HVPE(ハイドライド気相成長)

Field: Epitaxial growth

How the reaction works

HVPE (hydride vapor phase epitaxy) is a vapor-phase growth method that carries the group-III source in chloride form. For GaN, hydrogen chloride (HCl) is passed over heated metallic gallium upstream in the reactor to form gallium chloride (GaCl), which reacts with ammonia (NH3) near the substrate, heated downstream to about 1000 °C or more, to deposit GaN. Most reactors are hot-wall types that also heat the reactor walls, with hydrogen or nitrogen as the carrier gas. The name "hydride" comes from the use of hydrides such as ammonia as source gases.

Why growth is fast, and the benefits

HVPE's hallmark is a growth rate of tens to hundreds of µm per hour, tens of times faster than MOCVD. Chloride sources can be supplied in large amounts, and because no metal-organics are used, carbon incorporation is low. This speed is used to grow GaN hundreds of µm to millimeters thick on foreign substrates such as sapphire. Many dislocations form early in growth because of the lattice mismatch, but as the film thickens, dislocations meet and annihilate, so dislocation density falls toward the surface. On the other hand, HVPE is poorly suited to atomically abrupt interfaces or multilayers with finely varied composition.

Making free-standing substrates

Removing the original substrate from a thick GaN layer leaves a free-standing GaN substrate. Removal methods include laser lift-off, in which laser light decomposes the GaN at the interface; spontaneous separation that exploits the thermal-expansion mismatch during cooling after growth; and grinding or etching away the substrate. HVPE is often combined with masked dislocation-reduction techniques such as epitaxial lateral overgrowth (ELO). GaN substrates made this way are used for blue-violet laser diodes and for vertical GaN power devices that conduct current through the thickness.

Compared with MOCVD and other substrate methods

MOCVD uses metal-organics such as trimethylgallium and can switch composition on a scale of a few nanometers, so it is used to grow thin multilayer device structures such as LED quantum wells and HEMTs. HVPE, with its speed, suits thick films and substrates, so the two divide the work. Other routes to GaN substrates include the ammonothermal method, which grows crystals in supercritical ammonia, and the Na-flux method, which uses sodium as a solvent; they compete on low dislocation density and larger diameters. HVPE is also used for epitaxial growth of materials other than GaN, such as gallium oxide (Ga2O3).

FAQ

What is HVPE, in simple terms?

A vapor-phase growth method that forms gallium chloride from metallic gallium and hydrogen chloride and reacts it with ammonia to grow GaN. With growth rates of tens to hundreds of µm per hour, it is used to grow the thick crystals needed for free-standing GaN substrates.

What is the difference between HVPE and MOCVD?

HVPE uses chloride sources and excels at growing thick films tens of times faster than MOCVD. MOCVD uses metal-organic sources; it grows more slowly but can switch composition on a scale of a few nanometers, so it is used for the thin multilayer structures of LEDs and transistors.

Why does thicker growth reduce dislocations?

Some of the dislocations formed at the interface with the foreign substrate run at an angle through the film, so as the film thickens they have more chances to meet and annihilate or merge. As a result, dislocation density is lower toward the surface of a thick crystal.
