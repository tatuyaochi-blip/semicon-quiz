What is Heteroepitaxy?

Growing a single-crystal film of a material different from the substrate, such as SiGe on silicon or GaN on sapphire. Growth of the same material is homoepitaxy.

Japanese name: ヘテロエピタキシー

Field: Epitaxial growth

Lattice mismatch and critical thickness

When the film and substrate have different lattice constants, the mismatch is expressed as f = (a_film − a_substrate)/a_substrate. Germanium is about 4% larger than silicon, while GaN on sapphire has an effective in-plane mismatch of around 16%. A thin layer can grow pseudomorphically, stretching or compressing to match the substrate in plane and storing elastic strain. Beyond a critical thickness, which shrinks as mismatch grows, the strain relaxes by forming misfit dislocations at the interface, often accompanied by threading dislocations that run up through the film.

Thermal mismatch

Even a well-matched pair can crack or bow if the two materials expand differently with temperature. Epitaxy happens at high temperature, so on cooling a difference in thermal expansion coefficients builds up stress across the whole wafer. GaN grown on silicon, for example, ends up in tension and can crack unless the stack includes stress-compensating layers. Wafer bow from thermal mismatch also complicates lithography and wafer handling in later steps.

Buffer layers for defect control

Rather than growing a strongly mismatched film directly, engineers insert buffer layers. A compositionally graded SiGe buffer, raising the Ge fraction by about 10% per µm, lets misfit dislocations glide long distances within the buffer and can bring threading dislocation density in the top layer down to about 10⁵–10⁶ cm⁻², at the cost of several µm of buffer. For GaN on sapphire, a thin low-temperature nucleation layer of AlN or GaN is used, though threading densities remain far higher than in silicon.

Heteroepitaxy vs. homoepitaxy

Homoepitaxy grows the same material as the substrate, such as a lightly doped silicon layer on a heavily doped silicon wafer, and involves no lattice mismatch. Heteroepitaxy is harder, but it is what enables strain engineering, such as SiGe source/drain regions, and band-gap engineering in heterojunction bipolar transistors, high-electron-mobility transistors (HEMTs), quantum wells, LEDs and lasers, such as AlGaAs on GaAs. A thermally grown SiO2 layer is not epitaxy at all: it is amorphous and forms by consuming the silicon.

  1. ATo make the wafer thinner
  2. BTo change the lattice constant gradually, relaxing strain step by step and reducing dislocations threading into the upper layer
  3. CTo increase the growth rate
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. To change the lattice constant gradually, relaxing strain step by step and reducing dislocations threading into the upper layer

Growing a strongly mismatched material directly sends many dislocations through to the top layer. Inserting a compositionally graded buffer—SiGe increasing by about 10% Ge per µm, for example—relaxes strain gradually, letting misfit dislocations run long distances along interfaces within the buffer, cutting threading dislocations in the top layer to about 10⁵–10⁶ cm⁻². The trade-off is a buffer several µm thick.

FAQ

What is the critical thickness in heteroepitaxy?

It is the maximum thickness a mismatched layer can reach while staying coherently strained; above it, misfit dislocations form to relieve the strain. The larger the mismatch, the thinner the critical thickness.

Why is GaN grown on sapphire or silicon instead of GaN substrates?

Large, affordable native GaN substrates have historically been hard to make, so GaN is grown heteroepitaxially on sapphire, SiC or silicon despite the lattice and thermal mismatch.

Is SiGe on silicon heteroepitaxy?

Yes. SiGe has a larger lattice constant than silicon, so thin SiGe layers grow under compressive strain, which is exploited in transistors and HBTs.
