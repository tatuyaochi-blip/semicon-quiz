What is Quasi-Fermi level?

Separate Fermi levels for electrons and holes, used to describe a semiconductor driven out of equilibrium by light or current injection. The larger the split between them, the more excess carriers there are.

Japanese name: 擬フェルミ準位

Quasi-Fermi level appears in 1 practice question across 1 topic, including Semiconductor physics.

Field: Semiconductor physics

Why two Fermi levels are needed

In a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium, both the electron and hole densities are set by a single Fermi level, and the product np = ni² holds. Shining light on it or driving forward current through a PN junction creates more electrons and holes than in equilibrium, so np > ni², which a single Fermi level can no longer describe. However, electrons among themselves and holes among themselves approach thermal equilibrium within their own bands much faster than they recombine. Separate Fermi levels are therefore assigned to electrons and holes to describe their densities. These are the quasi-Fermi levels, also called imrefs, Fermi spelled backward.

Definition and equations

With EFn as the electron quasi-Fermi level and EFp as the hole quasi-Fermi level, a nondegenerate semiconductor has n = ni·exp((EFn − Ei)/kT) and p = ni·exp((Ei − EFp)/kT), where Ei is the intrinsic Fermi level. Multiplying the two gives np = ni²·exp((EFn − EFp)/kT), so the larger the gap between them, the more excess carriers there are. On returning to equilibrium the two coincide as the ordinary Fermi level. Under low injection, the minority-carrier quasi-Fermi level moves a lot while the majority-carrier one barely moves.

Voltage in PN junctions and solar cells

In a forward-biased PN junction, the quasi-Fermi levels stay nearly flat across the depletion layer, and their separation is nearly equal to the applied voltage (EFn − EFp ≈ qV). The minority-carrier density at the depletion-layer edge is therefore exp(qV/kT) times its equilibrium value, which leads to the diode current equation. In a solar cell, light-generated carriers split the quasi-Fermi levels, and the split with the terminals open corresponds to the open-circuit voltage. In a semiconductor laser, gain from stimulated emission appears once the quasi-Fermi-level separation exceeds the photon energy, a prerequisite for lasing.

Current and quasi-Fermi-level gradients

Quasi-Fermi levels let drift and diffusion currents be written together. The electron current density is Jn = n·µn·dEFn/dx and the hole current density is Jp = p·µp·dEFp/dx. In other words, current flows in proportion to the gradient of the quasi-Fermi level, and without a gradient no current flows. In regions with high carrier density, the quasi-Fermi level barely tilts even when a large current flows. Device simulators also commonly solve the current continuity equations using the quasi-Fermi levels, or the corresponding potentials, as variables.

Answer: B. Quasi-Fermi levels

Out of equilibrium, under illumination or current injection, one Fermi level cannot describe both electron and hole densities. Separate quasi-Fermi levels (imrefs) are defined for electrons and holes, so that n = ni·exp((EFn−Ei)/kT), and so on. Their separation, via np = ni²·exp(ΔEF/kT), corresponds to the voltage across a PN junction or a solar cell's open-circuit voltage. Current flows in proportion to the gradient of the quasi-Fermi levels.

FAQ

What is a quasi-Fermi level?

It is one of two Fermi levels defined separately to describe the electron and hole densities in a semiconductor driven out of thermal equilibrium by light or current injection. The electron level is written EFn and the hole level EFp; in thermal equilibrium they coincide.

What does the separation of the quasi-Fermi levels represent?

It represents how many excess carriers there are, through np = ni²·exp((EFn − EFp)/kT). In a PN junction the separation corresponds to the applied voltage, and in a solar cell the separation with the terminals open corresponds to the open-circuit voltage.

What is the difference between the Fermi level and a quasi-Fermi level?

The Fermi level is a single level that describes both electron and hole distributions in thermal equilibrium. Quasi-Fermi levels are defined separately for electrons and holes out of equilibrium, treating each as nearly in equilibrium within its own band.
