What is Selectivity?

In etching, the ratio of the etch rate of the target film to that of the mask or underlying film. Higher selectivity means less damage to the underlayer.

Japanese name: 選択比

Selectivity appears in 16 practice questions across 5 topics, including Etching, Process materials (gases and chemicals), CMP and cleaning.

Field: Etching

Calculating selectivity and the mask budget

Selectivity is computed separately for each film that must survive: selectivity to the mask and selectivity to the underlying layer are usually quoted as two different numbers. If oxide etches at 100 nm/min while the resist mask erodes at 20 nm/min, selectivity to the mask is 100 ÷ 20 = 5, so removing 500 nm of oxide consumes 100 nm of resist. After allowing for overetch and for faster erosion at mask edges, the mask should be at least about 150–200 nm thick. When resist cannot provide that margin, a hard mask such as oxide, nitride or a carbon film is used instead.

Protecting the underlayer during overetch

Etch rate and film thickness vary across the wafer, so when the endpoint is detected some film remains, especially along step sidewalls. An overetch, often 20–50% of the main etch time, clears it, but meanwhile the underlying layer is exposed. Gate etching shows why this matters: polysilicon over a gate dielectric only a few nanometres thick needs selectivity above 100. The usual recipe is a fast main etch that sets the profile, then a switch to gentler, highly selective conditions, typically Cl2/HBr chemistry that forms volatile SiCl4 and SiBr4 while barely attacking oxide.

How chemistry creates selectivity

Selective etching usually exploits a reaction that proceeds on one material and stalls on another. In oxide etching, carbon-rich fluorocarbons such as C4F8 or C4F6 deposit polymer on every surface; oxygen released from the oxide consumes the polymer so oxide keeps etching, while on nitride or silicon the polymer remains and etching stops, which enables self-aligned contacts. Adding oxygen to the gas burns off polymer and lowers selectivity. Wet and gas-phase examples include hot phosphoric acid for nitride over oxide, XeF2 for silicon with selectivity above 1000, and SiGe removal for nanosheet release.

Selectivity vs. anisotropy

Selectivity and anisotropy often pull against each other. Wet etching easily achieves high selectivity but proceeds isotropically, undercutting the mask by about the film thickness. Dry etching gains directionality from ion bombardment, yet energetic ions also sputter masks and underlayers, so raising ion energy tends to lower selectivity. Process engineers balance gas chemistry, ion energy and passivation to get both. Selectivity is also distinct from uniformity, which describes etch-rate variation across the wafer, and the same concept applies to CMP, where ceria slurries polish oxide tens to over a hundred times faster than nitride.

Answer: C. The ratio of the etch rate of the target film to that of the mask or underlying film

Selectivity is the ratio of the etch rate of the film being removed to that of a film that should remain (mask or underlying layer). Gate etching, for example, may need selectivity above 100 to the underlying gate dielectric only a few nm thick. Higher selectivity permits thinner masks and spares underlying layers. Variation of etch rate across the wafer is called uniformity.

  1. A0.2
  2. B5
  3. C80

Answer: B. 5

Selectivity is the etch rate of the target film divided by that of the mask (or underlayer): 100 ÷ 20 = 5. Etching 500 nm of oxide consumes 100 nm of mask, so allowing for overetch and edge erosion the mask should be at least about 150–200 nm thick. If selectivity is insufficient, a thicker mask or a hard mask is used.

FAQ

How is etch selectivity calculated?

Divide the etch rate of the film being removed by the etch rate of the film that should remain, such as the mask or underlayer. For example, 100 nm/min of oxide against 20 nm/min of resist gives a selectivity of 5.

What is the difference between selectivity and anisotropy?

Selectivity compares etch rates of different materials, while anisotropy compares vertical and lateral etch rates within the same material. An etch can be highly selective but isotropic, as many wet etches are.

Why does adding oxygen to a fluorocarbon etch reduce selectivity?

Oxygen burns off the carbon-containing polymer that protects nitride, silicon and resist, and it frees more fluorine radicals. Etching then proceeds on those materials too, and resist erodes faster.
