What is SPM (sulfuric acid-hydrogen peroxide mixture)?

A mixture of sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide. Its strong oxidizing power removes organics such as resist.

Japanese name: SPM(硫酸過水)

SPM (sulfuric acid-hydrogen peroxide mixture) appears in 4 practice questions across 1 topic, including CMP and cleaning.

Field: CMP and cleaning

How SPM destroys organics

Mixing concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) forms peroxymonosulfuric acid (H2SO5, Caro's acid), an extremely strong oxidizer. Sulfuric acid also dehydrates hydrocarbons, so photoresist and other organic films are broken down and oxidized to carbon dioxide and water. Typical mixtures run from about 2:1 to 4:1 acid to peroxide at roughly 120–150 °C; mixing is strongly exothermic, which supplies part of the heat. Because peroxide decomposes during use, baths are replenished with fresh H2O2 to keep their stripping power.

Where SPM sits in a process flow

SPM is the workhorse for removing resist residue after plasma ashing and for stripping resist after ion implantation, where heavy implant doses harden the resist surface into a crust. It is often the first step of a pre-furnace clean, followed by SC-1 (ammonia and peroxide) for particles, SC-2 (hydrochloric acid and peroxide) for metals and dilute HF for oxide. SPM leaves a thin chemical oxide on silicon and barely etches silicon or SiO2, but it dissolves many metals, which is why it can strip unreacted nickel after silicide formation yet must not touch exposed copper or aluminum.

SPM vs. ozonated water

Ozonated water, ultrapure water carrying a few to tens of ppm of dissolved ozone, also oxidizes organics and works at room temperature. It reverts to oxygen after use, so waste treatment is simple, whereas SPM consumes large volumes of sulfuric acid and peroxide and needs heating, exhaust and acid recovery. SPM remains stronger for thick or implant-hardened resist, so fabs replace it with ozone-based or other sulfuric-acid-free cleans where the residue is light enough.

Rinsing and safety

Sulfuric acid is viscous and adsorbs strongly, so a short or cold rinse leaves sulfate on the surface. That sulfate can later combine with airborne ammonia to grow ammonium sulfate crystals, seen as haze on wafers and, historically, as progressive haze defects on photomasks. Hot ultrapure-water rinses address this. Hot SPM is nicknamed piranha solution: it reacts violently with organic solvents, releases gas as peroxide decomposes, and must never be stored in sealed containers.

Answer: A. Removing organics such as resist

SPM (H2SO4:H2O2, e.g., 4:1 at about 120–150 °C) forms peroxymonosulfuric acid (Caro's acid), whose powerful oxidizing action decomposes organic residues such as resist. Used after ashing and for post-implant resist strip, it is so reactive it is nicknamed "piranha solution" and demands careful handling. Answer: A. Sulfate ions and the like remain on the surface and later cause haze and defects

Viscous sulfuric acid adsorbs readily, so inadequate rinsing leaves sulfate ions that react with airborne ammonia to precipitate ammonium sulfate microcrystals, causing haze and defects. On photomasks this produced progressive haze defects that reduced transmission. Thorough hot-water rinsing and sulfuric-acid-free cleaning are the countermeasures.

FAQ

Why is SPM called piranha solution?

Because hot sulfuric acid and peroxide attack organic material so aggressively that resist and other organics are eaten away within minutes. The nickname is also a reminder that the mixture is dangerous to handle.

What is the difference between SPM and SC-1 cleaning?

SPM is an acidic, strongly oxidizing mixture aimed at heavy organic contamination such as resist. SC-1 is an alkaline ammonia-peroxide mixture aimed mainly at particles and light organics, using slight etching of the surface to lift particles off.

Why must wafers be rinsed well after SPM?

Residual sulfate stays on the surface and can later react with ammonia in the air to form ammonium sulfate crystals that appear as haze and defects. Thorough, typically hot, ultrapure-water rinsing removes it.
