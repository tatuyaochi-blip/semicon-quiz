What is Auger recombination?

Recombination in which the released energy is given to another carrier instead of being emitted as light. It becomes significant at high carrier densities and affects lifetime in heavily doped layers and efficiency loss in LEDs.

Japanese name: オージェ再結合

Auger recombination appears in 2 practice questions across 2 topics, including Semiconductor physics, Compound semiconductors.

Field: Semiconductor physics

A three-carrier process

In Auger recombination an electron and a hole recombine and hand the released energy, roughly the band-gap energy, to a third carrier, either another electron (eeh process) or another hole (ehh process). That carrier is kicked high into its band and then loses the energy as heat by emitting phonons. The rate is commonly written R = Cn·n²p + Cp·np², with Auger coefficients Cn and Cp. In heavily doped n-type material this gives a lifetime that falls with the square of the doping.

Comparing the three recombination paths

A simple way to compare mechanisms is by how their rates scale with carrier density n under high injection: SRH recombination scales roughly with n, radiative recombination with n², and Auger recombination with n³. At low densities, SRH dominates in silicon; at very high densities from heavy doping or strong injection, Auger takes over. Auger processes also become stronger as the band gap narrows, which makes them a key loss in infrared detectors and long-wavelength lasers.

Where it limits devices

Heavily doped emitters of bipolar transistors and solar cells suffer short Auger lifetimes, which reduces emitter efficiency and increases recombination current. Power devices such as PiN diodes and IGBTs operate under high injection, where Auger recombination limits the stored carrier density. Because Auger recombination is intrinsic to the material and cannot be removed by purification, it sets the fundamental upper limit on silicon minority-carrier lifetime and contributes to the theoretical efficiency limit of silicon solar cells.

LED efficiency droop

Indium gallium nitride (InGaN) LEDs reach peak efficiency at relatively low current densities of a few A/cm² and become less efficient as current rises, a phenomenon called efficiency droop. Auger recombination, growing with the cube of carrier density, and electron leakage over the active region are considered the main causes. The often-used ABC model writes internal quantum efficiency as Bn² / (An + Bn² + Cn³). High-power lighting lowers current density by using larger or multiple chips.

Answer: B. Auger recombination

In Auger recombination, the energy released is handed to a third carrier and is eventually lost as heat. Because three carriers are involved, the rate scales roughly with the cube of carrier density and grows sharply at high concentration. It therefore limits heavily doped emitters and solar-cell efficiency, and is considered one cause of "droop," the drop in LED efficiency at high current. It differs from radiative recombination, which emits light.

Answer: A. Efficiency droop

InGaN LED efficiency peaks at relatively low current densities of a few A/cm² and declines as current increases—efficiency droop. Auger recombination, which scales with the cube of carrier density, and electron leakage over the active region are thought to be the main causes. High-power lighting lowers current density by using larger chips or many chips; laser-based lighting, with less droop, is also being explored.

FAQ

Why does Auger recombination scale with the cube of carrier density?

Three carriers must meet: an electron, a hole and a third carrier to receive the energy, so the rate is proportional to n²p or np², roughly n³ when n and p are similar.

Does Auger recombination emit light?

No. The energy goes to a third carrier and ends up as heat, which is why it is a non-radiative loss in LEDs and lasers.

Is Auger recombination important in silicon?

Yes, in heavily doped regions and under high injection, where it dominates over SRH recombination and limits lifetime regardless of material purity.
