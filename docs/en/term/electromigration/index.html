What is Electromigration?

The movement of metal atoms in interconnects caused by electric current. It creates voids and hillocks that lead to open or short circuits.

Japanese name: エレクトロマイグレーション

Electromigration appears in 26 practice questions across 8 topics, including Interconnect processes, Reliability, Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA).

Field: Reliability

The electron wind and where damage forms

Conduction electrons scatter off metal ions and transfer momentum, pushing atoms in the direction of electron flow, which is opposite to conventional current. Atoms travel mostly along fast diffusion paths: grain boundaries in aluminum and the top interface between copper and its dielectric cap in copper. Damage appears where this atomic flux is interrupted, such as at a via bottom blocked by the barrier metal. Atoms leave the upstream side without replacement and a void forms, raising resistance or opening the line, while atoms pile up downstream as hillocks that can crack the dielectric and short to a neighboring line.

Black's equation and lifetime testing

Black's equation, MTTF = A·J⁻ⁿ·exp(Ea/kT), says median lifetime falls with current density J, with n typically about 2, and falls exponentially with temperature. Reliability tests therefore stress lines at high current density and temperatures around 300 °C, then extrapolate to use conditions. Failure times follow a lognormal distribution, and because a chip holds hundreds of millions of wires, the design target is the time when a tiny fraction, such as 0.1 ppm, has failed. This sets per-wire current-density limits on the order of MA/cm².

Materials and structures that resist it

Adding about 0.5% copper to aluminum puts copper at grain boundaries and slows aluminum transport by an order of magnitude or more. Copper wiring, with resistivity about 1.7 µΩ·cm versus about 2.7 µΩ·cm for aluminum and a higher melting point, improved electromigration resistance further. Because copper moves mainly along its top surface, the dielectric cap (SiCN or SiN) and selective metal caps such as cobalt govern lifetime. A bamboo grain structure helps aluminum greatly but is not enough for copper. In short lines the Blech effect applies: back-stress stops net transport when current density times length is below a critical value.

Design checks and related failures

Designers add redundant vias, widen metal around vias, and run electromigration analysis together with IR-drop analysis at sign-off. Self-heating in FinFET and SOI devices raises nearby wire temperature and speeds failure. In flip-chip solder bumps, current crowding at the trace-to-bump corner nucleates voids, one reason copper pillars are favored. Electromigration should not be confused with stress migration, where voids form from thermal stress without current, or with ion migration, where moisture lets metal ions grow dendrites between biased electrodes on boards.

Answer: B. High current density and high temperature

Black's equation gives mean time to failure as MTTF = A·J⁻ⁿ·exp(Ea/kT): inversely proportional to current density J to the power n (about 2) and exponentially shorter at higher temperature. Tests therefore accelerate failure at elevated current density and temperature (e.g., around 300 °C) and extrapolate to use conditions with the equation. Designs impose per-wire current-density limits, on the order of MA/cm².

Answer: A. Where the flow of metal atoms is discontinuous, such as at the boundary between a via and a wire

In electromigration, atoms are pushed along by the electron flow (the electron wind). Where atomic flux is interrupted—at via-to-line interfaces or the barrier beneath a via—atoms leave the upstream side without replacement, forming voids, while they accumulate downstream as hillocks. Design countermeasures include redundant vias, wider metal around vias and attention to current direction.

FAQ

What is the difference between electromigration and stress migration?

Electromigration is driven by current, as electrons push metal atoms along the wire. Stress migration forms voids without any current, as atoms move to relieve tensile stress left by thermal mismatch, and it is worst around 150–250 °C.

Why does copper resist electromigration better than aluminum?

Copper has a higher melting point, so its atoms are less mobile at operating temperatures, giving an order of magnitude or more improvement in lifetime. Its lower resistivity also means less heating for the same current.

What is the Blech effect?

In short wires, atoms pushed toward one end build up a stress gradient that drives a backflow. When the product of current density and line length is below a critical value, this backflow cancels the electron wind and the line does not degrade.
