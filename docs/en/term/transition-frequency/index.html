What is Transition frequency?

The frequency at which the common-emitter current gain falls to 1, written fT. It is a key indicator of a transistor's high-frequency performance.

Japanese name: トランジション周波数

Field: Bipolar transistors

How fT is defined and measured

The transition frequency fT is the frequency at which the common-emitter current gain |hfe|, measured with the output AC-shorted, falls to 1 (0 dB). At low frequencies the gain is a nearly constant β0, but above the beta cutoff frequency fβ it falls in inverse proportion to frequency (−20 dB/decade). In that region |hfe| × f is nearly constant, so fT ≈ β0·fβ, and fT can be viewed as the gain-bandwidth product of the current gain. In practice, fT is usually found by measuring |hfe| at a frequency f below fT and taking fT = |hfe| × f, or by extrapolating the current gain derived from S-parameters along the −20 dB/decade slope.

The delays that set fT

The reciprocal of fT corresponds to the total time for a signal to travel from emitter to collector, roughly 1/(2πfT) ≈ (Cje + Cμ)/gm + τB + τC. The first term is the time to charge the junction capacitances. τB is the time minority carriers take to cross the base, about WB²/(2Dn) for a uniformly doped base of width WB with diffusion coefficient Dn. τC is the transit time through the collector depletion region. In the small-signal equivalent circuit this becomes fT ≈ gm/(2π(Cπ + Cμ)), where Cπ includes the base diffusion capacitance. Thinning the base, or grading the Ge content in a SiGe HBT to accelerate electrons, mainly serves to shorten τB.

How fT depends on collector current

fT varies strongly with bias. Because gm is proportional to collector current IC, at low currents the capacitance-charging time (Cje + Cμ)/gm dominates and fT rises as IC increases. At excessive current density, however, the charge of electrons flowing through the collector cancels the field in the depletion region and the effective base spreads toward the collector. This Kirk effect (base pushout) abruptly lengthens τB and fT falls, so fT peaks at a particular IC. Datasheet fT values are specified at a given IC and VCE, so the actual fT differs if a circuit operates at a different bias point.

fT vs. fmax, and fT in MOSFETs

fT is a current-gain metric and barely reflects the base resistance rb. The maximum oscillation frequency fmax, where power gain falls to 1, is roughly √(fT/(8π·rb·Cμ)) and drops when rb or Cμ is large, so amplifier and oscillator capability is judged with fmax as well. The fT definition also applies to MOSFETs and HEMTs, where fT ≈ gm/(2π(Cgs + Cgd)). In the long-channel approximation neglecting Cgd, fT ≈ 3μ(VGS − Vth)/(4πL²), inversely proportional to the square of gate length L; in short channels with strong velocity saturation it approaches vsat/(2πL), so fT rises with scaling.

Answer: B. The frequency at which the common-emitter current gain falls to 1

Current gain falls at high frequency, mainly because of base transit time and charging of junction capacitances. The frequency at which common-emitter current gain drops to 1 is the transition frequency fT, roughly fT ≈ gm/(2π(Cπ + Cμ)). It is a key high-frequency metric: a few hundred MHz for general-purpose small-signal transistors and above 300 GHz for some SiGe HBTs. The maximum oscillation frequency fmax, where power gain reaches 1, is also used.

FAQ

What is the transition frequency, in simple terms?

It is the frequency at which the common-emitter current gain falls to 1, a measure of the upper limit up to which a transistor can amplify current. Written fT, it is also called the current-gain cutoff frequency or unity-gain frequency. The higher it is, the higher the frequencies the transistor can amplify.

Is fT the same as the gain-bandwidth product?

Above the beta cutoff frequency fβ, current gain falls in inverse proportion to frequency, so |hfe| × f is nearly constant, and that value is fT. In this sense fT is the gain-bandwidth product of the current gain, with fT ≈ β0·fβ. It is, however, a different quantity from the gain-bandwidth product of a whole circuit such as an op amp.

How do fT and fmax differ?

fT is the frequency at which current gain falls to 1; fmax is where power gain falls to 1. fmax depends strongly on base resistance and collector-base capacitance, and depending on device structure it can be higher or lower than fT.
