What is Degenerate semiconductor?

A semiconductor doped so heavily that the Fermi level lies inside the conduction or valence band. It behaves almost like a metal and is used in tunnel diodes and low-resistance contacts.

Japanese name: 縮退半導体

Field: Semiconductor physics

When a semiconductor becomes degenerate

In a nondegenerate n-type semiconductor, Ec − EF ≈ kT·ln(Nc/ND), where Nc is the effective density of states in the conduction band, about 3×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for silicon at room temperature. As donor concentration ND approaches Nc, this gap shrinks to zero and the Fermi level enters the band. In practice silicon is treated as degenerate from roughly 10¹⁸–10¹⁹ cm⁻³ upward, and the usual criterion is that the Fermi level lies within a few kT of the band edge or inside the band. The same applies to p-type doping and the valence band.

What changes physically

The simple Boltzmann approximation to Fermi-Dirac statistics no longer holds, so formulas built on it, including n × p = ni², become inaccurate. Donor levels broaden into an impurity band that merges with the conduction band, and the band gap effectively shrinks, an effect called bandgap narrowing. Carriers no longer freeze out at low temperature, and resistivity changes little with temperature, much like a metal. Mobility, however, is low because carriers scatter strongly off the dense ionized impurities.

Where degenerate doping is used

MOSFET sources and drains are degenerately doped to minimize series resistance. At metal-semiconductor contacts, very high doping thins the depletion region enough that carriers tunnel through the Schottky barrier, giving a low-resistance ohmic contact. A tunnel (Esaki) diode uses degenerate doping on both sides so that, at small forward bias, electrons tunnel directly between bands, producing negative differential resistance. Bipolar emitters and transparent conducting oxides such as indium tin oxide are other examples of degenerately doped materials.

Degenerate vs. nondegenerate semiconductors

In a nondegenerate semiconductor the Fermi level sits in the gap, carrier density follows dopant density through simple exponential formulas, and the material is clearly semiconducting. In a degenerate one the Fermi level sits in a band, Fermi-Dirac integrals are needed to calculate carrier density, and conduction is quasi-metallic. Device simulators switch statistics accordingly; applying nondegenerate formulas at very high doping misplaces the Fermi level and ignores bandgap narrowing, which distorts calculated built-in potentials and currents.

Answer: B. Closer to the conduction band than the intrinsic Fermi level

In n-type material the conduction band holds many electrons, so the Fermi level moves from the intrinsic level near midgap toward the conduction band. Its position is given by Ec − EF ≈ kT·ln(Nc/ND), so it moves about 60 mV (at room temperature) closer to the conduction band for every tenfold increase in donor concentration. In p-type it moves toward the valence band. At very high doping it can even enter a band (a degenerate semiconductor).

  2. BInsulator

Answer: C. Degenerate semiconductor

When the doping is very high (roughly above 10¹⁸–10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in silicon), the Fermi level moves into the conduction or valence band and the material behaves almost like a metal. This is a degenerate semiconductor: the simple n × p = ni² relation no longer holds and the band gap appears smaller (bandgap narrowing). Examples include MOSFET sources and drains, regions for low-resistance metal contacts, and the heavily doped regions of tunnel diodes.

FAQ

At what doping level does silicon become degenerate?

Roughly above 10¹⁸–10¹⁹ cm⁻³, when the Fermi level reaches or enters the conduction or valence band; the exact threshold depends on temperature and the criterion used.

Why are contacts made to heavily doped regions?

Degenerate doping makes the depletion region under the metal so thin that carriers tunnel through the barrier, turning a rectifying Schottky contact into a low-resistance ohmic contact.

Is a degenerate semiconductor a metal?

It behaves almost like one, with weak temperature dependence of resistivity, but it is still a doped semiconductor with lower carrier density and mobility than a true metal.
