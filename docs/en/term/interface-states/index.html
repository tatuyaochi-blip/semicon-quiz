What is Interface states?

Energy levels within the band gap at a semiconductor-insulator interface, created by broken bonds and similar defects. They trap and release carriers, causing threshold shifts, lower mobility and noise.

Japanese name: 界面準位

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

Where interface states come from

At the Si/SiO2 interface not every silicon atom finds an oxygen partner, leaving unsatisfied dangling bonds. These defects create allowed energy levels spread across the silicon band gap. As-oxidized interfaces typically carry about 10¹¹ to 10¹² cm⁻²eV⁻¹, and the density depends on crystal orientation, which is one reason MOSFETs are built on (100) wafers. Unlike fixed oxide charge, the charge in interface states changes with gate bias, because whether each level is filled depends on where the Fermi level sits at the surface.

Effects on devices

Charge trapped in interface states shifts the flat-band and threshold voltages and stretches the capacitance-voltage (C-V) curve. Because the traps must be charged as the gate sweeps the surface potential, they add a capacitance that degrades subthreshold slope, so more gate voltage is needed per decade of current. Charged traps also scatter channel carriers and reduce mobility. Capture and emission of carriers produce low-frequency 1/f noise, and states near midgap act as generation centers that create leakage and dark current in image sensors.

Passivation and degradation

A forming gas anneal at about 400 to 450 °C, in nitrogen with a few percent hydrogen, ties hydrogen to dangling bonds and lowers the density to around 10¹⁰ cm⁻²eV⁻¹. Those Si-H bonds can later break: hot carriers near the drain, negative bias temperature instability (NBTI) in p-channel devices and ionizing radiation all generate new interface states, causing threshold drift over the device's life. High-k gate dielectrics form poorer interfaces with silicon, so a thin SiO2 interlayer is normally kept between them.

How they are measured

Interface trap charge (Qit) is one of four standard MOS oxide charge types, together with fixed charge (Qf), mobile ionic charge (Qm) and oxide trapped charge (Qot). It is distinguished by its bias and frequency dependence. Common methods compare high-frequency and quasi-static C-V curves, analyze the conductance of a MOS capacitor versus frequency, or use charge pumping, which pulses a transistor's gate and measures the resulting substrate current to obtain trap density directly on small devices.

Answer: B. Forming gas anneal

The Si/SiO2 interface retains interface states from dangling silicon bonds at about 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻²eV⁻¹. Annealing at about 400–450 °C in forming gas (a few percent hydrogen in nitrogen) lets hydrogen passivate those bonds, reducing interface states to about 10¹⁰ cm⁻²eV⁻¹ and improving threshold stability, subthreshold slope and mobility. It is often performed at the end of interconnect processing.

FAQ

What is the difference between interface states and fixed oxide charge?

Fixed oxide charge sits in the oxide near the interface and does not change with bias, so it only shifts the C-V curve. Interface states exchange carriers with the silicon, so their charge varies with gate bias and they also distort the curve and degrade subthreshold slope.

Why does forming gas anneal reduce interface traps?

Hydrogen diffuses to the Si/SiO2 interface during the anneal and bonds to silicon dangling bonds, removing their energy levels from the band gap.

How do interface states affect subthreshold slope?

They add a trap capacitance in parallel with the depletion capacitance, so the gate controls the surface potential less efficiently and more millivolts are needed per decade of drain current.
