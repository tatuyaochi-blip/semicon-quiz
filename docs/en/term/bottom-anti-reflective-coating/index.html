What is Bottom anti-reflective coating (BARC)?

A layer coated under the resist that suppresses standing waves in the resist and dimension variation over topography caused by light reflected from the substrate.

Japanese name: 反射防止膜(BARC)

Field: Lithography

What substrate reflection does to resist

Exposure light that passes through the photoresist reflects from the layers below, and silicon and metal films reflect strongly. The reflected wave interferes with the incoming wave and sets up standing waves, so the dose varies periodically through the resist thickness and developed sidewalls show ripples. The total energy coupled into the resist also oscillates with resist thickness, making linewidth swing periodically with small thickness changes, which is called the swing curve. Over topography, light reflected sideways from sloped edges exposes regions under the mask and causes notching.

How a BARC suppresses reflection

A bottom anti-reflective coating (BARC) is a thin layer placed between the substrate and the resist. Its refractive index n, absorption coefficient k and thickness are chosen so that light entering it is absorbed and the residual reflections from its top and bottom surfaces largely cancel by destructive interference. A well-tuned BARC reduces reflectivity back into the resist to below about 1%, which flattens the swing curve and removes most standing-wave and notching effects. Because the optimum thickness depends on wavelength and the underlying stack, BARC thickness is specified for each layer.

Organic vs. inorganic BARCs

Organic BARCs are spin-coated and baked polymers containing light-absorbing groups; they also smooth small topography and provide a good surface for resist adhesion, a role otherwise played by HMDS priming. Most do not dissolve in developer, so after lithography the BARC exposed in the resist openings is removed by a short plasma etch before the underlying film is etched, which consumes some resist. Inorganic BARCs, such as silicon oxynitride deposited by CVD, are tuned through composition and can remain in the film stack or double as a hard mask.

BARC vs. TARC and top coat

A top anti-reflective coating (TARC) sits on top of the resist and reduces reflection at the air-resist interface, which mainly reduces the swing effect; it does not stop reflection from the substrate. A top coat in ArF immersion lithography serves a different purpose: it keeps the immersion water from leaching resist components such as the photoacid generator and keeps the surface hydrophobic for fast stage motion. A pellicle, by contrast, is a membrane that protects the photomask from particles, not part of the wafer film stack.

Answer: B. To suppress standing waves in the resist and dimensional variation caused by reflection from the underlying layers

Light passing through resist and reflecting from the substrate interferes with incoming light, creating standing waves (vertical ripples) in the resist, periodic linewidth changes with slight resist-thickness variations (the swing curve), and notching where light reflects obliquely off topography. A BARC—an organic or inorganic absorbing layer—cuts substrate reflectivity below about 1%, improving dimensional accuracy.

FAQ

What does BARC stand for in lithography?

BARC stands for bottom anti-reflective coating, an absorbing layer coated under the photoresist to suppress reflections from the substrate during exposure.

What is the difference between BARC and TARC?

A BARC sits below the resist and suppresses substrate reflection, standing waves and notching, while a TARC sits on top of the resist and reduces reflection at the top surface, mainly reducing swing-curve variation.

How is a BARC removed after lithography?

Most organic BARCs are not removed by the developer, so the BARC in the resist openings is opened with a short plasma etch before the underlying film is etched, and the remainder is stripped with the resist.
