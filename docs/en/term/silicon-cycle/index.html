What is Silicon cycle?

The recurring boom-and-bust pattern of the semiconductor market driven by supply and demand. One cause is that fabs take years to build, so supply lags changes in demand.

Japanese name: シリコンサイクル

Why the cycle happens

When demand for chips rises faster than capacity, prices climb and manufacturers announce new fabs. A fab takes roughly two years from construction to volume production, so the new capacity arrives after the demand surge has often cooled. The result is oversupply, falling prices and inventory build-up, which leads companies to cut investment. With little new capacity coming online, the next demand upturn produces shortages again. It is a classic delay-driven oscillation, similar to a feedback loop with a long lag.

Period and what amplifies it

Historically the cycle has run about four years from peak to peak, but the length and depth vary with the economy and with new applications. Memory such as DRAM and NAND flash tends to swing hardest because the products are interchangeable commodities priced largely by supply and demand. Inventory adjustments along the supply chain magnify the swing: distributors and device makers over-order during shortages and stop ordering during gluts, so chipmakers and especially equipment suppliers see larger fluctuations than end demand.

Silicon cycle vs. Moore's law

The silicon cycle is a market phenomenon, while Moore's law and Dennard scaling are technology trends about transistor density and power. The two overlap: revenue follows a long-term growth trend driven by technology and new applications, with the cycle superimposed as booms and busts around it. Diversification into More than Moore products such as power, sensors and RF, or the rise of custom chips designed by large data-center operators, changes which segments lead each cycle but has not removed the underlying capacity lag.

How companies live with it

Because fabs are capital-intensive and must run near full utilization to be profitable, a downturn quickly turns into losses. Companies respond with countercyclical strategies such as investing during downturns so capacity is ready for the next upturn, long-term supply agreements with customers, and flexible capacity that can be shifted between products. Watching inventory levels, fab utilization and equipment orders helps engineers and analysts recognize which phase of the cycle the industry is in.

Answer: B. Silicon cycle

The silicon cycle is the recurring boom and bust in which capacity investment made during demand growth leads to oversupply a few years later, followed by price declines, investment cuts and renewed shortages. Because a fab takes about two years from construction to volume production, investment decisions lag demand changes; the cycle has traditionally been about four years. Moore's law and Dennard scaling are technology rules of thumb.

  1. ADennard scaling
  2. BSilicon cycle
  3. CMore than Moore

Answer: C. More than Moore

More than Moore refers to adding function and value through technologies other than scaling, such as sensors, MEMS, power, RF and advanced packaging, in contrast to More Moore, which pursues scaling. These products do not necessarily need leading-edge geometries and can create value on mature processes. Their importance grows with diverse applications such as IoT and automotive. Dennard scaling and the silicon cycle are different concepts.

FAQ

How long is the semiconductor cycle?

It has traditionally been about four years from one peak to the next, but it is not fixed; its length and depth depend on the wider economy, inventory levels and new demand drivers.

Why does the semiconductor industry have boom and bust cycles?

New fabs take years to build, so capacity decisions made during a boom come online after demand has cooled, causing oversupply and price drops; reduced investment then sets up the next shortage.

Why is the memory market more cyclical than logic?

Standard DRAM and NAND chips are largely interchangeable between suppliers, so prices respond strongly to small imbalances of supply and demand, whereas many logic chips are custom designs made under longer-term contracts.
