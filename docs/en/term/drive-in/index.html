What is Drive-in?

In thermal diffusion, the high-temperature step after a fixed amount of dopant has been introduced at the surface (predeposition), which pushes the dopant deeper.

Japanese name: ドライブイン

Field: Oxidation and diffusion

Where drive-in fits in two-step diffusion

Classical thermal diffusion separates dose control from depth control. First, predeposition exposes the wafer to a dopant source, such as phosphorus oxychloride for phosphorus or a boron compound for boron, while the surface stays at a constant concentration, typically the solid solubility at that temperature. This gives a shallow complementary error function (erfc) profile. The doped glass left on the surface is then etched off, and drive-in heats the wafer with no further supply, so the fixed dose spreads deeper to set the final junction depth.

The Gaussian profile and junction depth

With a fixed dose Q and no surface supply, the concentration follows a Gaussian: C(x,t) = Q/√(πDt) · exp(−x²/4Dt), where D is the diffusion coefficient. As drive-in continues, the profile broadens with the diffusion length √(Dt) while the surface concentration falls in proportion to 1/√(Dt). The junction lies where the dopant concentration equals the background doping of opposite type. Because D rises exponentially with temperature following an Arrhenius law, temperature control matters more than time, and the product Dt is called the thermal budget.

Interaction with oxidation

Drive-in is often done in an oxidizing ambient so a masking or passivating oxide grows at the same time, which couples it to oxidation physics. Dopants redistribute at the moving silicon-oxide interface: boron tends to be absorbed into the oxide, depleting the silicon surface, while phosphorus piles up on the silicon side. Oxidation also injects silicon interstitials, which enhances the diffusion of boron and phosphorus, an effect called oxidation-enhanced diffusion. Real profiles at high concentrations therefore deviate from the ideal Gaussian.

Drive-in today

In modern CMOS, ion implantation replaced predeposition because it sets the dose precisely, and short rapid thermal, spike or laser anneals activate dopants while minimizing their movement. Long drive-in style anneals remain useful wherever deep junctions are the goal: wells in mature processes, the deep diffused regions of power devices, and emitters of crystalline silicon solar cells, which are still commonly formed by phosphorus diffusion. Whatever the dose source, the same diffusion equations describe how dopants move during any high-temperature step.

Answer: C. Drive-in

Thermal diffusion was often done in two steps: predeposition introduces a set dose shallowly while holding surface concentration constant, then drive-in stops the supply and heats the wafer to spread that fixed dose to the desired depth and profile. Today dopants are usually introduced by ion implantation, which controls dose precisely, followed by anneals for activation and profile shaping, though diffusion remains in use for solar cells and deep junctions in power devices.

In thermal diffusion, which pair correctly gives the profile shapes for diffusion with a constant surface concentration and for diffusion of a fixed amount of impurity?

  1. AConstant surface concentration: complementary error function (erfc); fixed amount: Gaussian
  2. BBoth are linear profiles
  3. CConstant surface concentration: Gaussian; fixed amount: complementary error function
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Constant surface concentration: complementary error function (erfc); fixed amount: Gaussian

Predeposition with constant surface concentration produces a complementary error function (erfc) profile, with the incorporated dose growing over time. Drive-in of a fixed dose spreads it out while the surface concentration falls, producing a Gaussian profile. Combining the two allows surface concentration and junction depth to be set independently. Real high-concentration diffusion deviates from these ideal shapes.

FAQ

What is the difference between predeposition and drive-in?

Predeposition introduces dopant at a constant surface concentration and controls the dose, giving an erfc profile. Drive-in removes the source and heats the wafer to push that fixed dose deeper, giving a Gaussian profile.

Why does drive-in produce a Gaussian profile?

The total amount of dopant is fixed and only redistributes by diffusion, which is the classic solution of the diffusion equation for a constant-dose source at the surface.

Is drive-in still used after ion implantation?

Yes, long anneals still drive implanted dopants deep in wells and power devices, though advanced transistors use very short anneals to keep junctions shallow.
