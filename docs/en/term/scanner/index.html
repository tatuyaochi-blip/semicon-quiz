What is Scanner?

An exposure tool that moves the mask and wafer in sync under a slit of light. It is the mainstream production lithography tool and generally projects the mask pattern at 1/4 size.

Japanese name: スキャナー

Scanner appears in 9 practice questions across 5 topics, including Lithography, Metrology and analysis, Analog and electronic circuits.

Field: Lithography

How scanning exposure works

A scanner never illuminates the whole mask at once. Light passes through a slit a few millimeters wide, and the reticle and wafer stages move synchronously in opposite directions so the image sweeps across the field. Because the projection optics reduce the pattern by 4×, the reticle stage travels four times faster than the wafer stage. A standard field is about 26 mm × 33 mm, roughly 858 mm², with the long side in the scan direction. After each field the wafer steps to the next position, which is why the method is called step-and-scan.

Scanner vs. stepper

A stepper exposes an entire field in one static flash, so its lens must be nearly perfect over the full field area. A scanner only needs a good image over a narrow slit, using the lens's best central region, and it averages lens and illumination variations along the scan direction. The trade-off is mechanical: both stages must accelerate at several m/s² and still track each other with nanometer accuracy during the scan. That capability made larger fields and better uniformity possible, and scanners replaced steppers for critical layers in high-volume manufacturing.

What happens before the light turns on

Each wafer is measured before exposure. Alignment sensors read marks from earlier layers to correct position, rotation and expansion, and a level sensor maps surface height so the image stays within the shallow depth of focus as the wafer moves. Overlay results from downstream metrology are fed back as corrections for later lots. Many scanners carry two wafer stages so one wafer is measured while another is exposed. The tool is linked to a coater/developer track, and temperatures around it are held to about ±0.1 °C to limit thermal distortion.

Light sources and limits

Deep-ultraviolet (DUV) scanners use KrF (248 nm) or ArF (193 nm) excimer lasers with refractive lenses; ArF immersion fills the gap under the lens with water to reach a numerical aperture of about 1.35. Extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) scanners use 13.5 nm light, which every material absorbs, so they rely on mirrors and reflective masks coated with molybdenum/silicon multilayers reflecting about 70%. High-NA EUV halves the exposure field. The field size sets the reticle limit on die area, one reason very large designs are split into chiplets.

Answer: C. They expose while moving the mask and wafer synchronously relative to a slit of light

A scanner illuminates only a slit a few mm wide and moves the mask and wafer synchronously in opposite directions at a 4:1 speed ratio to expose each 26 × 33 mm field. It uses only the low-aberration center of the lens, exposes a long field in the scan direction and averages variations across the field. The stages accelerate at tens of m/s² (several g) or more while holding nanometer precision. A stepper, by contrast, exposes each field statically in one flash.

Answer: C. About 26 mm × 33 mm

A typical scanner exposes about 26 mm × 33 mm (roughly 858 mm²) per shot and steps across the wafer to cover it. This sets the practical maximum die size (the reticle limit), which large GPUs approach. Larger systems are built from chiplets connected in the package. High-NA EUV fields are half this size.

FAQ

What is the difference between a scanner and a stepper?

A stepper exposes each field in one static shot, while a scanner moves the reticle and wafer in sync under a narrow slit of light, which uses less lens area and gives larger, more uniform fields.

What is the maximum field size of a lithography scanner?

About 26 mm × 33 mm per exposure on standard scanners, which effectively limits the largest die that can be printed; high-NA EUV fields are half that size.

Why does the reticle move faster than the wafer in a scanner?

The projection lens shrinks the pattern by 4×, so the reticle must travel four times the distance, and thus four times the speed, of the wafer during each scan.
