What is Pattern collapse?

A defect in which tall, fine patterns topple or stick together during cleaning and drying because of the surface tension of liquid left between them.

Japanese name: パターン倒壊

Pattern collapse appears in 4 practice questions across 3 topics, including CMP and cleaning, Lithography, Process materials (gases and chemicals).

Field: CMP and cleaning

The capillary force behind collapse

When a rinse liquid dries out of the gap between two tall lines, the curved liquid surface creates a pressure difference (Laplace pressure) proportional to the surface tension times the cosine of the contact angle, divided by the gap width. If the liquid level differs on the two sides of a line, the line feels a net sideways force. Treating the line as a cantilever, the bending stress grows roughly with the square of its height-to-width ratio and inversely with the spacing, so shrinking pitch while keeping height makes collapse much more likely.

Where it shows up

Resist patterns are the classic case: after development and rinse, thin, tall resist lines lean and touch their neighbors, which is one reason resist thickness is kept only a few times the line width. The same failure appears after wet cleaning of etched structures such as silicon fins, DRAM capacitor pillars and deep stacked features. Collapsed lines may stay stuck together after drying through adhesion, a related effect often called stiction.

Countermeasures

Each term of the capillary force can be attacked. Replacing water (about 72 mN/m at room temperature) with isopropyl alcohol (IPA, about 22 mN/m) before drying cuts surface tension; treating surfaces so the contact angle approaches 90° drives the cosine toward zero. Supercritical carbon dioxide drying removes the liquid-gas interface altogether, and dry methods such as cryogenic aerosol cleaning avoid liquid entirely. For lithography, thinner resists with high etch resistance, such as metal-oxide resists, reduce aspect ratio.

Collapse vs. other drying defects

Pattern collapse is a mechanical failure of features caused by capillary forces, so it depends strongly on aspect ratio and spacing and appears as leaning or merged lines in top-down inspection. Watermarks, by contrast, are residues left where droplets dried, usually on hydrophobic surfaces, and do not involve deformation. Marangoni drying with IPA vapor helps against both by pulling water off the surface before it can evaporate in place.

Answer: B. Surface tension of liquid remaining between the patterns as it dries

When liquid remains between tall, narrow features during drying, Laplace pressure from surface tension pulls them together so they collapse or stick. The force grows with narrower spacing and higher aspect ratio. Countermeasures include displacing water with low-surface-tension IPA, hydrophobizing surfaces (bringing the contact angle near 90°) and supercritical CO2 drying, which has no liquid-gas interface.

FAQ

Why does IPA drying prevent pattern collapse?

IPA has roughly a third of the surface tension of water, so the capillary force pulling neighboring features together while the liquid evaporates is much smaller. It also mixes readily with water, so it can displace the rinse water completely.

What aspect ratio causes resist pattern collapse?

There is no single threshold, because the risk depends on spacing, resist stiffness, adhesion and the rinse liquid. Since the bending stress scales roughly with the square of aspect ratio, processes keep resist only a few times taller than it is wide.

How does supercritical CO2 drying avoid pattern collapse?

Above its critical point carbon dioxide has no distinct liquid and gas phases, so no meniscus forms as it is removed. Without a meniscus there is no capillary force on the features.
