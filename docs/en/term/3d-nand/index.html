What is 3D NAND?

NAND flash in which memory cells are stacked vertically in many layers to increase capacity. It was introduced to get past the limits of planar scaling, and layer counts now exceed 200.

Japanese name: 3D NAND

3D NAND appears in 19 practice questions across 8 topics, including SRAM and flash memory, Etching, DRAM.

Field: SRAM and flash memory

From planar cells to vertical strings

Planar NAND flash ran into limits around the 15 nm generation: each cell held only a handful of stored electrons, and coupling between neighboring cells grew severe. 3D NAND, which entered mass production around 2013 with 24 layers, turns the cell string vertical instead. A thick stack of alternating insulating and word-line layers is deposited, and cylindrical memory holes about 100 nm wide are etched through it, several micrometers deep. The channel runs up the hole, each layer forms the gate of one cell around it, and capacity grows by adding layers rather than by shrinking cells. Products now exceed 200 layers.

Charge-trap cells and word-line replacement

Most 3D NAND stores charge in a charge-trap layer of silicon nitride rather than a conductive floating gate. A silicon oxide, nitride, oxide (ONO) stack is deposited conformally on the hole wall, followed by a thin polysilicon channel. Charge stays localized in nitride traps, so a single defect cannot drain the whole cell and interference with neighbors is low. In stacks built from alternating oxide and nitride, the sacrificial nitride is later removed through slits with hot phosphoric acid, and the cavities are filled with tungsten by CVD from WF6 to form the word lines.

Process challenges at high layer counts

Memory holes have aspect ratios of 50 to 100, so etching them is the defining challenge. Amorphous-carbon hard masks several micrometers thick are needed, and holes tend to bow, twist or taper; cryogenic etching is being adopted to keep them straight and fast. Very tall stacks are split into two or more decks whose holes are aligned and joined, known as string stacking. Contacting hundreds of word lines requires a staircase at the array edge, formed by repeated etch and resist trim. Measuring hole shapes without cross-sectioning has prompted X-ray scattering methods such as CD-SAXS.

Putting the periphery under the array

Sense amplifiers, page buffers and other peripheral circuits originally sat beside the cell array. Building them first and stacking the array on top, in approaches called CMOS under Array or Periphery under Cell, saves roughly 20 to 30% of die area, though the periphery must then survive the thermal steps of array formation. Another approach fabricates the array and the peripheral CMOS on separate wafers and bonds them, letting each use its own optimized process and allowing parallel processing. Combined with three or four bits per cell (TLC or QLC), these steps drive cost per bit down.

  1. ADRAM cannot rewrite data
  2. BDRAM cell transistors can only be made of silicon
  3. CEach cell needs a capacitor to store charge, and today's capacitors are very tall vertical structures

Answer: C. Each cell needs a capacitor to store charge, and today's capacitors are very tall vertical structures

3D NAND stacks hundreds of word-line layers and etches vertical holes through them at once to form strings of cells. Each DRAM cell needs a capacitor to store charge, and planar DRAM secures capacitance by making the capacitor thin and deep, so it cannot simply be stacked. For 3D DRAM, researchers are studying structures that lay the transistor and capacitor on their sides in each layer, and capacitorless cells that hold charge on the parasitic capacitance of oxide-semiconductor transistors such as IGZO. Sideways cells take more area per layer, so stacking many layers at low cost is the key.

  2. BIt operates on three different supply voltages
  3. CIt stores 3 bits per cell

Answer: A. Memory cells are stacked vertically in many layers to increase capacity

As planar scaling neared its limit around 15 nm, 3D NAND, which stacks cells vertically, entered mass production around 2013 and became mainstream. Deep cylindrical channel holes are etched through a thick stack of alternating conductive and insulating layers, and cells are formed on the hole walls. Capacity grows without shrinking cells aggressively, and interference drops. Three bits per cell is TLC, commonly combined with 3D NAND.

FAQ

What is the difference between 2D NAND and 3D NAND?

2D (planar) NAND places cells side by side on the wafer surface and gains density by shrinking them. 3D NAND stacks cells vertically along channels in deep holes and gains density by adding layers, which avoids the interference and charge-count limits of tiny planar cells.

How many layers does 3D NAND have?

The first products had 24 layers, and mass-production devices now exceed 200 layers, with higher counts appearing. Very tall stacks are built in two or more decks joined together.

Why does 3D NAND use charge-trap cells?

A silicon nitride charge-trap layer can be deposited continuously on the walls of the memory holes, and its localized charge is less vulnerable to a single oxide defect and causes less interference. These traits suit vertically stacked strings better than conductive floating gates.
