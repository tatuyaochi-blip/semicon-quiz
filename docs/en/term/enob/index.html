What is ENOB (effective number of bits)?

The real performance of an ADC, including noise and distortion (SINAD), expressed as the bit count of an ideal ADC. A 12-bit ADC with an ENOB of around 10 bits is not unusual.

Field: A/D and D/A conversion

From SINAD to bits

An ideal N-bit ADC (analog-to-digital converter) still has quantization error, which behaves like noise with an rms value of LSB/√12. Comparing that with the rms value of a full-scale sine gives the ideal signal-to-noise ratio, 6.02N + 1.76 dB: about 74 dB at 12 bits and 98 dB at 16 bits. ENOB runs this formula backwards using the measured SINAD (signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio): ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76)/6.02. A 12-bit converter measuring 62 dB SINAD therefore has an ENOB of about 10 bits, and a 16-bit part with 74 dB behaves like an ideal 12-bit one.

What eats the bits

Real converters add several error sources on top of quantization. Thermal noise in the sampling switch, comparators and reference adds random noise. Nonlinearity in the transfer curve, from capacitor mismatch or amplifier gain error, creates harmonic distortion. Clock jitter turns timing uncertainty into amplitude error that grows with input frequency: the jitter-limited SNR is about −20·log10(2π·fin·σj), so 1 ps rms jitter caps SNR near 64 dB for a 100 MHz input. This is why ENOB usually falls as input frequency rises, and why the test frequency in a datasheet matters.

How ENOB is measured

ENOB is normally measured with a spectrally pure sine wave close to full scale, capturing a record of samples and computing an FFT (fast Fourier transform). Everything that is not the fundamental, apart from DC, counts as noise and distortion in SINAD. If the test signal is smaller than full scale, SINAD drops roughly decibel for decibel, so some datasheets quote ENOB normalized to full scale. Related specs separate the pieces: SNR excludes harmonics, while SFDR (spurious-free dynamic range) is the gap between the signal and the largest single spur.

ENOB vs. resolution

Resolution is just the number of output bits; ENOB is how many of them carry real information under specified conditions. The two can differ by several bits in fast converters. ENOB of a raw converter cannot meaningfully exceed its resolution, because quantization noise alone sets the ceiling, but oversampling and digital filtering can raise effective resolution within a narrower band: averaging four samples of uncorrelated noise improves SNR by about 6 dB, roughly one bit. ENOB also appears in common ADC figures of merit, which divide power by sampling rate times 2^ENOB.

Answer: A. About 12 bits

The SNR of an ideal N-bit ADC is determined only by quantization noise and is 6.02N + 1.76 dB. Using this in reverse gives ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76)/6.02 = (74 − 1.76)/6.02 ≈ 12.0 bits. It means that, despite its 16-bit output, noise and distortion make its actual performance equivalent to an ideal 12-bit ADC. SINAD varies with input signal frequency and amplitude, so check the value or graph at a frequency close to your operating conditions. Resolution and actual accuracy are different things.

FAQ

How do you calculate ENOB from SNR or SINAD?

ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76)/6.02, with SINAD in dB. Using SNR instead of SINAD gives a value that ignores distortion, so it overstates ENOB when harmonics are significant.

Why is ENOB lower than the ADC's resolution?

Thermal noise, distortion from nonlinearity, and clock jitter all add error beyond ideal quantization noise, so the converter performs like an ideal ADC with fewer bits.

Why does ENOB decrease at high input frequencies?

Clock jitter error grows in proportion to input frequency, and the sampling circuit's bandwidth and settling limits add distortion, so SINAD and ENOB fall as the input frequency rises.
