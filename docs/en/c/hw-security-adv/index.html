Hardware Security: Advanced Topics and Trends: key points

With the spread of cloud computing, AI and IoT devices, both what hardware security protects and how it is attacked have changed greatly. Confidential computing that protects data even from a cloud provider's administrators, hardware that stops pointer errors, cryptographic hardware for the era of quantum computers, and protecting a semiconductor supply chain involving many companies are major themes today. Building on the basics in "Hardware Security", this topic covers newer and deeper material.

It starts with confidential computing: the difference between VM-based and enclave-based TEEs and the trusted computing base (TCB), why integrity protection is needed in addition to encryption and a calculation of its storage cost, what a verifier checks in an attestation report, Arm CCA realms and confidential computing that includes GPUs. It continues with CHERI capabilities, a calculation of the probability that MTE tags match by chance, pointer authentication, and control-flow integrity through shadow stacks and protection of indirect branches. For attacks on DRAM it covers why Rowhammer becomes more likely with scaling, research that bypassed TRR, the limits of ECC, PRAC in DDR5 and a calculation relating refresh rate to activation counts; for transient execution attacks, MDS, retpoline and IBRS, and a calculation of the slowdown caused by mitigations. The second half covers speeding up polynomial multiplication with the number-theoretic transform, ML-KEM key sizes, implementation precautions for decapsulation, hardware designed so that cryptography can be replaced, OpenTitan and Caliptra, DICE, A/B updates with anti-rollback, HBOMs, trust between chiplets, split manufacturing, IC camouflaging and logic locking, and protecting keys against reverse engineering. It ends with FIPS 140-3 levels, Common Criteria EALs, composition in PSA Certified and SESIP, authenticated debug, lifecycle states and RMA, protecting scan chains, and fault injection by voltage glitches, lasers and electromagnetic pulses, including calculations of success probabilities and of the effect of countermeasures. The aim is to understand principles and countermeasures from the viewpoint of designers and defenders, not attack procedures. The values in the calculation questions, including those of the wrong choices, have been checked by computing them in Python. Product names, standard versions and years are given as of the time stated in each question. Side-channel attacks, Spectre, Meltdown and KPTI, power analysis, the basics of fault injection and Rowhammer, secure boot, TPMs, TEEs, TrustZone and enclaves, PUFs, true random number generators, hardware Trojans and disabling JTAG are covered in "Hardware Security"; the ML-KEM and ML-DSA standards and HSMs in "Applied Cryptography"; DRAM refresh and the name of the Rowhammer phenomenon in "DRAM"; chiplets and UCIe in "Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets)"; scan design in "Testing and inspection"; A/B firmware updates in "Embedded Systems & Firmware"; and ISO/SAE 21434 in "Automotive Semiconductors and Functional Safety".
