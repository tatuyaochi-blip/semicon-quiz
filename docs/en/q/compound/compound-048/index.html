Compound semiconductors

Why does Mg-doped GaN grown by MOCVD remain highly resistive rather than p-type right after growth, and what is the countermeasure?

  1. AMg evaporates away, so more Mg is added
  2. BHydrogen bonds with Mg and passivates the acceptors, so they are activated by annealing in nitrogen or electron-beam irradiation
  3. CGaN is an insulator, so p-type doping is impossible
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Hydrogen bonds with Mg and passivates the acceptors, so they are activated by annealing in nitrogen or electron-beam irradiation

Hydrogen from MOCVD precursors such as ammonia bonds with Mg to form Mg-H complexes that keep it from acting as an acceptor. In 1989 Hiroshi Amano and colleagues achieved p-type GaN by electron-beam irradiation, and in 1992 Shuji Nakamura and colleagues found that annealing at about 700 °C in nitrogen drives out the hydrogen—a method suited to mass production. Identifying hydrogen as the culprit rapidly enabled practical blue LEDs and lasers.

  • A. Mg does not disappear; it is passivated by bonding with hydrogen.
  • B. Correct. Driving out hydrogen activates the Mg.
  • C. GaN can be made p-type with suitable treatment.
