Fundamentals

Semiconductor physicsPractice questions →

Acceptor
An impurity that accepts electrons from a semiconductor and creates holes. For silicon, group III elements such as boron (B) act as acceptors and make p-type semiconductors.
Auger recombination
Recombination in which the released energy is given to another carrier instead of being emitted as light. It becomes significant at high carrier densities and affects lifetime in heavily doped layers and efficiency loss in LEDs.
Band gap
The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band. About 1.12 eV for silicon at room temperature. A larger band gap means better tolerance to high temperatures and voltages.
Degenerate semiconductor
A semiconductor doped so heavily that the Fermi level lies inside the conduction or valence band. It behaves almost like a metal and is used in tunnel diodes and low-resistance contacts.
Diffusion length
The average distance minority carriers diffuse before recombining, given by L = √(Dτ) from the diffusion coefficient D and lifetime τ. It strongly affects the efficiency of solar cells and photodetectors.
Donor
An impurity that supplies electrons to a semiconductor. For silicon, group V elements such as phosphorus (P) and arsenic (As) act as donors and make n-type semiconductors.
Effective mass
An apparent mass used to treat electrons and holes in a crystal as if they were free particles responding to external forces. It is set by the curvature of the band; a smaller effective mass tends to give higher mobility.
Einstein relation
The relation D = μkT/q that links the diffusion coefficient D and mobility μ of carriers. It shows that materials with higher mobility also allow faster diffusion.
Electron affinity
The energy difference between the vacuum level and the bottom of the conduction band of a semiconductor. It is used to estimate band offsets at heterojunctions and barrier heights at metal contacts.
Fermi level
The energy at which the probability of an electron occupying a state is exactly one half. It lies closer to the conduction band in n-type material and closer to the valence band in p-type.
Indirect band gap
A band structure in which the bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band occur at different momenta. Transitions need a phonon, so light emission is inefficient. Silicon and germanium are typical examples.
Intrinsic carrier density
The density of thermally generated electrons and holes in an undoped semiconductor, written ni. For silicon it is about 1×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at room temperature and rises steeply with temperature. In equilibrium, n × p = ni².
Mobility
How easily carriers move when an electric field is applied. In silicon, electron mobility is higher than hole mobility.
Quasi-Fermi level
Separate Fermi levels for electrons and holes, used to describe a semiconductor driven out of equilibrium by light or current injection. The larger the split between them, the more excess carriers there are.
SRH recombination (Shockley-Read-Hall recombination)
Recombination of electrons and holes through deep levels (traps) in the band gap. It mainly determines minority-carrier lifetime in silicon and is accelerated by metal contamination and crystal defects.
Work function
The energy difference between the vacuum level and the Fermi level: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from a material. It affects the threshold voltage of metal gates and the height of Schottky barriers.

PN junctions and diodesPractice questions →

Avalanche breakdown
A phenomenon in which carriers accelerated by a strong reverse field strike atoms and create new carriers, which multiply in a chain reaction so that a large current flows.
Built-in potential
The potential difference across the depletion layer of a PN junction even with no external voltage applied. About 0.6 to 0.8 V in silicon, it forms the barrier that stops further carrier diffusion.
Conductivity modulation
A large drop in the resistance of a lightly doped layer when many minority carriers are injected into it. PIN diodes and IGBTs use it to combine high breakdown voltage with low on-state voltage.
Depletion layer
A region near a PN junction boundary that contains almost no carriers. It widens under reverse bias.
Diffusion capacitance
Capacitance in a forward-biased PN junction caused by injected minority carriers stored in the neutral regions. It grows in proportion to forward current and limits high-speed operation.
Forward bias
Applying a positive voltage to the p side and a negative voltage to the n side. The barrier drops and current flows readily. The opposite is reverse bias, under which almost no current flows.
Ideality factor
The factor n in the diode equation I ∝ exp(qV/nkT). It is close to 1 when diffusion current dominates and close to 2 when recombination in the depletion layer dominates, so it indicates junction quality.
Junction capacitance (depletion capacitance)
Capacitance arising because the depletion layer of a PN junction acts like a parallel-plate capacitor. It decreases as reverse bias widens the depletion layer; varactor diodes use this property.
Ohmic contact
A metal-semiconductor contact with low resistance in both directions and a linear current-voltage relation. It is made by heavily doping the semiconductor surface so carriers can tunnel through the barrier.
Reverse recovery time
The time during which reverse current keeps flowing after a conducting diode is reverse-biased, until the stored minority carriers are removed. It causes switching loss and noise.
Schottky barrier diode
A diode that uses a metal-semiconductor junction (Schottky junction). It has a low forward voltage and switches fast because there is no minority-carrier storage.
Tunnel diode (Esaki diode)
A diode with very heavily doped p and n sides that shows negative resistance in forward bias due to tunneling. It was discovered by Leo Esaki in 1957.
Zener diode
A diode that provides a constant voltage by using the nearly fixed reverse breakdown voltage.

Bipolar transistorsPractice questions →

Common-emitter circuit
An amplifier circuit in which the emitter is the common terminal for input and output. It provides both current gain and voltage gain and is the most widely used configuration.
Crossover distortion
Distortion in a class-B push-pull amplifier near 0 V, where neither transistor conducts during the handover between positive and negative half-cycles. Class-AB biasing with a small idle current reduces it.
Current gain (hFE)
The ratio of collector current to base current (IC/IB) in a bipolar transistor.
Current mirror
A circuit that uses two matched transistors to copy the current flowing in one into the other (or at a fixed ratio). It is widely used for bias current sources and active loads in analog ICs.
Cutoff region
The state of a bipolar transistor in which both junctions are reverse-biased and almost no collector current flows. It corresponds to a switch in the off state.
Darlington connection
A connection that feeds the emitter current of one transistor into the base of a second. The overall current gain is roughly the product of the two, so a small base current can control a large current.
Early effect
A phenomenon in which raising the collector voltage widens the base-collector depletion layer, narrowing the effective base width and slightly increasing collector current.
Emitter follower
A common-collector amplifier. Its voltage gain is about 1, but its high input impedance and low output impedance make it a buffer that drives the next stage without loading the signal.
Forward-active region
The state with the base-emitter junction forward-biased and the base-collector junction reverse-biased. A collector current proportional to the base current flows, and it is used for amplification.
HBT (heterojunction bipolar transistor)
A bipolar transistor using materials with different band gaps for the emitter and base. Types include SiGe and GaAs-based devices, used in high-frequency amplifiers.
Miller effect
The effect by which a capacitance between the input and output of an inverting amplifier appears (1 + gain) times larger from the input. It degrades the high-frequency response of common-emitter and common-source stages.
Saturation region
The state of a bipolar transistor in which both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions are forward-biased. The collector-emitter voltage falls to about 0.1-0.3 V, corresponding to a switch in the on state.
Transition frequency
The frequency at which the common-emitter current gain falls to 1, written fT. It is a key indicator of a transistor's high-frequency performance.

MOSFETPractice questions →

Body effect
An increase in threshold voltage when reverse bias is applied between source and substrate (body), because the depletion charge grows. Also called the substrate bias effect; it is sometimes used to tune the threshold.
Channel-length modulation
In a saturated MOSFET, raising the drain voltage moves the pinch-off point toward the source, shortening the effective channel and slightly increasing drain current. It makes the output resistance finite.
CMOS
A circuit style that combines nMOS and pMOS transistors in a complementary way. Almost no current flows through it in steady state, so power consumption is low.
DIBL
Short for drain-induced barrier lowering. In short-channel MOSFETs, a higher drain voltage lowers the potential barrier at the source, reducing the threshold voltage and increasing off-state leakage.
Flat-band voltage
The gate voltage at which the bands at the semiconductor surface of a MOS structure are flat. It is set by the work-function difference between gate and substrate and by charges in the oxide, and is one component of the threshold voltage.
GIDL (gate-induced drain leakage)
Off-state leakage caused by band-to-band tunneling in the strong field where the gate overlaps the drain. It is a concern for data retention in DRAM, among others.
Inversion layer
A layer formed at the semiconductor surface by the gate voltage, where carriers of the type opposite to the substrate gather. It becomes the MOSFET's channel.
LDD structure
Short for lightly doped drain. The drain is made lightly doped only at its edge next to the gate, relaxing the electric field and suppressing hot-carrier degradation.
Punch-through
A condition in which the source and drain depletion regions merge and current flows that the gate cannot control. It occurs when the channel is too short or the substrate doping is too low.
Short-channel effect
Phenomena in which, as gate length shrinks, the drain's electric field lowers the threshold voltage and increases off-state leakage current.
Short-circuit current
Current that flows from supply to ground in a CMOS gate during the brief time while the input switches and both the pMOS and nMOS are on. Also called crowbar current, it adds to power consumption.
Subthreshold swing
The gate voltage needed to change drain current tenfold below threshold. Its theoretical lower limit at room temperature is about 60 mV per decade; the smaller it is, the sharper the on/off switching.
Threshold voltage
The gate voltage at which a channel (inversion layer) forms in a MOSFET and current starts to flow. Written as Vth.
Transconductance
The ratio of the change in drain current to the change in gate voltage, written gm. It expresses a transistor's amplifying ability; amplifier gain is roughly gm times the load resistance.
Transmission gate
An analog switch made of an nMOS and a pMOS connected in parallel. The nMOS passes 0 well and the pMOS passes 1 well, so both values pass without voltage loss.
Velocity saturation
Under strong electric fields, carrier drift velocity stops increasing in proportion to the field and levels off at about 1×10⁷ cm/s. In short-channel MOSFETs it makes drain current smaller than the square law predicts.

Logic circuitsPractice questions →

Clock skew
The difference in arrival time of the same clock signal at different flip-flops. Because it changes setup and hold margins, clock tree synthesis keeps it small.
Combinational circuit
A logic circuit whose output depends only on its current inputs. Examples include adders, multiplexers and decoders.
De Morgan's theorem
The rules for transforming logic expressions: NOT (A AND B) equals (NOT A) OR (NOT B), and NOT (A OR B) equals (NOT A) AND (NOT B).
Decoder
A circuit that activates exactly one of 2ⁿ outputs according to an n-bit input. It is used to select the row addressed in a memory. The reverse function is an encoder.
Flip-flop
The basic element of sequential circuits that stores one bit. A D flip-flop captures its input at a clock edge.
Full adder
A circuit that adds two bits plus a carry-in from the lower digit and outputs a sum and a carry-out. A version without carry-in is a half adder. Chaining full adders makes a multi-bit adder.
Glitch
An unwanted momentary pulse on an output, caused for example by differing delays along signal paths. In combinational logic it is also called a hazard; in DACs it appears when the code changes.
Gray code
A code in which successive values differ in only one bit. It prevents read errors in rotary encoders and when passing counter values between different clock domains.
Hold time
The minimum time after the active clock edge during which a flip-flop's data input must stay unchanged. Violations are not fixed by lowering the clock frequency, so delay is added to the path.
Karnaugh map
A diagram that arranges a truth table so that adjacent cells differ in one bit. Grouping neighboring 1s lets you simplify a logic expression visually.
Metastability
A state in which a flip-flop's output stays between 0 and 1 for a while when setup or hold time is violated. Asynchronous signals are passed through two flip-flops (a synchronizer) to guard against it.
Multiplexer
A circuit that selects one of several inputs according to select signals and passes it to the output. The reverse, routing one input to one of several outputs, is a demultiplexer.
Sequential circuit
A logic circuit that stores state in flip-flops and the like, so its output depends on both inputs and past state. Examples include counters and registers.
Setup time
The minimum time before the active clock edge during which a flip-flop's data input must be stable. Paths that cannot meet it limit the maximum operating frequency.
Shift register
A chain of flip-flops that moves data one bit to the next stage on each clock. It is used for serial-parallel conversion and as the scan chain in scan testing.
Tri-state buffer
A buffer that can output 0, 1 or a disconnected high-impedance state. It prevents output conflicts when several circuits share one bus.
Truth table
A table listing the output for every combination of inputs. It is used to describe the behavior of logic circuits.
Two's complement
The usual binary representation of negative integers. Inverting all bits and adding 1 negates a number. n bits cover −2ⁿ⁻¹ to 2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1, and the same adder can be used for subtraction.

Analog and electronic circuitsPractice questions →

1/f noise (flicker noise)
Noise that grows larger at lower frequencies. In MOSFETs it comes from traps at the gate-oxide interface capturing and releasing carriers, and it matters in low-frequency analog circuits.
Bandgap voltage reference
A circuit that adds a diode voltage that falls with temperature to a voltage that rises with temperature, producing a reference of about 1.2 V that barely depends on temperature. It is widely used in ADCs and power ICs.
CMRR (common-mode rejection ratio)
The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain in a differential amplifier. The larger it is, the better the circuit rejects noise that appears equally on both inputs.
Comparator
A circuit that compares two input voltages and outputs the result as a binary high or low. It is also a basic building block of ADCs.
Cutoff frequency
The frequency at which the output of a filter or amplifier falls 3 dB (to about 0.707 times) below the passband. For an RC circuit it is 1/(2πRC).
Differential amplifier
A circuit that amplifies the difference between two inputs while rejecting components common to both, such as noise. It is used as the input stage of op-amps.
Gain-bandwidth product (GBW)
The product of an op-amp's gain and bandwidth, which stays roughly constant. With a GBW of 1 MHz, for example, a gain-of-10 circuit has a bandwidth of about 100 kHz.
Instrumentation amplifier
A differential amplifier, typically built from three op-amps, with high input impedance and high CMRR. It amplifies small differential signals from sensors with high accuracy.
LDO
Short for low-dropout regulator: a linear regulator that works with only a small difference between input and output voltage. It is low-noise, but the voltage difference times the current is lost as heat.
Negative feedback
Returning part of the output to the input with opposite polarity. Gain drops, but it becomes stable, and distortion and bandwidth improve.
Operational amplifier (op-amp)
An amplifier with differential inputs and very high gain. Used with negative feedback in amplifiers, filters and more.
Phase margin
How far the phase lag is from 180° at the frequency where the feedback loop gain is 1 (0 dB). A small margin causes ringing, and zero or negative margin causes oscillation; 45-60° or more is a common target.
PLL (phase-locked loop)
A circuit that detects the phase difference from a reference clock and controls an oscillator to produce a clock locked to the reference, for example at an integer multiple of its frequency. It is used to generate CPU clocks and synthesize radio frequencies.
PSRR (power supply rejection ratio)
A measure of how little supply-voltage variation reaches a circuit's output. The larger it is, the more immune the circuit is to supply noise.
Schmitt trigger
A circuit whose switching threshold differs for rising and falling inputs (hysteresis). Its output does not chatter even with slowly changing or noisy signals.
Slew rate
The maximum rate at which an op-amp's output voltage can change, expressed in V/µs. If it is insufficient for large, high-frequency signals, the waveform distorts.
Thermal noise (Johnson noise)
Noise caused by the thermal motion of electrons in a resistor. Its mean-square voltage is 4kTRΔf, proportional to temperature, resistance and bandwidth.
Time constant
A quantity describing how fast a circuit such as an RC network responds to a step; for an RC circuit τ = RC. The response reaches about 63% of its final value after τ and has nearly settled after about 5τ.
Transimpedance amplifier
An amplifier that converts an input current to a voltage. The current enters the op-amp's inverting input and a feedback resistor turns it into a voltage. It is used to read photodiode signals.
Virtual short
The property of an ideal op-amp with negative feedback that its two input voltages become nearly equal. Although no current flows between them, they can be treated as shorted, which makes gain calculations simple.
Voltage follower
A unity-gain op-amp circuit with the output tied directly to the inverting input. With high input impedance and low output impedance, it is used as a buffer.

A/D and D/A conversionPractice questions →

Aliasing
A phenomenon in which components above half the sampling frequency falsely appear as lower-frequency components. It is prevented with a low-pass filter before A/D conversion.
Delta-sigma ADC
An ADC that oversamples at a high rate, pushes quantization noise to high frequencies and then filters it out. Used in high-resolution applications such as audio and measurement.
DNL (differential nonlinearity)
The error in each code step width of an ADC relative to the ideal 1 LSB. When it reaches −1 LSB, a missing code occurs.
ENOB (effective number of bits)
The real performance of an ADC, including noise and distortion (SINAD), expressed as the bit count of an ideal ADC. A 12-bit ADC with an ENOB of around 10 bits is not unusual.
Flash ADC
The fastest type of ADC, which compares the input against many comparators at once. N bits need 2ᴺ − 1 comparators, so the circuit grows rapidly with resolution.
INL (integral nonlinearity)
The maximum deviation of an ADC's or DAC's actual transfer characteristic from the ideal straight line. It corresponds to the accumulated DNL.
Jitter
Small fluctuations in the timing of clock or signal edges from their ideal positions. When sampling high-frequency signals, even slight jitter causes large voltage errors.
Missing code
A defect in which an ADC never outputs a particular code no matter how the input varies. It occurs when DNL reaches −1 LSB.
Noise shaping
A technique that uses a feedback loop to push quantization noise toward high frequencies, lowering noise in the signal band. Delta-sigma ADCs combine it with oversampling to achieve high resolution.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sampling frequency. Components above it are folded back to lower frequencies when sampled (aliasing).
Oversampling
Sampling at a rate well above what the signal requires (twice its bandwidth). It spreads quantization noise over a wider band and reduces noise in the signal band; it is the basis of delta-sigma ADCs.
Pipelined ADC
An ADC that splits conversion into stages, each resolving a few bits and passing the residue to the next, like an assembly line. It combines high speed with medium to high resolution.
Quantization error
The error that arises when rounding a continuous analog value to a digital value with a finite number of bits. At most half of one LSB.
R-2R ladder DAC
A DAC built from a ladder of only two resistor values, R and 2R. It needs few resistor types, and the resistor ratio does not grow as bits are added.
Sample-and-hold circuit
A circuit that captures (samples) the input voltage at an instant and holds it constant until AD conversion finishes. It is built from a switch and a capacitor.
Sampling theorem
The theorem stating that a signal can be reconstructed if it is sampled at a frequency higher than twice its highest frequency component. Also called the Nyquist-Shannon theorem.
Successive-approximation ADC
An ADC that uses an internal DAC and comparator to determine one bit at a time, starting from the most significant. It balances speed, accuracy and power well.

Devices

DRAMPractice questions →

1T1C cell
The DRAM memory cell that stores one bit with one transistor and one capacitor. The presence or absence of charge on the capacitor represents 0 or 1.
Bank
One of several independently operating sections inside a DRAM. Overlapping activation and access across different banks raises effective performance.
Bit line
The wire used to read and write data in memory cells. In DRAM, the small change in bit-line potential caused by a cell's charge is amplified by a sense amplifier.
CAS latency
The number of clock cycles in DRAM from a read command (specifying the column address) until data appears at the output, abbreviated CL. Smaller values mean a faster response.
DDR SDRAM
Synchronous DRAM that transfers data on both the rising and falling clock edges. Successive generations such as DDR4 and DDR5 have raised the data rate. It is the standard main memory of PCs and servers.
DIMM
A memory module that mounts several DRAM chips on a small board so it can be plugged into a slot in a PC or server.
ECC (error-correcting code)
A scheme that adds redundant bits to data so that bit errors can be detected and corrected on readout. It is used in server memory, flash memory and inside DDR5 chips, among others.
LPDDR
Short for low-power DDR: a DRAM standard for smartphones and laptops with lower supply voltages and enhanced power-saving features.
Memory wall
The problem that memory bandwidth and latency improve more slowly than processor performance, limiting overall performance. HBM, larger caches and advanced packaging are used to address it.
Precharge
Charging the bit lines to a set potential (half the supply voltage in DRAM, for example) before a read. In DRAM it also means closing the open row to prepare for activating the next one.
Redundancy repair
Replacing rows or columns that contain defective memory cells with spare rows or columns prepared in advance. The defect locations are found by testing and the switch is made with fuses or similar. It greatly improves yield.
Refresh
The periodic reading and rewriting of DRAM data before the charge in its capacitors is lost through leakage.
Rowhammer
A DRAM phenomenon in which repeatedly activating the same row in a short time drains charge from cells in neighboring rows and flips their data. Because it can be exploited in attacks, countermeasures are built into DRAMs and memory controllers.
Sense amplifier
A circuit that amplifies the tiny voltage difference read from a memory cell and decides whether it is 0 or 1.
Word line
The wire connected to the gates of memory-cell transistors that selects the row to read or write.

SRAM and flash memoryPractice questions →

3D NAND
NAND flash in which memory cells are stacked vertically in many layers to increase capacity. It was introduced to get past the limits of planar scaling, and layer counts now exceed 200.
6T SRAM cell
The SRAM memory cell made of two cross-coupled inverters (four transistors) and two access transistors.
Charge-trap cell
A flash memory cell that stores electrons in traps within an insulating film such as silicon nitride instead of in a floating gate. It is mainly used in 3D NAND.
Floating gate
An electrode in a flash memory cell that is completely surrounded by insulator. Storing electrons on it shifts the transistor's threshold voltage, so data is kept even without power.
Fowler-Nordheim tunneling
Current that flows when a strong electric field lets electrons tunnel through the triangular barrier of a thin insulator. It is used to program and erase NAND flash.
Garbage collection
In an SSD, moving the still-valid data out of blocks that contain invalid data and then erasing those blocks to create free space.
NAND flash memory
Non-volatile memory in which memory cells are connected in series. It is dense and easy to scale to large capacities, and is used in SSDs and memory cards.
Nonvolatile memory
Memory that retains stored data when power is removed, such as flash memory and MRAM. DRAM and SRAM are volatile.
NOR flash memory
Flash memory with cells connected in parallel. Random reads are fast, and it is used to store programs for microcontrollers and the like.
NVMe
An interface standard for fast access to SSDs connected over PCI Express. It processes many commands in parallel and has lower latency than SATA, which was designed for HDDs.
Read disturb
In NAND flash, the gradual shift in threshold voltage of unread cells caused by the weak voltage applied to them when the same block is read many times.
SLC (single-level cell)
A flash format that stores one bit per cell. With only two threshold-voltage states it is faster and more durable than multi-level formats.
TLC (triple-level cell)
A flash format that stores three bits per cell, distinguishing eight threshold-voltage states. It lowers cost per bit but reduces endurance. Four bits per cell is QLC.
TRIM
A command by which the operating system tells an SSD which areas belong to deleted files. Knowing which data is no longer needed makes garbage collection more efficient.
Wear leveling
A technique that spreads writes so rewrites do not concentrate on particular flash blocks, extending the memory's life.
Write amplification
The amount of data actually written to flash exceeding what the host wrote, because of garbage collection and similar operations. The larger it is, the shorter the SSD's life.

Emerging memoriesPractice questions →

3D XPoint
Cross-point non-volatile memory jointly developed by Intel and Micron. It stacked phase-change material with OTS selectors and was sold as Optane, but the business was wound down in 2022.
Chalcogenide
A compound containing group 16 elements such as sulfur, selenium or tellurium. It is used as the recording material of phase-change memory (such as GST) and in selectors (OTS) that switch on abruptly above a threshold voltage.
Cross-point memory
Memory with a storage element at each intersection of row and column lines. It is very dense, but a selector is placed in series with each element to stop current sneaking through unselected cells.
Electroforming
An initialization step for ReRAM in which a relatively high voltage is first applied after fabrication to create the conductive filament that later switches resistance.
FeFET (ferroelectric FET)
A transistor with a ferroelectric gate insulator. Its threshold voltage depends on the polarization direction, so a single transistor can store data non-volatilely and be read non-destructively.
FeRAM (ferroelectric RAM)
Nonvolatile memory that stores information in the polarization direction of a ferroelectric. Writes are fast and low-power, and it is used in IC cards and the like.
In-memory computing
Performing computations such as multiply-accumulate inside the memory array to cut data movement between memory and processor. Research is under way on using resistive elements and the like for AI inference.
Magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ)
A structure with a thin insulating film between two magnetic layers. Resistance is low when the layers' magnetizations are parallel and high when antiparallel. It is the storage element of MRAM.
MRAM
Non-volatile memory that stores data as the difference in resistance caused by the magnetization direction of a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ).
PCM (phase-change memory)
Nonvolatile memory that stores information by the difference between the crystalline (low-resistance) and amorphous (high-resistance) states of a chalcogenide material.
ReRAM (resistive RAM)
Nonvolatile memory that applies voltage to form and break conductive paths in an oxide or similar material, storing information as differences in resistance.
SOT-MRAM
MRAM that flips magnetization by spin-orbit torque from a current in a heavy-metal layer. The write and read current paths are separate, giving fast, durable writes.
Storage-class memory
Non-volatile memory intended to bridge the gap in speed, capacity and cost between DRAM and NAND flash. Products based on 3D XPoint were the best-known example.
STT-MRAM
MRAM that writes by flipping the magnetization of the MTJ's free layer using the spin-transfer torque of a spin-polarized current. It is in volume production as embedded non-volatile memory.
TMR ratio
In an MTJ, the difference between the resistances in the parallel and antiparallel states divided by the parallel-state resistance. The larger it is, the easier it is to tell 0 from 1; MgO barriers give large values.

CPUs and architecturePractice questions →

Amdahl's law
The law that when only part of a program is parallelized, overall speedup is limited by the part that cannot be. For example, parallelizing 90% gives at most a 10x speedup.
Branch prediction
A technique that predicts the outcome of conditional branches before they execute, so later instructions can proceed without stalling the pipeline. A misprediction forces the work to be redone.
Cache coherency
In a multicore CPU, the mechanism that keeps copies of the same address in different cores' caches consistent, for example by invalidating other copies when one core writes.
Cache memory
Fast, small memory placed close to the CPU. It holds frequently used data to bridge the speed gap with main memory. Mostly built from SRAM.
DMA (direct memory access)
A mechanism that transfers data directly between peripherals and memory without going through the CPU, so the CPU can do other work during large transfers.
DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling)
Changing operating frequency and supply voltage dynamically according to load. Since dynamic power scales with the square of voltage, lowering voltage during light loads saves a lot of power.
GPU
Short for graphics processing unit: a processor with many simple execution units that processes large amounts of data in parallel. Besides graphics, it is widely used for AI training and inference.
Instruction set architecture (ISA)
The processor specification as seen by software, including the instructions and registers it supports. Examples include x86, Arm and RISC-V.
Interrupt
A CPU mechanism that temporarily suspends the current task to handle a request, for example from an external device, and then returns to the original task.
IPC
Short for instructions per cycle: the number of instructions executed per clock. It expresses design efficiency; performance is roughly IPC times clock frequency. Its reciprocal is CPI.
Microcontroller
A small computer that integrates a CPU, memory and peripherals such as timers and ADCs on one chip. It is embedded in appliances, cars and other equipment to control them. Also called an MCU.
NPU (neural processing unit)
A dedicated circuit for running neural-network inference quickly and at low power. It is built into the SoCs of smartphones and PCs.
Out-of-order execution
Executing instructions as soon as their dependencies allow, in a different order from the program, to improve performance. Results are committed in program order through a reorder buffer.
Pipelining
A method that splits instruction processing into stages such as fetch, decode and execute, and processes several instructions at once, each offset by a stage.
SIMD
Short for single instruction, multiple data: one instruction performs the same operation on several data elements at once. It speeds up image processing and AI calculations.
SMT (simultaneous multithreading)
A technique in which one CPU core processes instructions from several threads at once, making better use of its execution units. Intel calls it Hyper-Threading.
Superscalar
A processor design that issues and executes several instructions per clock cycle. It has multiple execution units and processes independent instructions in parallel.
Systolic array
An arrangement of many multiply-accumulate units in a grid that computes matrix products by passing data to neighboring units step by step. It is used in AI accelerators.
TDP (thermal design power)
A guide to the heat a CPU or similar chip generates under a typical heavy load. Cooling systems are designed around it.
TLB (translation lookaside buffer)
A small cache that holds recent virtual-to-physical address translations. It avoids walking the page tables in memory on every access, speeding up address translation.
Virtual memory
A system in which the OS and hardware map the addresses a program uses (virtual addresses) to physical memory addresses. Each program gets a large, independent address space.
Von Neumann architecture
A computer organization that keeps programs and data in the same main memory and fetches and executes instructions in sequence.

Power semiconductorsPractice questions →

Body diode
A parasitic diode formed between source and drain by the structure of a power MOSFET. It can serve as the freewheeling diode in an inverter, but its reverse recovery needs attention.
DC-DC converter
A circuit that converts one DC voltage into another. Using switches, inductors and capacitors, buck types lower the voltage and boost types raise it.
Dead time
A short interval during which both the upper and lower switches of an inverter are off while switching between them. It prevents both from being on at once and shorting the supply.
Gate driver
A circuit that receives control signals and drives a power device's gate with the right voltage and enough current. Many include isolation or level shifting to drive high-side switches.
IGBT
Insulated-gate bipolar transistor. Driven by a MOS gate, it achieves a low on-state voltage even at high voltage ratings through conductivity modulation.
Normally-off
The property of passing no current when the gate voltage is 0 V. It is required of power devices for safety, since current stops even if the drive circuit fails.
On-resistance
The resistance of the current path when a power device is on. The smaller it is, the lower the conduction loss.
Power MOSFET
A MOSFET built to handle large currents and voltages. It switches quickly and is widely used in power supply circuits and the like.
Short-circuit withstand time
The time a power device can survive a short circuit, such as both inverter switches turning on at once, without being destroyed. Protection circuits must cut the current within this time.
SiC (silicon carbide)
A wide-band-gap semiconductor with a band gap of about 3.3 eV. Its high breakdown field makes it suitable for high-voltage, low-loss power devices.
Snubber circuit
A network such as a resistor and capacitor placed across a power device to suppress the voltage spikes (surges) and ringing that occur during switching.
Soft switching
Turning a switch on or off when the voltage across it or current through it is nearly zero, as in zero-voltage switching (ZVS). It reduces switching loss and noise.
Super-junction MOSFET
A power MOSFET whose drift layer has alternating p-type and n-type pillars. It allows higher drift-layer doping without losing breakdown voltage, achieving on-resistance below the conventional silicon limit.
Switching loss
Loss that occurs while a power device switches between on and off, when voltage and current are present at the same time. It grows with switching frequency.
Tail current
Current that keeps flowing like a tail as an IGBT turns off, because stored minority carriers prevent it from dropping to zero immediately. It causes turn-off loss.

Image sensors and optical devicesPractice questions →

Backside illumination (BSI)
An image sensor structure in which light enters from the back of the substrate, where there is no wiring. Light is not blocked by wiring, so sensitivity stays high even with small pixels.
Bayer pattern
A color filter arrangement of red, green, green and blue over each 2×2 block of pixels, with two greens because the eye is most sensitive to green. Missing colors at each pixel are interpolated from neighbors (demosaicing).
CCD image sensor
An image sensor that shifts pixel charges along like a bucket brigade and converts them to voltage at an output stage. It offered low noise and high image quality but used more power, and CMOS sensors have replaced it in most uses.
CMOS image sensor
An image sensor made with a CMOS process that has an amplifier in each pixel. It is low-power, can integrate peripheral circuits, and dominates smartphone cameras and more.
Correlated double sampling (CDS)
Taking the difference between a pixel's voltage just after reset and its voltage when the signal is read. It removes reset noise and per-pixel offsets.
Dark current
Current that flows in a photodiode even without light, caused for example by thermal generation of carriers. It rises with temperature and appears as noise in dark images.
Fixed-pattern noise
Noise that appears in the same place every time, caused by variations between pixels or readout circuits. It is removed by correlated double sampling or calibration.
Full-well capacity
The maximum number of electrons one image-sensor pixel can hold. The larger it is, the brighter the scene can be before highlights clip, widening dynamic range.
Global shutter
An electronic shutter method that exposes all pixels at the same time. Unlike a rolling shutter, which reads row by row, it does not distort images of moving objects.
Microlens
A tiny lens placed over each pixel of an image sensor. It gathers light and directs it efficiently onto the photodiode, raising sensitivity.
Photodiode
A diode that generates charge (current) when it receives light. Used as the light-receiving element in image sensor pixels.
Pinned photodiode
A photodiode with a heavily doped p+ layer at the surface that keeps the collection region away from the interface. It suppresses dark current from interface states and allows complete charge transfer; it is standard in CMOS image sensors.
Quantum efficiency
The fraction of incident photons converted into signal electrons. The higher it is, the better the sensitivity.
Rolling shutter
A readout method that exposes and reads pixel rows one after another. It is simple, but fast-moving subjects appear skewed. Exposing all pixels at once is a global shutter.
Shot noise
Noise from statistical fluctuation in the number of photons or electrons. Its size is proportional to √N for a signal of N electrons, so it is relatively larger when light is scarce.
SPAD (single-photon avalanche diode)
A photodetector biased above breakdown so that even a single photon triggers a large avalanche pulse. It is used for ranging, such as in LiDAR.
Stacked CMOS image sensor
An image sensor whose pixel array and signal-processing circuitry are made on separate chips and bonded together. Each can use the most suitable process, making the chip smaller and processing faster.
ToF (time of flight)
Measuring distance from the time light takes to travel to an object and back. ToF sensors are used in face recognition, robotics and automotive LiDAR.

Compound semiconductorsPractice questions →

2DEG (two-dimensional electron gas)
A dense sheet of electrons confined at a heterojunction interface such as AlGaN/GaN. With little impurity scattering and high mobility, it serves as the channel of a HEMT.
Alloy semiconductor
A semiconductor crystal that mixes several elements, such as AlGaAs or InGaN. Changing the composition tunes the band gap and lattice constant continuously.
Direct band gap
A band structure in which the bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band are at the same wave number. Electron-hole recombination emits light efficiently. Example: GaAs.
Efficiency droop
The drop in luminous efficiency of InGaN LEDs as current increases. Auger recombination is thought to be one cause.
Ga2O3 (gallium oxide)
An oxide semiconductor with a band gap of about 4.5-4.9 eV, even wider than SiC or GaN. Its crystals can be grown from the melt, and it is being researched as a next-generation power semiconductor material.
GaAs (gallium arsenide)
A III-V compound semiconductor of gallium and arsenic with a direct band gap of about 1.42 eV. Its high electron mobility makes it useful for high-frequency transistors and infrared LEDs and lasers.
GaN (gallium nitride)
A III-V compound semiconductor with a band gap of about 3.4 eV. Used in blue LEDs and in high-frequency and power devices.
HEMT (high-electron-mobility transistor)
A transistor that uses the high-mobility two-dimensional electron gas formed at a heterojunction interface as its channel. It is used in high-frequency and high-power applications.
III-V compound semiconductor
Semiconductors made of group III elements (Ga, In, Al, etc.) and group V elements (As, P, N, etc.), such as GaAs, InP and GaN.
InP (indium phosphide)
A compound semiconductor of indium and phosphorus. As a substrate for lattice-matched layers such as InGaAs, it is used for fiber-optic lasers and photodetectors and for high-speed transistors.
MMIC
Short for monolithic microwave integrated circuit: a high-frequency IC that integrates transistors together with passive components such as inductors and capacitors on a substrate such as GaAs.
Quantum well
A structure in which a thin layer, a few nanometers thick, with a small band gap is sandwiched between layers with a larger band gap. It confines carriers to promote recombination and is used as the active layer of LEDs and lasers.
VCSEL (vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser)
A semiconductor laser that emits light perpendicular to the substrate. It can be tested on the wafer and easily arranged in 2D arrays, so it is used in data links and as the light source for face recognition.
Vegard's law
The empirical rule that the lattice constant of an alloy varies almost linearly with composition. It is used to find the composition that lattice-matches a given substrate.
Wide-bandgap semiconductor
Semiconductors with a larger band gap than silicon, such as SiC and GaN. Their high breakdown field makes them strong for high-voltage, high-temperature operation.
Wurtzite structure
The hexagonal crystal structure of GaN, ZnO and others. It has polarization along certain crystal directions, which creates the 2DEG in GaN HEMTs.

Advanced transistors (FinFET and GAA)Practice questions →

CFET (complementary FET)
A structure that stacks nMOS and pMOS transistors vertically. It is under development because it could greatly shrink the area of CMOS circuits.
DTCO (design-technology co-optimization)
Optimizing cell area and performance by considering circuit design and process technology together, as scaling becomes harder.
EOT (equivalent oxide thickness)
The thickness of SiO2 that would give the same gate capacitance as a gate insulator such as a high-k film. A physically thick film can have a small EOT, giving high capacitance while limiting tunneling current.
FD-SOI (fully depleted SOI)
Building transistors in a very thin silicon layer on buried oxide so the channel is fully depleted. The channel can be lightly doped, and body bias can tune the threshold voltage easily.
FinFET
A 3D transistor whose channel stands up like a fin and is surrounded by the gate on three sides. It suppresses short-channel effects well.
GAA (gate-all-around)
A transistor structure in which the gate surrounds the channel on all sides. It controls current better than FinFET and is used in the generations after FinFET.
Gate-last (replacement metal gate)
Building the transistor first with a dummy gate, then removing it after high-temperature steps such as source/drain activation and replacing it with a high-k dielectric and metal gate.
High-k / metal gate
The combination of a high-dielectric-constant gate insulator and a metal gate electrode. It keeps gate capacitance high while suppressing gate leakage.
Nanosheet
A GAA transistor structure that stacks several thin, plate-like silicon channels vertically, each surrounded by the gate. Current can be tuned by the sheet width.
Random dopant fluctuation (RDF)
Threshold-voltage variation in small transistors caused by statistical variation in the number and position of dopant atoms in the channel. FinFETs and FD-SOI, which allow lightly doped channels, reduce it.
SADP (self-aligned double patterning)
Forming spacers on the sidewalls of a core pattern, removing the core and using the spacers as the mask to make a pitch half of what lithography alone allows. Doing it twice is SAQP.

MEMS and sensorsPractice questions →

Accelerometer
A sensor that detects acceleration from the movement of a small spring-supported mass. It is used for detecting smartphone orientation, in airbags and elsewhere.
Anodic bonding
Joining silicon to sodium-containing glass by heating them together while applying a voltage. It is used, for example, to seal MEMS devices.
Bosch process (deep RIE)
A dry-etching technique that alternates short etch and sidewall-passivation steps to cut deep, vertical holes and trenches in silicon. It is used for MEMS and TSVs.
Capacitive sensing
A method that detects displacement, pressure and the like from changes in capacitance between movable and fixed electrodes. It is widely used in MEMS sensors.
Coriolis force
An apparent force acting perpendicular to the motion of an object moving in a rotating frame. Vibratory MEMS gyroscopes detect angular rate from the displacement it causes.
Gyroscope
A sensor that detects rotation rate (angular velocity). MEMS versions use the Coriolis force acting on a vibrating structure.
Hall-effect sensor
A sensor that detects magnetic fields using the Hall effect: a magnetic field applied to a current-carrying semiconductor produces a voltage perpendicular to both. It is used, for example, to sense motor rotation.
MEMS
Micro-electro-mechanical systems. Devices that integrate tiny mechanical structures and electronic circuits using semiconductor microfabrication.
Piezoresistive effect
The change in a material's electrical resistivity under mechanical stress. It is especially large in silicon and is used in semiconductor pressure sensors and similar devices.
Sacrificial layer etching
A typical MEMS fabrication method that etches away a temporary layer (the sacrificial layer) beneath a structure, freeing beams or membranes so they can move.
Stiction
The sticking of tiny moving MEMS parts to the substrate through surface tension or surface adhesion, so they no longer move. It often happens during drying after sacrificial-layer removal.

Front-end processes

Silicon wafersPractice questions →

Crystal orientation
The direction of crystal planes and axes, expressed with Miller indices such as (100). The (100) surface is standard for silicon wafers used for MOSFETs.
Czochralski (CZ) method
A method of making single-crystal silicon by dipping a seed crystal into molten silicon in a crucible and slowly pulling it up while rotating.
DZ (denuded zone)
A layer near the wafer surface with few defects such as oxygen precipitates, made by out-diffusing oxygen during heat treatment. Devices are built in this layer while precipitates deeper inside provide gettering.
FZ method (float zone)
A method that melts a zone of a polysilicon rod and moves that molten zone along to form a single crystal. With no crucible, oxygen content is low, suiting high-resistivity wafers.
Gettering
A technique that collects and traps metal impurities in a wafer at locations away from the surface where devices are built, using oxygen precipitates and the like.
Ingot
A cylindrical single-crystal block grown by the CZ method or similar, before it is cut into wafers. The ends are removed and the outside is ground to a uniform diameter before slicing.
MCZ method (magnetic-field-applied CZ)
A CZ variant that applies a magnetic field to the crucible to suppress melt convection. It makes the oxygen concentration in the crystal easier to control and is used for large-diameter crystals.
Monitor wafer
A wafer run through a process separately from products to check equipment or measure film thickness and particles. Also called a test wafer, it is often stripped, repolished and reused as a reclaimed wafer.
Necking
Growing a thin neck just after the seed at the start of CZ pulling. It lets dislocations created by thermal shock when the seed touches the melt escape, giving a dislocation-free crystal.
Notch
A small cut in a wafer's edge that indicates its crystal orientation. Used on 200 mm and larger wafers; smaller wafers used an orientation flat (a straight edge) instead.
Oxygen precipitates (BMD)
Tiny defects formed inside a CZ silicon wafer when its oxygen precipitates during heat treatment. Also called BMDs (bulk micro defects), they serve as gettering sites that capture metal impurities.
Segregation coefficient
The ratio of impurity concentration in the crystal to that in the melt during solidification. For most impurities it is below 1, so they concentrate in the melt and the later part of a CZ crystal has higher impurity levels.
Siemens process
A method that reduces trichlorosilane with hydrogen to deposit high-purity polycrystalline silicon on heated silicon rods. It is the main way to make raw silicon for semiconductors.
Slip
A defect in which crystal planes shift and dislocations form under stress from temperature differences or the wafer's own weight during high-temperature processing of large wafers. It can cause problems such as overlay errors.
SOI wafer
A wafer with a thin silicon layer on a buried oxide (BOX) layer over a silicon substrate. It is made, for example, by bonding a hydrogen-implanted wafer and splitting it. Its low parasitic capacitance suits FD-SOI devices and MEMS.
Thermal donor
Oxygen clusters that act as donors when CZ silicon is heated around 450°C. Because they change the wafer's resistivity, they are annihilated by a higher-temperature treatment.

Epitaxial growthPractice questions →

4H-SiC
The SiC polytype mainly used for power devices. It has a wide band gap of about 3.3 eV and relatively high electron mobility.
Autodoping
Unintended incorporation into a growing epitaxial layer of dopant from the substrate or elsewhere via the gas phase. It is a problem when growing lightly doped layers on heavily doped substrates.
Critical thickness
The maximum thickness to which a lattice-mismatched layer can be grown while staying strained without forming dislocations. Beyond it, misfit dislocations appear.
Epitaxial growth
A technique for growing a single-crystal thin film on a single-crystal substrate with the same crystal orientation as the substrate.
Epitaxial lateral overgrowth (ELO)
Growing crystal from mask openings and letting it spread sideways over the mask. Dislocations from below do not propagate into the overgrown regions, reducing dislocations in materials such as GaN.
Heteroepitaxy
Growing a single-crystal film of a material different from the substrate, such as SiGe on silicon or GaN on sapphire. Growth of the same material is homoepitaxy.
HVPE (hydride vapor phase epitaxy)
Vapor-phase growth using gallium chloride, formed by reacting HCl with gallium, together with ammonia. Its very high growth rate is used to grow thick GaN crystals for free-standing substrates.
Lattice mismatch
A difference in the atomic spacing (lattice constant) between a grown film and its substrate. A large mismatch causes strain and defects such as dislocations.
MBE (molecular beam epitaxy)
Epitaxial growth in ultra-high vacuum by supplying source materials to the substrate as molecular beams. It controls thickness and composition at the atomic-layer level and is used in compound-semiconductor research and quantum structures.
Misfit dislocation
A dislocation at the interface between crystals with different lattice constants that relieves strain. It forms when the grown layer exceeds the critical thickness.
MOCVD (metal-organic chemical vapor deposition)
A method that grows crystals using metal-organic compounds and source gases such as ammonia. It is widely used to grow compound semiconductors such as GaN and GaAs.
Selective epitaxial growth
A method that grows crystal only where silicon is exposed, not on areas covered by insulating film. It is used for SiGe in transistor sources and drains and elsewhere.
SiGe (silicon-germanium)
An alloy of silicon and germanium with a larger lattice constant than silicon. Grown epitaxially, it is used to strain channels, as the base of HBTs and as the sacrificial layer in GAA transistors.
Stacking fault
A planar defect in which the stacking order of atomic planes is disrupted. It is a concern in epitaxial layers, thermal oxidation (OSF) and SiC crystals.
Step-flow growth
A growth mode in which the crystal extends sideways from atomic steps on a slightly off-axis substrate. In SiC it is used so the layer inherits the substrate's polytype.
Strained silicon
Silicon whose band structure is modified by strain to raise carrier mobility. Tensile strain helps nMOS and compressive strain helps pMOS; it is applied with SiGe source/drain regions, nitride films and the like.
Superlattice
A periodic structure of alternating layers of different materials, each a few nanometers thick. It shows electronic properties absent in bulk crystals and is used in lasers, buffer layers and more.
Threading dislocation
A dislocation that runs up through the grown layer to the surface. GaN on sapphire or silicon has high densities (about 10⁸-10⁹ cm⁻²), which degrade device performance and reliability.

Oxidation and diffusionPractice questions →

Bird's beak
In LOCOS, the beak-shaped encroachment of oxide under the edge of the nitride mask. Because it wastes active area, it led to the switch to STI at finer geometries.
Deal-Grove model
A model of thermal oxidation in which the oxidant diffuses through the oxide and reacts with silicon at the Si/SiO2 interface. Thickness grows linearly with time for thin oxides and with the square root of time for thick ones.
Drive-in
In thermal diffusion, the high-temperature step after a fixed amount of dopant has been introduced at the surface (predeposition), which pushes the dopant deeper.
Dry oxidation
Thermal oxidation with dry oxygen. Growth is slow, but it produces dense, high-quality oxide, used for gate oxides and the like.
Forming gas anneal
Heat treatment in a mixture of hydrogen and nitrogen (forming gas). Hydrogen terminates dangling bonds at the Si/SiO2 interface, reducing interface states.
Interface states
Energy levels within the band gap at a semiconductor-insulator interface, created by broken bonds and similar defects. They trap and release carriers, causing threshold shifts, lower mobility and noise.
LOCOS
A method that isolates devices by thickly oxidizing only areas not covered by nitride. It forms a beak-like encroachment at the edges (the bird's beak) and has been replaced by STI.
Mobile ions
Ions such as sodium and potassium that can move within the gate oxide. They drift with field and temperature and shift the threshold voltage. Because they can come from sources such as human sweat, they are tightly controlled.
Oxidation-enhanced diffusion (OED)
Faster diffusion of boron and phosphorus during oxidation than without it, caused by silicon interstitials injected into the silicon by the oxidation.
Oxynitride (SiON)
Gate oxide with nitrogen incorporated. It blocks boron penetration from the gate electrode and has a slightly higher permittivity, so it was used as the gate insulator before high-k films.
RTP (rapid thermal processing)
Heating single wafers to high temperature for a few seconds, typically with lamps. It allows annealing and thin oxide growth while limiting unwanted dopant diffusion.
STI (shallow trench isolation)
Isolating devices by etching shallow trenches in silicon, filling them with insulator and planarizing by CMP. It has no bird's beak like LOCOS and is the standard isolation for fine-pitch ICs.
Thermal diffusion
A doping method that diffuses impurity atoms into a semiconductor at high temperature. They spread from regions of high concentration to low.
Thermal oxidation
A method of forming an SiO2 film on silicon by heating it in oxygen or water vapor. There are dry and wet oxidation.
Wet oxidation
Thermal oxidation in an atmosphere containing water vapor. It grows faster than dry oxidation and is used to make thick oxides.

Ion implantationPractice questions →

Annealing
Heat treatment. Used to repair crystal damage after ion implantation and to activate dopants, among other purposes.
Channeling
A phenomenon in which ions pass through gaps between rows of crystal atoms and penetrate deeper than expected. It is prevented by tilting the wafer during implantation and similar measures.
Faraday cup
A device that collects an ion beam and measures the resulting current. In ion implanters it is used to determine the implanted dose.
Halo implant (pocket implant)
Angled implantation of dopant of the same type as the channel near the source and drain edges. It limits the spread of the drain depletion region and suppresses short-channel effects.
Implant dose
The number of ions implanted per unit area in ion implantation, expressed in cm⁻². It is set by beam current and implant time.
Ion implantation
A technique that ionizes impurities, accelerates them and implants them into the wafer. The dose and depth can be controlled precisely.
Laser anneal
Heating only the near-surface region of a wafer for an extremely short time with laser light. It achieves high activation with almost no diffusion.
Plasma doping
Implanting low-energy ions by exposing the wafer directly to a plasma containing the dopant. It delivers high doses quickly and dopes 3D structures such as fins fairly uniformly.
Pre-amorphization implant (PAI)
Implanting germanium or similar first to make the surface amorphous, so that the following implant does not channel and a shallow junction results.
Projected range
The average depth at which implanted ions come to rest. It is deeper with higher acceleration energy and with lighter ions.
Retrograde well
A well whose doping is higher deeper below the surface, made by high-energy implantation. It suppresses punch-through and latch-up while keeping the channel doping at the surface low.
Shadowing
When implanting at a tilt, the effect of tall patterns such as resist casting shadows so that some areas receive no implant.
Solid solubility
The maximum concentration of an impurity that can dissolve in a crystal at a given temperature. It sets the upper limit of electrically active dopant concentration.
Spike anneal
An anneal that ramps the wafer quickly to a high temperature and cools it again with almost no hold time. It activates dopants while limiting diffusion, for shallow junctions.
Transient enhanced diffusion (TED)
A temporary speed-up of boron and other dopant diffusion early in post-implant annealing, caused by excess silicon interstitials from the implant. It hinders the formation of shallow junctions.

Deposition (CVD and PVD)Practice questions →

ALD
Atomic layer deposition. A deposition method that repeats self-limiting surface reactions of precursor gases to control film thickness one atomic layer at a time.
Area-selective deposition
Growing a film only in certain places by exploiting differences in the underlying materials. Because it can save lithography and etch steps, it is being researched and introduced for advanced interconnects.
CVD
Chemical vapor deposition. A method of depositing thin films on a wafer through chemical reactions of source gases.
Electroplating
Depositing metal by passing current through a plating bath with the wafer as the cathode. It fills copper interconnect trenches, with additives making the fill proceed from the bottom up.
Electrostatic chuck
A mechanism that holds the wafer to its stage by electrostatic force. It works in vacuum and allows backside gas for temperature control; it is widely used in deposition and etch tools.
HDP-CVD (high-density plasma CVD)
CVD using a high-density plasma that deposits while ion sputtering trims film at the trench openings. It has been used to fill narrow trenches with insulator without voids.
LPCVD (low-pressure CVD)
Thermal CVD performed under reduced pressure. Faster gas diffusion gives good thickness uniformity and step coverage, and many wafers can be processed at once in a vertical furnace. It is used for polysilicon and nitride films.
Magnetron sputtering
Sputtering with magnets behind the target that trap electrons near it and raise plasma density. It increases deposition rate and is the mainstream sputtering method today.
PECVD (plasma-enhanced CVD)
CVD that decomposes source gases with plasma to deposit films. It can deposit at lower temperatures than thermal CVD and is used for insulating films in interconnect steps and the like.
Reactive sputtering
Sputtering a metal target in an atmosphere containing a reactive gas such as nitrogen or oxygen to form nitride or oxide films. It is used for films such as titanium nitride (TiN).
Spin coating
Dispensing a liquid at the center of the wafer and spinning it at high speed so centrifugal force spreads a thin, uniform film. It is used for photoresist and spin-on dielectrics.
Sputtering
A type of PVD in which ions in a plasma bombard a target and the ejected atoms are deposited on the wafer.
Step coverage
A measure of how uniformly a film covers the sidewalls and bottoms of steps and holes compared with flat areas. ALD gives excellent step coverage.
TEOS
Short for tetraethoxysilane, a liquid organosilicon compound widely used as the source for CVD silicon oxide. It gives oxide films with good step coverage.
Vacuum evaporation
Forming a film by heating a material in vacuum until it evaporates and condenses on the substrate. Electron-beam heating handles high-melting-point materials, but step coverage is poor.

LithographyPractice questions →

ArF excimer laser
A laser using argon and fluorine that emits 193 nm ultraviolet light. Combined with immersion, ArF immersion lithography is a leading-edge technique alongside EUV. The 248 nm version is the KrF excimer laser.
Bottom anti-reflective coating (BARC)
A layer coated under the resist that suppresses standing waves in the resist and dimension variation over topography caused by light reflected from the substrate.
Chemically amplified resist
A resist in which acid generated by exposure catalyzes a chain of reactions during the post-exposure bake (PEB). It needs little exposure dose and is widely used from KrF onward, but is sensitive to basic contamination.
Depth of focus (DOF)
The range of focus over which a pattern prints with acceptable quality. Raising the numerical aperture improves resolution but reduces DOF, which makes wafer planarity important.
EUV lithography
Lithography using extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nm. It uses reflective optics and a vacuum environment.
High-NA EUV
Next-generation EUV lithography that raises the projection optics' numerical aperture from 0.33 to 0.55 to improve resolution.
Immersion lithography
Exposure with the space between the lens and the wafer filled with water. It allows a numerical aperture above 1, so ArF excimer lasers can form finer patterns.
Line edge roughness (LER)
Nanometer-scale roughness along the edges of fine resist lines. It causes line-width variation (LWR) and leads to variation in transistor characteristics.
Multiple patterning
Forming patterns with a pitch too fine for a single exposure by splitting them across several exposures or process steps. Splitting into two is double patterning.
Nanoimprint lithography
Transferring patterns by pressing a mold with fine features into resist on the wafer. It can make fine patterns without expensive optics.
Numerical aperture (NA)
A value that expresses the range of angles of light a lens can collect. A larger NA gives higher resolution.
OPC (optical proximity correction)
A technique that corrects mask patterns in advance, anticipating distortion of the printed pattern caused by diffraction of light and similar effects.
Overlay
How accurately a new pattern is placed relative to the patterns made in earlier steps. Misalignment causes defects such as missed contacts, so it is controlled at the nanometer level.
Pellicle
A thin membrane stretched a short distance above a photomask. It keeps particles off the mask surface, and particles landing on the pellicle are out of focus and do not print.
Phase-shift mask
A photomask that shifts the phase of light passing through neighboring openings, using interference to improve image contrast and resolution.
Photomask (reticle)
The master pattern used for exposure, typically a light-blocking pattern on a quartz substrate (reflective for EUV). For reduction scanners it is made four times larger than the pattern on the wafer.
Photoresist
A light-sensitive material whose solubility in developer changes when exposed. In positive resist the exposed areas dissolve; in negative resist they remain.
Positive photoresist
A photoresist whose exposed areas dissolve and are removed during development. One whose exposed areas remain is a negative resist.
Process window
The range of exposure dose and focus over which pattern dimensions stay within tolerance. The wider it is, the more robust manufacturing is to variation.
Scanner
An exposure tool that moves the mask and wafer in sync under a slit of light. It is the mainstream production lithography tool and generally projects the mask pattern at 1/4 size.

EtchingPractice questions →

ALE (atomic layer etching)
Etching by alternating surface modification and removal so that each cycle removes about one atomic layer. It gives extremely precise control of etch depth.
Anisotropic etching
Etching that proceeds only in a particular direction (mainly vertically). Needed to pattern fine features.
Antenna effect
Damage to a gate oxide when a connected conductor, such as a long wire, collects charge like an antenna during plasma processing. Design rules limit the area ratio of such wires to prevent it.
ARDE (aspect-ratio-dependent etching)
The slowdown of etch rate in narrower, deeper features because ions and radicals reach the bottom less easily. Also called the microloading effect.
Ashing
Removing photoresist that is no longer needed after etching or implantation by oxidizing it to ash, typically with an oxygen plasma.
Aspect ratio
The ratio of a hole's or trench's depth to its width. Higher ratios make etching and deposition harder; memory holes in 3D NAND reach several tens or more.
Buffered hydrofluoric acid (BHF)
Hydrofluoric acid buffered with ammonium fluoride. It is used for wet etching of SiO2 with a stable etch rate and little damage to resist.
Dry etching
Removing material with plasma or gas instead of liquid chemicals. Combining ion bombardment allows anisotropic etching, which is essential for fine patterns.
Endpoint detection
Detecting the moment when the target film has been fully etched, commonly by monitoring changes in the plasma's emission spectrum.
Hard mask
A durable mask of nitride, carbon, metal or similar used when photoresist alone cannot survive the etch, for example in deep-hole etching.
ICP (inductively coupled plasma)
A method that generates a high-density plasma with RF current in a coil. It makes it easy to control plasma density and the energy of ions reaching the wafer independently.
Isotropic etching
Etching that proceeds at the same rate vertically and laterally. It cuts in under the mask (undercut).
Overetch
Continuing to etch for a set time after endpoint so the film is removed everywhere on the wafer. It is done under conditions with high selectivity to the underlying layer.
Plasma
A state in which a gas is ionized into a mixture of electrons, ions and radicals (reactive neutrals). It is used in many processes, including dry etching, plasma CVD and ashing.
Radical
A neutral atom or molecule with an unpaired electron that is highly reactive chemically. Plasmas contain far more radicals than ions, and they drive chemical etching and deposition.
RIE (reactive ion etching)
Dry etching that combines reactive species in a plasma with ions accelerated by an electric field. It enables vertical, anisotropic etching.
Seasoning
Running dummy wafers before product to stabilize the condition of an etch (or similar) chamber's walls, since wall changes alter process results.
Selectivity
In etching, the ratio of the etch rate of the target film to that of the mask or underlying film. Higher selectivity means less damage to the underlayer.
Self-bias
The negative DC potential that the wafer electrode naturally acquires in a capacitively coupled plasma. It accelerates positive ions toward the wafer, aiding anisotropic etching.
Wet etching
Etching that dissolves films by immersing them in chemicals. Selectivity is high and damage low, but it generally proceeds isotropically and does not suit fine patterning.

CMP and cleaningPractice questions →

Ceria (cerium oxide)
An abrasive used in CMP slurries. It reacts chemically with oxide and gives high selectivity to nitride, so it is used for STI CMP.
CMP
Chemical mechanical polishing. Planarizes the wafer surface by combining the chemical action of a slurry with mechanical polishing by a pad.
Dilute hydrofluoric acid (DHF)
Hydrofluoric acid diluted with water. It removes native oxide from silicon and leaves a hydrophobic, hydrogen-terminated surface.
Dishing
A CMP phenomenon in which the center of a wide metal line is over-polished into a dish-like depression.
Erosion
In CMP, excessive polishing of an entire region of dense lines, insulator included, so it ends up lower than its surroundings. Along with dishing of wide lines, it is a typical CMP defect.
Megasonic cleaning
A cleaning method that applies ultrasound of about 1 MHz to the cleaning solution to remove fine particles from wafer surfaces.
Native oxide
A thin oxide about 1 nm thick that forms naturally when silicon is exposed to air or water. Because it raises contact resistance and disturbs epitaxial growth, it is removed right before such steps.
Pad conditioning
Abrading the surface of a CMP polishing pad with a diamond tool to restore its texture and slurry-holding ability, stabilizing the removal rate. Also called dressing.
Pattern collapse
A defect in which tall, fine patterns topple or stick together during cleaning and drying because of the surface tension of liquid left between them.
Preston's equation
The empirical rule that the CMP removal rate is proportional to the product of polishing pressure and the relative speed between pad and wafer.
RCA clean
A cleaning method that removes particles and organics with SC-1 (ammonia and hydrogen peroxide) and metal contamination with SC-2 (hydrochloric acid and hydrogen peroxide).
SC-1 (APM)
A mixture of ammonia, hydrogen peroxide and water used first in RCA cleaning. It removes particles and organics while lightly etching the surface.
SC-2 (HPM)
A mixture of hydrochloric acid, hydrogen peroxide and water used after SC-1 in RCA cleaning to remove metal contamination.
Single-wafer cleaning
A cleaning method that processes one wafer at a time. It minimizes cross-contamination and allows per-wafer control. Immersing many wafers in a tank at once is batch cleaning.
Slurry
The liquid used in CMP, with fine abrasive particles dispersed in chemicals. It flattens surfaces by combining chemical action with mechanical polishing.
SPM (sulfuric acid-hydrogen peroxide mixture)
A mixture of sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide. Its strong oxidizing power removes organics such as resist.

Interconnect processesPractice questions →

Air gap
Leaving part of the insulator between wires as empty space. Since air has a relative permittivity of about 1, it greatly reduces capacitance between wires.
Backside power delivery
Placing the power wiring on the back of the wafer so the front-side wiring can be used for signals. It reduces voltage drop and routing congestion.
Barrier metal
A thin metal film around copper wiring that prevents copper from diffusing into insulating films or silicon, such as Ta or TaN.
Black's equation
An empirical formula for interconnect lifetime under electromigration in terms of current density and temperature. Lifetime is inversely proportional to a power of current density and falls exponentially as temperature rises.
Crosstalk
Noise that appears on one wire when the signal on a neighboring wire changes, mainly through capacitive coupling. Wider spacing and shield wires reduce it.
Damascene process
A method of forming interconnects by etching trenches in an insulating film, filling them with metal, and removing the excess metal by CMP. Used for copper wiring.
Dual damascene
Forming both the trench for a wire and the hole for a via in the insulator, filling them with copper together and finishing with a single CMP step. It reduces process steps.
Interconnect via
A conductor that electrically connects the wiring layers above and below it in a multilevel interconnect. It is made by filling a hole in the insulator with metal.
IR drop
The drop in supply voltage reaching a circuit because current (I) flows through the resistance (R) of the power wiring. It increases delay and can cause malfunctions.
Low-k material
An interlayer dielectric with a low dielectric constant. It lowers the capacitance between wires, reducing signal delay and power consumption.
MOL (middle of line)
The contacts and local interconnects that link transistors (FEOL) to the multilevel wiring (BEOL). With scaling, their resistance has grown and now limits performance.
Multilevel interconnect
A structure that stacks many wiring layers separated by insulating films and connects them vertically with vias. Leading-edge ICs have more than ten layers.
Passivation layer
A film formed as the topmost layer of a chip to protect the circuitry from moisture, contamination and mechanical damage. Silicon nitride and polyimide are typical materials.
RC delay
Signal delay determined by the product of wire resistance (R) and capacitance (C). It grows as scaling makes wires thinner, limiting performance.
Seed layer
A thin copper film deposited by sputtering or similar before copper electroplating. It provides the conductive path for the plating current.
Silicide
A compound of silicon and a metal, such as NiSi or TiSi2. It is formed on source, drain and gate surfaces to reduce resistance and contact resistance.
Skin effect
The concentration of high-frequency current near the surface of a conductor, which raises its effective resistance. The higher the frequency, the thinner the surface layer carrying the current.
Transistor contact
The connection between a transistor's source, drain or gate and the lowest wiring layer. It is made by filling a contact hole in the insulator with tungsten or similar metal.

Process materials (gases and chemicals)Practice questions →

Arsine (AsH3)
A gas compound of arsenic and hydrogen used to introduce n-type dopant (arsenic) and to grow compound semiconductors such as GaAs. It is extremely toxic.
Mass flow controller (MFC)
A device in deposition and etch tools that precisely measures the mass flow of a gas and holds it at a set value.
NF3 (nitrogen trifluoride)
A gas used for cleaning CVD chambers and the like. Its global warming potential is high, so emission reduction is required.
Phosphine (PH3)
A gas compound of phosphorus and hydrogen used to introduce n-type dopant (phosphorus) into silicon. It is extremely toxic.
Precursor
A source compound that reacts to form the desired film in CVD or ALD, such as TEOS or metal-organic compounds.
Silane (SiH4)
A gas compound of silicon and hydrogen used as the source for CVD of polysilicon, silicon oxide and silicon nitride. It ignites spontaneously in air, so it is handled under strict control.
Specialty gases
High-purity gases with toxic or flammable properties used in semiconductor manufacturing, such as silane, arsine and phosphine. They are supplied under strict control.
TMAH
Tetramethylammonium hydroxide, a metal-ion-free organic alkali used as a photoresist developer. Care is needed because it can cause poisoning through skin absorption.
Ultrapure water
Water from which ions, particles and organic matter have been removed to the extreme. Used in large amounts for wafer cleaning, with purity controlled by resistivity and other measures.

Back-end and evaluation

Packaging and assemblyPractice questions →

Backgrinding
Grinding the back of a wafer before dicing to thin it for packaging. Chips for stacking are thinned to a few tens of micrometers.
BGA
Ball grid array. A high-pin-count package with solder balls arranged in a grid on its underside.
CSP (chip-scale package)
A general term for packages shrunk to nearly the size of the chip. They are widely used in compact devices such as smartphones.
Dicing
Cutting a wafer into individual chips (dies). Besides sawing with a rotating blade, there are methods such as stealth dicing, which forms a modified layer inside the wafer with a laser.
Die bonding
Attaching and fixing a diced chip at its position on a lead frame or package substrate, using adhesive, solder or sintered material.
Flip chip
A mounting method in which the chip is placed face down and connected directly to the substrate with bumps. Short connections make it suitable for high pin counts.
Lead frame
A thin metal sheet combining the pad that holds the chip with the external terminals (leads). It is used in packages such as QFP.
Reflow soldering
Printing solder paste on a circuit board, placing components and heating everything in an oven to melt the solder. It is the standard method for surface mounting.
Thermal resistance
A measure of how hard it is for heat to flow, in °C/W. Multiplying dissipated power by thermal resistance gives the temperature rise. Junction-to-ambient thermal resistance is written θJA.
Underfill
Resin filled into the gap between the chip and the substrate in flip-chip assembly. It spreads out stress from thermal expansion mismatch and protects the bump joints.
Wire bonding
A method of connecting the chip's bond pads to a lead frame or substrate with thin wires of gold, copper or similar metals.
WLP (wafer-level package)
A package in which redistribution wiring and external terminals are formed while the chips are still in wafer form, before dicing. The result is a small package about the size of the chip.

Testing and inspectionPractice questions →

ATE (automatic test equipment)
Equipment that applies test patterns to chips, measures their outputs and automatically judges pass or fail. Also called a tester.
ATPG (automatic test pattern generation)
Automatically generating test patterns that detect faults from a circuit's netlist. It is used together with scan design.
BIST (built-in self-test)
Test circuitry built into the chip so that it generates test patterns and checks results by itself. It is widely used, for example, to test memories.
Burn-in
A test that operates products under stress such as high temperature and high voltage to remove units prone to early failure before shipment.
DFT (design for testability)
A design approach that builds test features such as scan chains and BIST into the circuit in advance.
Fault coverage
The fraction of modeled faults that a set of test patterns can detect. The higher it is, the less likely defective chips are to escape.
IDDQ test
A test that measures the supply current of a CMOS circuit at rest and flags defects such as bridging faults when it is abnormally high.
KGD (known good die)
A chip confirmed to be good before packaging. It matters when several chips go into one package, because a single bad chip ruins the whole module.
Probe card
A fixture that presses many needles (probes) onto chip electrodes on the wafer to connect them electrically to a tester.
Scan test
A test method that uses scan chains, formed by linking a circuit's flip-flops in series, to set and observe internal states.
Stuck-at fault
The basic fault model in logic testing, which assumes a signal line is stuck at 0 or 1.
Wafer test (probe test)
Checking the electrical characteristics of each chip on the wafer by touching probes to its pads before dicing. It finds bad chips before assembly so they are not packaged needlessly.

Metrology and analysisPractice questions →

AFM (atomic force microscope)
A microscope that traces a sharp tip over a sample and measures fine surface topography from the forces between tip and surface. It is used, for example, to evaluate surface roughness.
CD-SEM
A scanning electron microscope for measuring pattern line widths (critical dimensions). Used for dimensional control after lithography and etching.
Defect inspection system
Equipment that finds particles and pattern defects on wafers, for example by detecting scattered light or by comparing images of neighboring dies.
EDX (energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy)
An analysis method that measures the energies of characteristic X-rays emitted under an electron beam to identify which elements are present and how much. It is used with SEM or TEM.
Ellipsometry
A measurement method that shines polarized light on a sample and determines thin-film thickness and refractive index from the change in polarization on reflection. It is nondestructive.
FIB (focused ion beam)
An instrument that mills or deposits material with a finely focused ion beam. It is used for cross-sectioning tiny areas, preparing thin TEM samples and editing circuits.
Four-point probe method
A method that passes current through the outer two of four collinear needles and measures voltage across the inner two to find the sheet resistance of wafers and thin films.
SEM (scanning electron microscope)
A microscope that scans a finely focused electron beam over a sample and detects secondary electrons and other signals to image the surface. It resolves features of a few nanometers, far beyond optical microscopes.
SIMS (secondary ion mass spectrometry)
A method that sputters a sample with primary ions and mass-analyzes the secondary ions to measure impurity depth profiles with high sensitivity.
TEM (transmission electron microscope)
A microscope that observes thinned samples by passing an electron beam through them. With atomic-level resolution, it reveals cross-sectional structures and crystal defects.
TXRF (total reflection X-ray fluorescence)
An analysis method that shines X-rays at a grazing angle so they totally reflect, then sensitively measures the fluorescent X-rays from trace metal contamination on the wafer surface.
XPS (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy)
An analysis method that determines the elements in the top few nanometers of a surface and their chemical bonding states from the energies of photoelectrons emitted under X-ray irradiation.
XRD (X-ray diffraction)
An analysis method that shines X-rays on a crystal and uses the angles and intensities of the diffracted beams to determine crystal structure, lattice constant, film strain and crystal quality.

Yield and quality controlPractice questions →

APC (advanced process control)
Automatically adjusting the process settings for the next lot or wafer based on earlier measurements, as in run-to-run control. It reduces variation.
Cause-and-effect diagram
A diagram that organizes the causes affecting a problem (the effect) in the shape of a fish skeleton. Also called a fishbone diagram.
Control chart
A chart that plots process measurements over time against a center line and control limits (usually ±3σ) to judge whether the process is stable. Points outside the limits or runs on one side signal problems.
FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis)
A method that lists the possible failure modes of a product or process, evaluates their effects and severity, and plans countermeasures in advance.
Killer defect
A defect that makes a chip fail, such as particles or pattern anomalies of a size and location that cause shorts or opens in wiring.
Pareto chart
A chart that lists items such as defect types as bars in descending order of count, with a line for the cumulative share. It shows which items to tackle first.
Poisson yield model
A model that assumes defects are randomly distributed and expresses yield as Y = exp(−D×A), where D is defect density and A is chip area.
Process capability index (Cpk)
An index of how much margin a process's variation and mean offset leave relative to the specification limits. A common benchmark is 1.33 or higher.
SPC (statistical process control)
A method of maintaining quality by monitoring process data with control charts and the like and detecting anomalies statistically.
Systematic defect
A defect that occurs repeatedly at particular patterns or locations because of interactions between design and process. It is distinguished from random defects caused by chance particles.
Yield
The proportion of good products to the total produced. A key factor in semiconductor manufacturing cost.

ReliabilityPractice questions →

Arrhenius model
A model in which reaction or degradation rates vary with temperature as exp(−Ea/kT). It is used to estimate lifetime at operating temperature from accelerated tests at higher temperatures.
Bathtub curve
A curve showing how the failure rate changes over time. It consists of the early-failure, random-failure and wear-out periods and is shaped like a bathtub.
Electromigration
The movement of metal atoms in interconnects caused by electric current. It creates voids and hillocks that lead to open or short circuits.
ESD (electrostatic discharge)
A sudden discharge of static electricity from a charged person or object. Applied to an IC pin it can destroy gate oxides and other structures, so I/O pins have protection circuits.
FIT rate
A unit of failure rate: 1 FIT is one failure per billion (10⁹) device-hours. It is used for parts with very low failure rates, such as semiconductors.
Hot carrier injection (HCI)
Degradation in which carriers accelerated by the strong field near the drain are injected into the gate insulator, shifting the threshold voltage and reducing drive current.
Latch-up
A condition in which a parasitic thyristor structure in a CMOS circuit turns on and a large current keeps flowing between supply and ground. It is prevented by well-contact placement, guard rings and similar measures.
MTTF (mean time to failure)
The average time until a non-repairable product fails. It is used as an indicator of product lifetime.
NBTI (negative bias temperature instability)
A degradation phenomenon in which the magnitude of a pMOS threshold voltage gradually increases under negative gate voltage at high temperature.
Soft error
A temporary flip of data stored in memory or flip-flops caused by neutrons from cosmic rays, alpha particles and the like. The device is not damaged, so rewriting restores correct operation.
TDDB (time-dependent dielectric breakdown)
A phenomenon in which an insulating film under sustained voltage eventually breaks down even below its breakdown voltage. It is used to evaluate the lifetime of gate oxides and the like.
Weibull distribution
A probability distribution widely used to analyze lifetime data. Its shape parameter can represent early-failure, random-failure and wear-out behavior.

Design and industry

IC design flow and EDAPractice questions →

Clock gating
A low-power design technique that stops the clock to idle circuit blocks, avoiding wasted switching power.
DRC (design rule check)
Verification that checks whether a layout follows the geometric rules required for manufacturing, such as line widths and spacings.
FPGA
A chip whose logic configuration the user can rewrite after manufacture. It is used for prototypes, low-volume products and ASIC verification.
High-level synthesis (HLS)
Automatically generating RTL from an algorithm written in C, C++ or similar. It is used in developing FPGAs and accelerators.
Logic synthesis
The design step that converts a circuit described in RTL into a netlist made of gates from a cell library.
LVS
Short for layout versus schematic: checking that the connectivity extracted from the layout matches the schematic or netlist. It is always run before tape-out, along with DRC.
Netlist
Data describing the elements (cells) of a circuit and how they are connected. It is the output of logic synthesis and the input to place-and-route and timing analysis.
PDK (process design kit)
The set of design data a foundry gives to designers, including design rules, device models, cell libraries and verification rule decks.
Place and route
The design step that arranges cells on the chip (placement) from the netlist produced by logic synthesis and determines the wiring between them (routing).
RTL (register-transfer level)
A design abstraction that describes circuits by how data moves and is processed between registers. It is written in Verilog or VHDL and serves as the input to logic synthesis.
SPICE
A circuit simulator that calculates voltages and currents in transistor-level circuits in detail using device models. It is used for analog design and cell characterization.
STA (static timing analysis)
A method that calculates the delay of every path in a circuit without test patterns and checks that timing constraints are met.
Standard cell
A pre-designed, pre-verified building block such as a logic gate or flip-flop. Cells share a common height so automatic place-and-route can line them up to build large circuits.
Tape-out
Handing over the finished, verified layout data to manufacturing for mask making. The name comes from the days when data was delivered on magnetic tape.

Cleanrooms and equipmentPractice questions →

Airborne molecular contamination (AMC)
Gaseous contaminants in the air such as acids, alkalis and organics. Particle filters cannot remove them, so chemical filters are used.
Downflow
A cleanroom airflow method that sends clean air in one direction from the entire ceiling to the floor, quickly carrying generated particles away under the floor.
EFEM
Short for equipment front end module: the unit at the front of a tool that opens FOUPs and transfers wafers into the tool in a clean environment. It includes load ports and a transfer robot.
FFU (fan filter unit)
A unit combining a fan with a high-efficiency filter. Arrays of them in the cleanroom ceiling blow clean air downward.
FOUP
A sealed container for transporting and storing 300 mm wafers.
HEPA filter
A high-efficiency air filter that captures at least 99.97% of 0.3 µm particles. Filters that capture even finer particles are ULPA filters.
ISO 14644-1
The international standard that defines cleanroom cleanliness classes by the number of airborne particles. The smaller the number, the cleaner.
Mini-environment
Keeping only the small space around the wafers highly clean. Sealed carriers such as FOUPs combined with EFEMs on the tools lower the cleanliness required of the room as a whole, reducing cost.
OHT (overhead hoist transport)
Vehicles that run along ceiling rails and automatically carry FOUPs between tools. They are the backbone of automated transport in 300 mm fabs.
ULPA filter
A high-performance air filter that captures even finer particles than a HEPA filter. It is used in the ceilings of high-cleanliness cleanrooms.

Environment, health and safetyPractice questions →

Abatement system
Equipment that removes hazardous substances from tool exhaust gas by combustion, adsorption, water scrubbing and the like.
GHS
The internationally harmonized system that communicates chemical hazards with pictograms and precautionary statements.
Local exhaust ventilation
Equipment that draws in gases and mists at their source, such as tools handling chemicals, and exhausts them. It keeps workers from inhaling hazardous substances.
Lockout/tagout (LOTO)
A procedure that isolates energy sources such as power and gas before maintenance and fits locks and tags so no one can accidentally restore them during the work.
Lower explosive limit (LEL)
The lowest concentration in air at which a flammable gas can burn or explode. Gas detectors are often set to alarm at a percentage of this level.
Oxygen deficiency
A condition in which the oxygen concentration in air falls below 18%. It tends to occur where large amounts of nitrogen are used or in confined spaces, and can cause unconsciousness within a few breaths.
Perfluorocarbons
Also called PFCs. Fluorinated gases such as CF4 and C2F6 used in etching and chamber cleaning. Their greenhouse effect is very large.
Risk assessment
Identifying the hazards of tasks or chemicals, estimating the size of the risks and deciding countermeasures in order of priority.
RoHS Directive
The EU regulation restricting the use of specific hazardous substances such as lead and mercury in electrical and electronic equipment.
SDS (safety data sheet)
A document describing the hazards of a chemical, how to handle it, first-aid measures and more.

Semiconductor industry and marketPractice questions →

ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit)
An IC designed specifically for one application or customer. It beats general-purpose chips on performance and power, but high development costs suit it to high-volume uses.
Dennard scaling
The rule that shrinking transistor dimensions and supply voltage by the same factor gives faster, denser circuits at constant power density. It broke down in the mid-2000s when voltages could no longer be lowered easily.
Discrete semiconductor
The category of semiconductor products that perform one function as a single device, such as diodes, individual transistors and power MOSFETs, as opposed to integrated circuits.
Fabless
A company model that owns no fabrication plant and focuses on designing and selling semiconductors.
Foundry
A company or business model that specializes in manufacturing semiconductors designed by other companies.
IDM (integrated device manufacturer)
A semiconductor company that does everything in-house, from design through front-end and back-end manufacturing to sales.
Moore's law
The empirical rule that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles about every two years. It has served as a target for semiconductor scaling.
MPW (multi-project wafer)
Prototyping several customers' designs together on one wafer (one mask set). Sharing the expensive mask cost makes it popular with universities and startups.
OSAT
A company that provides back-end services such as semiconductor assembly and testing under contract.
Silicon cycle
The recurring boom-and-bust pattern of the semiconductor market driven by supply and demand. One cause is that fabs take years to build, so supply lags changes in demand.
SoC (system on chip)
An IC that integrates the functions a system needs, such as CPU, GPU, memory controller and communications, on one chip. The heart of a smartphone is a typical example.

Advanced packaging (3D and chiplets)Practice questions →

2.5D packaging
Placing several chips side by side on an intermediate substrate such as a silicon interposer and connecting them with dense, fine wiring. It is used, for example, to connect GPUs and HBM.
3D packaging
Stacking chips vertically and connecting them with TSVs or hybrid bonding. Shorter connections improve speed and power, but heat removal becomes a challenge.
Chiplet
A small chip made for a specific function. Several chiplets are connected inside one package to build a larger system.
CoWoS
TSMC's 2.5D packaging technology, which places logic chips and HBM side by side on a silicon interposer. It is widely used for AI GPUs and similar products.
HBM
High Bandwidth Memory. Multiple DRAM dies stacked with TSVs, achieving very high bandwidth through an extremely wide bus.
Hybrid bonding
A technique that directly bonds copper electrodes and dielectric surfaces without bumps, providing huge numbers of connections at extremely fine pitch.
Microbump
A bump far smaller than an ordinary solder ball, used to connect TSV-stacked chips or chips to an interposer. The pitch is a few tens of micrometers.
Redistribution layer (RDL)
A wiring layer formed on a chip or wafer surface to route pads out to the positions of the external terminals. It is used in fan-out packages and interposers.
Silicon interposer
A silicon intermediate substrate that holds chips side by side and connects them densely with fine wiring. It is used to connect GPUs and HBM, among other things.
TSV (through-silicon via)
An electrode that passes through the silicon substrate. It connects stacked chips vertically over a short distance.
UCIe
Short for Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express: an industry-standard die-to-die interface intended to let chiplets from different companies connect to each other.

Computer systems

Computer ArithmeticPractice questions →

Arithmetic shift
A right shift of a signed integer that copies the sign bit into the vacated high bits. Shifting a negative number right by one divides by 2, rounding toward negative infinity. Filling with zeros is a logical shift.
bfloat16
A 16-bit floating-point format with 1 sign, 8 exponent and 7 significand bits. Sharing FP32's exponent width gives it a wide range, so despite low precision it is used for AI training. FP16, with 5 exponent and 10 significand bits, is more precise but has a narrow range.
Booth's algorithm
A multiplication method that recodes the multiplier's bits, for example in pairs, into signed digits to reduce the number of partial products. Radix-4 Booth encoding roughly halves them.
Carry-lookahead adder
An adder that computes carries in parallel from each bit's generate signal g = a·b and propagate signal p = a ⊕ b, without waiting for carries to ripple. Delay stays small even for wide operands.
Carry-save adder
An adder that reduces three numbers to two, a sum and a carry, without propagating carries. Made of independent full adders per bit, its delay is constant, so multipliers use it to add many partial products.
Catastrophic cancellation
Loss of significant digits when subtracting two nearly equal numbers, as their leading digits cancel and the relative weight of existing rounding error grows. It is avoided by rearranging formulas.
Exponent field
The bits of a floating-point number giving the power of two. To represent negative exponents, the true exponent plus a bias (127 for single precision) is stored.
Fixed point
Representing fractions with ordinary integer bit patterns and an agreed binary-point position. Integer arithmetic circuits suffice, giving small, low-power hardware, but the range is narrow. Formats are often named by fraction bits, as in Q15.
Floating-point absorption
Loss of the smaller operand's low-order digits when adding numbers of very different magnitude, because they do not fit in the significand. It matters when summing many small values.
FP8
8-bit floating-point formats: E4M3 (4 exponent, 3 significand bits) and E5M2 (5 exponent, 2 significand bits), chosen to trade precision against range. Used to make AI training and inference faster and more power-efficient.
Fused multiply-add
An operation computing a × b + c in one instruction, rounding only once at the end without rounding the intermediate product. It is faster and more accurate than separate multiply and add and is heavily used in matrix computation.
IEEE 754
The international standard defining floating-point formats and arithmetic, including single (32-bit) and double (64-bit) precision, rounding, and special values such as infinity and NaN, so results agree across computers.
INT8
An 8-bit signed integer (−128 to 127). In AI inference, quantizing weights and activations to INT8 with a scale factor greatly cuts computation and memory and allows fast processing on small integer circuits.
Machine epsilon
The difference between 1 and the next larger representable floating-point number, a measure of relative precision: 2⁻²³ (about 1.19 × 10⁻⁷) in single precision and 2⁻⁵² (about 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶) in double.
NaN
Short for Not a Number, a floating-point special value produced by operations with no numeric result, such as 0 ÷ 0 or the square root of a negative number. It has an all-ones exponent and a nonzero significand, and compares unequal even to itself.
Round half to even
Rounding to the nearest representable value and, on an exact tie, to the one whose last bit is even (0). It is the IEEE 754 default and keeps rounding errors from accumulating in one direction.
Saturating arithmetic
Arithmetic that clamps results exceeding the representable range to the maximum or minimum instead of wrapping around. Used in audio and image signal processing to avoid large errors.
Significand
The bits of a floating-point number holding its significant digits. For normalized numbers the leading 1 is implied, so single precision stores 23 bits but has 24 bits of precision.
Subnormal number
A floating-point number whose exponent field is all zeros, without an implied leading 1, representing values smaller than the smallest normal number. It fills the gap to zero gradually (gradual underflow), so x − y = 0 implies x = y.
Wallace tree
A multiplier structure that reduces many partial products in parallel with a tree of carry-save adders. Its depth grows only logarithmically with width, so it is used in fast multipliers; a final adder sums the last two numbers.

ISA & AssemblyPractice questions →

Addressing mode
The way an instruction specifies the address of data or a jump target. Examples include immediate, register, base register plus offset and PC-relative.
Application binary interface (ABI)
The machine-level agreement that lets compiled programs and libraries work together, covering the calling convention, data type sizes and alignment, how system calls are made and more.
Assembler
Software that converts a program written in assembly language into machine instruction bit patterns. It turns labels into addresses and expands pseudo-instructions into real instructions.
Branch delay slot
An ISA rule that the instruction right after a branch always executes, taken or not. Found in older ISAs such as MIPS, it exposed a pipeline detail in the ISA and later became a burden.
Callee-saved register
A register guaranteed to keep its value across a call; if the called function uses it, the function saves and restores it. In RISC-V these are s0-s11.
Caller-saved register
A register the called function may overwrite freely; if the caller needs its value after the call, the caller saves it. In RISC-V these include t0-t6 and a0-a7.
Calling convention
The agreement on how arguments and return values are passed in registers or on the stack, and who saves which registers, when calling a function. It is a key part of the ABI.
Compressed instruction
An instruction that encodes a common operation in fewer bits than usual. The RISC-V C extension adds 16-bit instructions and cuts code size by roughly 25-30%.
Condition code
Flags such as zero, negative, carry and overflow that record the outcome of a compare or arithmetic operation. Conditional branches in x86 and Arm test them; RISC-V has none.
Frame pointer
A register that keeps pointing at the stack position on function entry; s0 (fp) in RISC-V. Locals can be addressed at fixed offsets, and the call chain is easy to walk.
Immediate operand
A constant embedded directly in an instruction. In RISC-V addi it is a 12-bit signed integer that is sign-extended before use.
Leaf function
A function that calls no other functions. Its return address is never overwritten, so it need not be saved, and it often needs no stack frame at all.
Load-store architecture
An ISA style in which only load and store instructions access memory and arithmetic works only on registers. Used by RISC-V and Arm, it gives instructions a uniform shape that is easy to pipeline.
Micro-operation
A simple internal operation into which a CPU decodes an instruction, also written μop. x86 CPUs split complex instructions into μops and execute them with RISC-like fast techniques.
PC-relative addressing
Specifying a branch target or data address as a distance from the current instruction's address (PC). The distance is unchanged wherever the program is loaded, so it underpins position-independent code.
Pseudo-instruction
Something written like an instruction in assembly that the assembler replaces with one or more real instructions, such as RISC-V nop, mv, li, j and ret.
Return address
The address to go back to when a function finishes. In RISC-V, jal puts the next instruction's address in ra (x1); in x86, call pushes it on the stack.
Sign extension
Widening a signed integer to more bits by copying its top (sign) bit into all the new upper bits, which preserves its value. Filling the upper bits with zeros is zero extension.
Stack frame
The area reserved on the stack each time a function is called. It holds the return address, saved registers and local variables and is freed when the function returns.
Stack pointer
The register that points to the current top of the stack; sp (x2) in RISC-V. In many ISAs the stack grows toward lower addresses.

CompilersPractice questions →

Abstract syntax tree
A tree representing program structure with operations and statements as nodes. Meaningless details such as parentheses and separators are omitted, and operator precedence is expressed by the tree's shape.
Basic block
A sequence of instructions with no branch entering or leaving in the middle, always executed from start to end. Basic blocks are the nodes of a control-flow graph and units of local optimization.
Common subexpression elimination
An optimization that computes a repeatedly evaluated expression once and reuses the result, after confirming its operands do not change in between.
Constant folding
An optimization that evaluates expressions whose values are known at compile time and replaces them with the resulting constant, such as turning 3 * 4 into 12. It removes run-time computation.
Control-flow graph
A graph representing a program's execution flow, with basic blocks as nodes and branches between them as edges. It is used to detect loops and analyze data flow.
Function inlining
Replacing a function call with the body of the called function. It removes call overhead and enables further optimizations but enlarges the code.
Instruction scheduling
Reordering instructions without changing results so that latencies of operations and memory accesses are filled with independent instructions.
Intermediate representation
A form of the program inside a compiler that depends on neither the source language nor the target CPU. Most optimizations operate on it, separating language-specific front ends from CPU-specific back ends.
JIT compilation
Compiling frequently executed parts of a program to machine code while it runs, enabling optimizations based on run-time information. Used in runtimes for Java, JavaScript and others.
Lexical analysis
The first compiler phase, which splits the characters of source code into tokens such as identifiers, numbers, operators and keywords. Whitespace and comments are removed here.
LLVM
A compiler infrastructure built around a common intermediate representation (LLVM IR), combining language front ends, optimization passes and CPU back ends. It is used for many languages and CPUs.
Loop unrolling
Replicating a loop body several times to reduce the number of iterations. It cuts branch and counter overhead and gives more freedom to reorder instructions, at the cost of larger code.
Register allocation
Assigning program variables and temporaries to the CPU's limited registers, ensuring values in use at the same time get different registers, often via coloring an interference graph.
Register spilling
Saving some values to memory (the stack) during register allocation when more values are live than there are registers. The extra stores and reloads slow the code.
SSA form
An IR form in which variables are renamed so each is assigned exactly once, with φ functions selecting values at join points. Clear definition-use relationships make optimization easier.
Strength reduction
Replacing expensive operations such as multiplication with cheaper ones such as addition or shifts, for example adding 4 each iteration instead of computing i * 4 every time.
Syntax analysis (parsing)
The compiler phase that checks whether the token sequence follows the language grammar and builds the program's structure as a tree. Grammar errors are detected here.
Vectorization
Transforming a loop that performs the same computation on each element so that SIMD instructions process several elements at once. It requires no data dependence between iterations.

Linkers, Loaders & ExecutablesPractice questions →

Address space layout randomization (ASLR)
A countermeasure that changes the addresses of the stack, heap, shared libraries and executable every run so attackers cannot easily predict them.
BSS section
The area for global and static variables with no initializer (or zero). Only its size is recorded in the file and it is zero-filled at load time, so it does not enlarge the executable.
Dynamic linker
Software that, when a dynamically linked program starts, finds and loads shared libraries, applies relocations and binds symbols before starting the program, such as ld.so on Linux.
Dynamic linking
Linking that loads and binds shared libraries at run time. Libraries can be shared between processes and fixed by replacement, but a compatible library must be present on the system.
Entry point
The address where a program begins execution, recorded in the ELF header. In C it is usually start-up code such as _start, which initializes and then calls main.
Executable and Linkable Format (ELF)
The file format used for object files, executables and shared libraries on Linux and other UNIX-like systems and in embedded devices.
Global offset table
A table of the actual addresses of external variables and functions, filled in by the dynamic linker at run time. Position-independent code refers to them through it, so code pages need no patching.
Heap memory
The area from which a running program obtains memory of any size with malloc and the like and returns it with free. Repeated allocation and release can cause fragmentation.
Linker
Software that combines object files and libraries, performs symbol resolution and relocation, and produces an executable or shared library.
Linker script
A file telling the linker at which memory addresses to place each section. Embedded development uses it to lay out flash and RAM and to fix the interrupt vector address.
Memory fragmentation
The condition in which repeated allocation and release split free memory into small pieces, so a large contiguous block cannot be allocated even though the total is sufficient.
Name mangling
A C++ compiler's encoding of argument types and namespaces into a function's symbol name, so the linker can tell overloaded functions apart.
Object file
The file a compiler produces from one source file. It contains machine code, initial data, a symbol table and relocation entries, but addresses in other files are unresolved, so it cannot run until linked.
Position-independent code
Machine code that runs without modification at any load address, also called PIC. It uses PC-relative references and the GOT and is used for shared libraries and PIEs.
Position-independent executable
An executable whose main program is also built as position-independent code. It runs at any address, so ASLR can randomize the main program's code too.
Procedure linkage table (PLT)
A table of small stubs used to call shared-library functions. With lazy binding, the dynamic linker resolves the address on the first call and writes it into the GOT.
Program loader
The OS function that reads an executable into memory, maps its parts with the right permissions, sets up arguments on the stack and starts the program.
Relocation
Rewriting placeholder addresses in instructions and data with correct values once final addresses are decided. The linker does it, and with dynamic linking part of it is done at run time by the dynamic linker.
Shared library
A library loaded at run time by dynamic linking: .so on Linux, .dll on Windows. Built as position-independent code, its code pages can be shared by many processes.
Static library
An archive bundling many object files (.a on Linux). During static linking, the linker extracts only the objects it needs into the executable.
Static linking
Linking that copies the code of the libraries used into the executable. It runs without the libraries present, but the file is larger and library fixes require rebuilding.
Symbol resolution
The linker step that matches each function or variable name referenced in a file with its definition. No definition gives an undefined-reference error; more than one gives a multiple-definition error.
Symbol table
The list of function and variable names, with their addresses and attributes, contained in object files and executables. Used for linking and debugging; strip can remove it.
Weak symbol
A fallback definition replaced by any ordinary (strong) definition with the same name. Embedded code uses it for default interrupt handlers and similar.

OS & HardwarePractice questions →

ASID
A number tagged onto each TLB entry indicating which process's address space the translation belongs to. The TLB need not be flushed on a process switch, reducing TLB misses afterward.
Context switch
Switching the process or thread running on a CPU. Registers, the program counter and other state are saved and restored, and switching processes also switches address spaces.
Copy-on-write
Sharing duplicated memory with the original at first and copying a page only when either side tries to write it. It speeds process creation and saves memory.
Demand paging
Loading a program's pages into physical memory only when first accessed and a page fault occurs, rather than all at once. It speeds startup and saves memory for unused parts.
Device driver
An OS component that operates a specific peripheral. It handles the device's registers and interrupts and lets applications use the device through a common interface.
Hypervisor
Software that runs multiple virtual machines on one computer, allocating CPU, memory and devices to each guest OS and isolating them from one another. Also called a virtual machine monitor.
Interrupt handler
The routine a CPU runs when an interrupt or exception occurs. It saves the interrupted state, handles the cause and returns. Handlers should be kept as short as possible.
Interrupt vector table
A table listing, for each type of interrupt or exception, the start address of its handler. The CPU indexes it by the cause number to find where to begin handling.
IOMMU
A unit that translates and protects the addresses peripherals use for DMA. It keeps devices from writing to unauthorized memory and is also used when assigning devices to virtual machines.
Memory-mapped I/O
Assigning peripheral registers to specific addresses in the same address space as memory and accessing them with ordinary load and store instructions. That region is configured as uncached.
Nested paging
Hardware that walks two levels of page tables in succession, one managed by the guest OS and one by the hypervisor. It reduces hypervisor intervention and speeds up virtualization.
Non-maskable interrupt
An interrupt that is always accepted regardless of interrupt masking. It is used to report uncorrectable memory errors, serious hardware faults and watchdog events.
Page fault
An exception raised when an accessed page is not mapped to physical memory or the access type is not permitted. The OS either supplies the page and retries the instruction or treats it as an illegal access.
Page table
A table recording the mapping from virtual page numbers to physical page numbers. The OS builds one per process, and the CPU's MMU walks it to translate addresses. Each entry also holds protection bits.
Privileged mode
A CPU operating mode reserved for the OS kernel, in which dangerous operations such as setting page tables and controlling I/O are allowed. It is distinguished from user mode, where applications run.
System call
The mechanism by which applications ask the OS for services such as file I/O or memory allocation. A dedicated instruction raises an exception that switches to privileged mode and invokes the kernel.
Thrashing
A state in which physical memory is so short that pages are constantly evicted and reloaded, and little useful work gets done. It is relieved by running fewer processes or adding memory.
TLB shootdown
On a multiprocessor, the procedure for making other cores discard stale translations from their TLBs after a page table is modified. It uses inter-processor interrupts or instructions that broadcast the invalidation to all cores, and is costly.

Memory HierarchyPractice questions →

Average memory access time
The average time per memory access, computed as hit time + miss rate × miss penalty. A basic metric for comparing cache designs.
Cache line
The unit in which data moves between a cache and the level below, also called a cache block. Many CPUs use 64 bytes, and the low address bits give the position (offset) within the line.
Capacity miss
A cache miss caused by the working set being larger than the cache. It occurs even in a fully associative cache and is reduced by a larger cache or by improving data reuse.
Compulsory miss
A cache miss that inevitably occurs on the first access to data. A larger cache does not reduce it, but larger lines or prefetching can.
Conflict miss
A cache miss caused when several blocks compete for the same set and evict each other even though capacity remains. Higher associativity or shifting data placement reduces it.
Direct-mapped cache
A cache organization in which each address can be placed in exactly one location. It is simple with short hit time, but prone to conflict misses when addresses compete for the same slot.
Fully associative cache
A cache organization in which any data can be placed in any location. It has no conflict misses but must compare against every tag, so it suits small structures such as TLBs rather than large caches.
Loop blocking
An optimization that processes large arrays in small blocks that fit in the cache so loaded data is reused many times before eviction. Very effective for matrix multiplication.
LRU replacement
A cache replacement policy that evicts the least recently used data. It exploits temporal locality well, but exact tracking is costly with many ways, so approximations are used.
Miss penalty
The extra time needed to fetch data from the next level when a cache miss occurs, determined by the lower level's access time and the line transfer time.
Non-blocking cache
A cache that keeps servicing other accesses while a miss is outstanding. It can handle multiple misses at once, overlapping and hiding memory latency.
Prefetching
Loading data into the cache before it is requested because it is likely to be needed soon. Hardware prefetchers detect access patterns, and software prefetching uses explicit instructions.
Set-associative cache
A cache organization in which data can go in any of several locations (ways) within the one set selected by the address. An n-way cache compares n tags in parallel on each access.
Spatial locality
The tendency to access addresses near one just accessed, as when reading an array in order. It is why caches fetch data a whole cache line at a time.
Temporal locality
The tendency to access the same data or instructions again soon after a first access, as with variables reused inside a loop. One of the reasons caches work.
Write-allocate
A policy that, on a write miss, first brings the line into the cache and then writes it. Usually paired with write-back; writing directly to the lower level without fetching is called no-write-allocate.
Write-through
A write policy that writes to the level below (such as main memory) at the same time as the cache. The lower level stays up to date, but write traffic is heavy, so it is paired with a write buffer.

Multicore & SynchronizationPractice questions →

ABA problem
The problem that when a value changes from A to B and back to A, a CAS comparing only values fails to notice and succeeds. Lock-free data structures must guard against it.
Acquire-release semantics
An ordering guarantee: operations after an acquire cannot move before it, and operations before a release cannot move after it. Used for lock acquire and release, it is lighter than a full barrier.
Atomic instruction
An instruction that performs a memory read and write as one operation no other core can interrupt, such as CAS, fetch-and-add and LR/SC.
Barrier synchronization
Synchronization in which threads that arrive first wait until all threads reach the same point, keeping them in step phase by phase. Distinct from a memory barrier.
Bus snooping
A coherence approach in which each cache watches other cores' reads and writes on a shared bus or similar and updates its own line states. It suits small core counts.
Compare-and-swap
An atomic instruction that writes a new value only if memory still holds an expected value. Wrapped in a retry loop, it enables lock-free updates.
Condition variable
A synchronization mechanism that puts a thread to sleep until a condition holds and wakes it on a signal from another thread. The condition must be rechecked after waking.
Critical section
A part of a program that operates on shared data and must be run by only one thread at a time. It is protected by a lock and kept as short as possible.
Data race
A situation in which threads access the same variable concurrently without synchronization and at least one access is a write. Results vary between runs; in C and C++ it is undefined behavior.
Deadlock
A state in which threads each hold a resource while waiting for one held by another, so none can proceed. Fixing a global lock order prevents it.
Directory-based coherence
A coherence approach that records in a table which cores hold each line and notifies only those cores. Used in systems with many cores.
False sharing
A slowdown in which threads update different variables that happen to share a cache line, so the line bounces between cores.
Load-reserved/store-conditional (LR/SC)
A pair of instructions, also called LL/SC: if another core writes the address after the load-reserved, the store-conditional fails, enabling atomic updates.
Lock-free algorithm
A property of an algorithm that some thread can always make progress even if others stall partway. Built with atomic instructions such as CAS.
Memory barrier
An instruction guaranteeing that memory operations before it become visible to other cores before those after it, also called a fence, such as RISC-V fence.
Memory consistency model
The contract specifying in what order a core's reads and writes to multiple addresses may be observed by other cores. Examples include sequential consistency, x86 TSO and the weak models of Arm and RISC-V.
MESI protocol
A coherence protocol that tracks each cache line in one of four states: Modified, Exclusive, Shared or Invalid. On a write it invalidates other cores' copies.
Mutex
A lock that only one thread can hold at a time. Typically a thread that cannot get it sleeps and yields the CPU, and the OS wakes it when the lock is released.
Non-uniform memory access (NUMA)
An organization in which each core has near and far memory, and far accesses take longer. Placing data and threads on the same side is faster.
Reader-writer lock
A lock that admits any number of readers together but only one writer at a time, excluding everyone else while writing.
Semaphore
A synchronization mechanism holding a counter of remaining units: decrement before use (waiting at 0) and increment when done. Used to manage resources with a fixed number of units.
Sequential consistency
The most intuitive memory consistency model: all cores' operations appear in one order in which each core's operations keep their program order.
Spinlock
A lock that waits by repeatedly checking the lock variable in a loop. Fast for short waits, but it keeps using the CPU while waiting.
Store buffer
A small buffer in which a core holds writes temporarily. The core can move on without waiting for writes to complete, but other cores see the writes later.
Total store order (TSO)
The x86 memory consistency model. It allows only a later read of a different address to overtake an earlier write, reflecting the presence of a store buffer.
Transactional memory
A mechanism that runs critical sections speculatively, committing them as a whole if there is no conflict and rolling back and retrying if there is.

GPU ProgrammingPractice questions →

Bank conflict
In GPU shared memory, multiple threads of a warp accessing different addresses in the same bank at once, which serializes the accesses. Padding can avoid it.
Constant memory
A small GPU region for values that do not change during a kernel. When every thread in a warp reads the same address, one read is broadcast to all, which is fast.
CUDA
NVIDIA's platform for general-purpose computing on its GPUs. Kernels are written in an extended C++, with parallelism expressed as a grid, blocks and threads.
GPU kernel
A function that many threads execute simultaneously on a GPU. It is launched from the CPU-side program, and each thread uses its index to choose its data. Distinct from an OS kernel.
GPU occupancy
The ratio of warps actually resident on a GPU SM to the maximum it can hold, determined by register and shared-memory use.
GPU shared memory
Small, fast memory inside each GPU SM that threads of the same block can share. The program decides what is stored there.
Grid-stride loop
Writing a kernel as a loop in which each thread processes elements spaced by the total thread count, decoupling launch size from element count.
Latency hiding
Keeping waits, such as for memory, from showing up in performance by doing other work in the meantime. GPUs do it by switching among many warps.
Memory coalescing
Combining accesses into a few memory transactions when adjacent threads in a warp access adjacent addresses.
Mixed precision
Using different numeric precisions for different parts of a computation, such as multiplying in FP16 and accumulating in FP32, to balance speed and accuracy.
OpenCL
A standard parallel-programming framework for GPUs, CPUs, FPGAs and more from multiple vendors, defined by the Khronos Group industry consortium.
Parallel reduction
Computing a sum or similar over many values in parallel with a tree that repeatedly combines neighbors, finishing n values in log2 n stages.
Pinned memory
Memory locked so the OS will not move or evict its physical pages. The GPU can access it directly by DMA, speeding transfers and enabling asynchronous copies.
Prefix sum
Computing the running totals of an array from the start, also called a scan. It can be computed in parallel and is used to find write positions when compacting output.
Single instruction, multiple threads (SIMT)
An execution model in which programs are written per thread while hardware bundles threads into warps (32 on NVIDIA) that execute the same instruction in lockstep.
Streaming multiprocessor
On NVIDIA GPUs, a unit bundling arithmetic units, registers, shared memory and warp-scheduling circuitry. A GPU is built from many SMs.
Tensor core
A GPU unit that computes the product and sum of small matrices in one instruction, mainly with low-precision numbers, greatly speeding up AI.
Thread block
A group of GPU threads that runs on a single SM. Threads in a block can share data through shared memory and synchronize.
Unified memory
Memory accessible from CPU and GPU through the same pointer, with data migrating automatically on access. It simplifies programs, but migration costs need attention.
Warp divergence
The splitting of threads within one warp onto different paths at a conditional branch. The paths execute one after another, lowering efficiency.

AI Hardware & Software StackPractice questions →

AI compiler
Software that analyzes a model's computational graph, optimizes fusion, data layout and tiling, and generates code for the target hardware, such as XLA and TVM.
AI inference
Running only the forward pass with weights fixed by training to produce answers from inputs. It needs less computation and memory than training.
All-reduce
Communication that sums values held by all GPUs element by element and gives the result to every GPU, used to average gradients in data-parallel training.
Arithmetic intensity
Operations performed divided by bytes moved to and from memory. High values tend toward compute-bound, low values toward memory-bound.
Attention mechanism
The Transformer mechanism in which each token gathers information by computing its relationship to every other token. Its cost grows with the square of sequence length.
Backpropagation
The training computation that propagates the error between output and target backward from output to input to obtain each weight's gradient.
Batch size
The number of inputs processed together. Larger batches reuse weights for efficiency but increase latency and memory.
Computational graph
A representation of a model's computation with operations as nodes and tensor flows as edges, underpinning automatic differentiation and AI compiler optimization.
Data parallelism
Distributed training in which each GPU holds a full model replica, processes its share of the data, and gradients are averaged across GPUs before updating.
FlashAttention
A fast attention implementation that tiles the computation in on-chip memory and never writes the large intermediate table to HBM. Its results match standard attention.
Gradient checkpointing
Storing only some intermediate forward results and recomputing the rest during backpropagation, trading extra computation for less memory.
Inference runtime
Software that loads a trained model and runs inference quickly through graph optimization, quantization and hardware-specific kernel selection.
Knowledge distillation
Training a small student model to mimic a large teacher model's outputs so the small model achieves similar performance.
KV cache
Memory in LLM inference holding the attention keys and values of already processed tokens. It avoids recomputation but grows with context length and batch size.
Model FLOPs utilization (MFU)
The percentage of hardware peak performance achieved, computed from the operations a model actually requires.
ONNX
Open Neural Network Exchange: a common model format for exchanging models between frameworks and inference runtimes.
Operator fusion
An optimization that combines several operations into one kernel, avoiding writing intermediate results to memory and reading them back.
Pipeline parallelism
Splitting a model's layers across GPUs in sequence, streaming micro-batches to reduce the time GPUs sit idle (the bubble).
Post-training quantization
Converting a trained model's weights and activations to lower precision after examining value ranges with representative data.
Prefill
The first stage of LLM inference, processing all input prompt tokens together; it tends to be compute-bound. The following token-by-token generation is called decode.
Pruning
Zeroing out low-impact weights in a neural network to reduce computation and memory. Structured pruning, which follows fixed patterns, is easier to accelerate in hardware.
Quantization-aware training
Training that simulates quantization rounding so weights adapt to its error, preserving accuracy even at low bit widths.
Roofline model
A model expressing attainable performance as the smaller of peak compute and memory bandwidth × arithmetic intensity, used to tell compute-bound from memory-bound work.
Speculative decoding
Speeding up inference by having a small model draft several tokens ahead and a large model verify them together, accepting the correct ones at once.
Structured sparsity
Sparsity in which zero weights follow a fixed pattern; 2:4 sparsity, with two zeros in every four weights, lets matrix units compute about twice as fast.
Tensor parallelism
Splitting one layer's weight matrix by rows or columns across GPUs, computing partial products and combining them through communication.
TOPS
Trillions of operations per second, a performance figure for AI accelerators, usually a theoretical peak counted at low precision.

Embedded Systems & FirmwarePractice questions →

Baud rate
The number of symbols sent per second in serial communication; for a UART it equals bits per second and must match on both sides.
Bootloader
A small program that runs first at power-on, verifying and starting the main firmware or updating it with new firmware.
Brown-out detection
A function that resets the microcontroller when the supply voltage drops below the operating minimum, preventing unreliable behavior and corrupt flash writes.
Contact bounce
The rapid on-off flickering of a mechanical switch's contacts when pressed or released, removed by debouncing.
Debouncing
Processing that removes switch bounce, by software that accepts a change only after repeated identical readings or by hardware filtering.
Duty cycle
The fraction of each period during which a periodic pulse signal is high. In PWM it sets the average power.
GPIO
General-purpose input/output: microcontroller pins that software can configure as inputs or outputs, used for reading switches, lighting LEDs and so on.
Hard real-time
A strict timing requirement in which missing a deadline even once is a system failure, as in automotive brake or airbag control. Where occasional lateness is acceptable, it is soft real-time.
I2C
A synchronous serial bus connecting several devices on two wires, clock (SCL) and data (SDA), selecting the partner by address. The lines are open-drain with pull-ups.
Input capture
A timer function in which hardware records the counter value at the instant an input signal changes, allowing precise measurement of pulse width or frequency.
JTAG
A standard interface for chip testing and debugging that lets developers halt programs, inspect internal values and program flash. Arm also uses SWD, which needs fewer wires.
Over-the-air update
Updating a device's firmware via wireless communication, often using two flash slots so the device can still boot if an update fails.
Polling
A method in which the CPU repeatedly checks a peripheral's status to detect changes. Simple, but it consumes time and power even when nothing happens.
Prescaler
A circuit that divides the clock fed to a timer or similar by a fixed ratio, bringing a fast clock into a range the timer can count.
Priority inheritance
Temporarily raising a low-priority task holding a resource to the priority of the high-priority task waiting for it, preventing priority inversion.
Priority inversion
A situation in which a high-priority task waiting for a resource held by a low-priority task is held up because a medium-priority task preempts the low one.
Pull-up resistor
A resistor connecting a signal line to the supply so it rests at logic 1 when nothing drives it. It keeps input pins from floating and is used on I2C lines and elsewhere.
Pulse-width modulation (PWM)
A method that adjusts average voltage or power by varying the high time of an output while keeping the period constant. Used to dim LEDs and control motor speed.
Read-modify-write
A sequence that reads a value, changes part of it and writes it back. If interrupted midway, other changes can be lost.
Real-time operating system (RTOS)
An embedded OS that switches tasks by priority so the system responds within set time limits, such as FreeRTOS and Zephyr.
Sleep mode
A microcontroller state that stops clocks or power to the CPU and peripherals while idle to save power, waking on an interrupt or similar.
SPI
Serial Peripheral Interface: synchronous serial communication in which data is exchanged in both directions on a master-driven clock, with devices selected by chip-select lines. Used for fast parts.
Super loop
A firmware structure without an OS in which an infinite loop in main calls each task in turn, with urgent work done in interrupts.
UART
A circuit for asynchronous serial communication without a clock wire, in which both sides agree on a baud rate and characters are framed by start and stop bits.
Watchdog timer
A timer that resets the microcontroller if software does not kick it within a set time, recovering from runaway or hung programs.

HDL & VerificationPractice questions →

Asynchronous reset
A reset that initializes flip-flops as soon as the reset signal is asserted, regardless of the clock. Release should be synchronized to the clock.
Blocking assignment
Verilog's "=" assignment, which updates the left side immediately so the next statement sees the new value. Used in combinational always blocks and testbenches.
Combinational loop
A structure in which combinational output feeds back to its own input without a flip-flop. It can oscillate or fail to settle and defeats timing analysis, so it is avoided.
Constrained random verification
Verification that generates random input values within constraints allowed by the specification, broadly exercising cases people might not think of.
Four-state logic
The four values used in HDL simulation: 0, 1, X (unknown) and Z (high impedance). Uninitialized or conflicting signals become X.
Gate-level simulation
Simulation of the post-synthesis or post-layout netlist, optionally with delay information, to catch problems invisible at RTL.
Generate statement
A Verilog construct that expands hardware descriptions by loops or conditions, used for example to instantiate a module repeatedly in a regular pattern.
Hardware description language (HDL)
A language for describing the structure and behavior of digital circuits, such as Verilog, SystemVerilog and VHDL, converted into circuits by logic synthesis.
Lint tool
A tool that checks HDL or other code against rules without simulation, flagging problem-prone constructs such as width mismatches and unintended latches.
Mealy machine
A state machine whose outputs depend on both the current state and the inputs. Input changes reach outputs within the same cycle, but so can input glitches. A Moore machine's outputs depend only on state.
Nonblocking assignment
Verilog's "<=" assignment, which evaluates every right-hand side at a time step before updating all left-hand sides together. It matches flip-flop behavior and is used for sequential logic.
One-hot encoding
State encoding that assigns one flip-flop per state with exactly one set at a time. Logic tends to be simple and fast, and it is common on FPGAs.
Reference model
A separate model, often in C, implementing the same function as the circuit. Its results for the same inputs serve as expected values for the design's outputs.
Regression testing
Re-running existing tests after each design change to confirm that previously working features still work.
Reset synchronizer
A circuit that applies an asynchronous reset immediately but aligns its release to the clock through two flip-flops before distributing it.
Sensitivity list
The list in @( ) after a Verilog always that names the signals triggering the block. Omissions in combinational logic make simulation and hardware disagree.
Synchronous reset
A reset sampled at clock edges to initialize flip-flops. Timing is easier to analyze, but it does not act while the clock is stopped.
SystemVerilog
An extension of Verilog adding design features such as the logic type and always_ff/always_comb, and verification features such as classes, constrained random and assertions.
Testbench
Simulation-only code that drives inputs to the design under test and checks its outputs. Since it is never synthesized, delays, display statements and similar can be used freely.
Unintended latch
A latch created by synthesis when a description intended as combinational logic fails to assign an output under some condition, so the value must be held.
Value change dump (VCD)
A standard file format recording signal value changes during simulation, viewed in waveform tools for debugging.
Verilog
A hardware description language widely used for digital design and verification, describing circuits as modules with behavior expressed through assign and always.
X-optimism
In HDL simulation, the tendency for conditions containing unknown X to resolve to definite values instead of propagating X, hiding bugs.

I/O Interfaces & BusesPractice questions →

128b/130b encoding
Line coding that sends 128 data bits with a 2-bit header, about 98.5% efficient, relying on scrambling for transitions. Used from PCI Express 3.0 onward.
8b/10b encoding
Line coding that sends 8 data bits as a 10-bit code with balanced 0s and 1s and short runs. It eases clock recovery but limits efficiency to 80%.
AXI
An on-chip bus from Arm's AMBA specifications that connects blocks in an SoC, with five independent channels and a VALID/READY handshake. Variants include AXI4-Lite and AXI4-Stream.
Bit error rate (BER)
The fraction of transmitted bits received incorrectly. High-speed serial links typically require values such as 10^-12 or lower.
Characteristic impedance
The ratio of voltage to current seen by a wave traveling along a transmission line, set by trace width and layer spacing. Changes along the path cause reflections.
Clock and data recovery (CDR)
A receiver circuit that extracts clock timing from transitions in the received data and decides each bit, removing the need for a dedicated clock wire.
Compute Express Link (CXL)
A standard on the PCI Express physical layer that enables coherent memory sharing between CPUs and accelerators or memory devices.
Credit-based flow control
A scheme in which the receiver advertises free buffer space as credits and the sender transmits only within them, so buffers never overflow.
Differential signaling
Signaling that sends opposite signals on two wires and represents 0 and 1 by their voltage difference. Noise picked up equally on both cancels, allowing fast transmission with small swings.
Eye diagram
A plot overlaying many bit periods of a waveform. The height of the central eye shows noise margin and its width shows timing margin.
Hot plugging
Connecting and disconnecting devices with power on while the OS automatically recognizes or detaches them. Used with USB, server SSDs and more.
Isochronous transfer
A USB transfer type that reserves bandwidth and sends at fixed intervals without retrying on error. Used for audio and video.
Message-signaled interrupts (MSI)
A scheme in which a device signals an interrupt by writing to a designated address. It needs no dedicated interrupt wire and lets one device have many interrupts.
Network-on-chip (NoC)
An on-chip interconnect of routers and links arranged as a network that carries data as packets, moving many transfers between blocks simultaneously.
NRZ
Non-return-to-zero: the most basic signaling scheme, sending 1 bit per symbol with two voltage levels.
PAM4
Four-level pulse amplitude modulation, sending 2 bits per symbol. It doubles the bit rate at the same symbol rate but reduces noise margin.
PCI Express
A high-speed serial expansion interface connecting the CPU with GPUs, SSDs, network cards and more, also written PCIe. Lanes are bundled for bandwidth, and speed roughly doubles each generation.
Retimer
A circuit that receives a high-speed serial signal, decides the bits and retransmits a clean signal. Inserted mid-channel to extend reach.
Root complex
The root of the PCI Express tree, connected to the CPU and memory, from which links fan out through switches to devices (endpoints).
SerDes
Serializer/deserializer: a circuit that turns parallel on-chip data into a fast serial bit stream for transmission and restores the parallel data at the receiver. It is the heart of high-speed serial physical layers.
Signal equalizer
A circuit that compensates for a channel attenuating high frequencies more, which smears the signal. Examples include receiver CTLE and DFE and transmitter de-emphasis.
Spread-spectrum clocking (SSC)
Slowly varying a clock's frequency slightly to spread electromagnetic emission energy across a wider band and lower its peaks.
USB Power Delivery
A specification in which source and sink negotiate voltage and current over USB, allowing up to 48 V and 240 W.

Performance AnalysisPractice questions →

Benchmark
A representative program run under fixed conditions to compare computer or program performance. Choosing ones close to actual use is important.
CPU time
The total time the CPU spent executing a program's instructions, excluding waits; with parallel execution it can exceed wall-clock time.
Cycles per instruction (CPI)
The average number of clock cycles per instruction, one factor in execution time = instruction count × CPI × cycle time, and the reciprocal of IPC.
Flame graph
A profiling visualization stacking call chains vertically, with each function's width showing its share of time.
Geometric mean
The nth root of the product of n values, used to average performance ratios because the comparison does not depend on the reference chosen.
Gustafson's law
The observation that when processors are added and the problem is enlarged, the parallel fraction grows, giving gains nearly proportional to processor count.
Harmonic mean
The reciprocal of the mean of reciprocals, used to average rates over equal amounts of work.
Little's law
The relationship that the average number in a system equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends there: L = λW.
Microbenchmark
A benchmark timing a tiny isolated operation; beware pitfalls such as the compiler optimizing the computation away.
Parallel efficiency
Parallel speedup divided by the number of processors, indicating how effectively the cores are used.
Performance counter
A hardware counter in the CPU that tallies events such as instructions executed, cache misses and branch mispredictions.
Probe effect
The phenomenon in which adding a measurement mechanism changes the behavior of the program being measured.
Profiler
A tool that measures which functions or parts of a program consume execution time, using sampling or instrumentation.
SPEC CPU
A standard CPU benchmark suite of many programs based on real applications, reporting ratios against a reference machine combined by geometric mean.
Speedup
Execution time before an improvement divided by the time after; a value of 2 means twice as fast.
STREAM benchmark
A benchmark that copies and adds large arrays to measure the memory bandwidth actually achievable.
Strong scaling
Parallel performance evaluation that fixes problem size and measures how much time drops as processors are added.
Tail latency
The slow end of the response-time distribution, such as the 99th percentile, which governs overall latency when a task waits on many components.
Throughput
The amount of work, requests or data processed per unit time, a different metric from latency, which describes how fast one task completes.
Top-down analysis
A method using performance counters to divide CPU issue slots into frontend bound, backend bound, bad speculation and retiring, narrowing down bottlenecks.
Wall-clock time
The actual elapsed time from start to finish, including I/O waits and time when other programs ran.
Weak scaling
Parallel performance evaluation that fixes problem size per processor while growing the total and checks whether time stays constant.

Hardware SecurityPractice questions →

Anti-rollback protection
A mechanism recording a minimum version number in unchangeable storage so software cannot be reverted to older vulnerable versions.
Cold boot attack
An attack reading DRAM contents that persist briefly after power-off to extract keys and other secrets; cooling extends persistence. Prevented by memory encryption.
Constant-time implementation
A way of writing cryptographic code so that branch directions, loop counts and memory addresses accessed do not depend on secret values.
Counterfeit chip
A used, defective or lower-grade chip relabeled and sold as genuine, leading to failures and safety problems.
eFuse
One-time-programmable storage written bit by bit by blowing thin wires with current, used for per-device keys, settings and anti-rollback counters.
Fault injection attack
An attack that deliberately makes a chip malfunction with voltage or clock glitches, lasers and the like to bypass authentication or infer keys.
Hardware Trojan
Malicious circuitry inserted during design or manufacturing to leak information or disable operation. It activates only under rare conditions, making it hard to detect.
Kernel page-table isolation (KPTI)
An OS countermeasure to Meltdown that removes most kernel memory from the page tables while user programs run.
Measured boot
A mechanism that cumulatively records hashes of each boot stage's software in a TPM or similar so the boot state can be verified later.
Meltdown
A vulnerability in some CPUs where kernel memory could be read speculatively before the permission exception took effect, with values inferred from cache traces. Mitigated by KPTI and other measures.
Physically unclonable function (PUF)
A mechanism using per-chip manufacturing variation to produce values unique to each chip and hard to clone, allowing keys to be regenerated without storing them.
Power analysis attack
A side-channel attack inferring secrets from a chip's power consumption traces. Differential power analysis (DPA), which processes many traces statistically, is especially powerful.
Remote attestation
A mechanism in which a device signs the state of its running software with an on-chip key and presents it to a remote server for trust verification.
Root of trust
The component unconditionally trusted as the starting point of security checks, such as immutable ROM boot code and a public key inside the chip.
Secure boot
A mechanism that verifies the digital signature of the next software at each boot stage and starts only authentic software, forming a chain of trust.
Secure enclave
A TEE approach in which part of an application runs in a region whose memory the CPU encrypts and protects, hidden even from the OS and hypervisor.
Side-channel attack
An attack inferring secrets such as keys from physical information other than the intended output, such as processing time, power consumption or electromagnetic emissions.
Spectre
A class of vulnerabilities, disclosed in 2018, that induces mispredicted speculative execution to read secret data and infers its value from traces left in the cache.
Speculative execution
A CPU technique that executes instructions ahead based on predictions, such as branch outcomes, before they are known. Wrong results are discarded, but traces such as cache state may remain.
Tamper resistance
The ability to withstand physical attacks such as opening a chip or probing it, protecting internal secrets, for example by erasing keys on detection.
Timing attack
A side-channel attack exploiting processing time that varies with secret values, prevented by constant-time implementation.
True random number generator (TRNG)
A circuit generating random numbers from unpredictable physical phenomena such as thermal noise or oscillator jitter, serving as the source for cryptographic keys.
Trusted execution environment (TEE)
A hardware-protected execution environment isolated even from the normal OS, used for critical work such as key handling and authentication, for example Arm TrustZone.
Trusted Platform Module (TPM)
A security chip or function that generates and stores keys, records measurements of booted software and attests to platform state.
TrustZone
Arm's processor security architecture, switching between secure and normal worlds and using bus signals to distinguish and restrict access to memory and peripherals.

Power ManagementPractice questions →

ACPI
Advanced Configuration and Power Interface: the standard mechanism by which an OS manages device power states, power saving and thermals.
Activity factor
The fraction of a circuit's signals that switch per clock cycle, the α in the dynamic power formula αCV²f. It can be reduced by clock gating and similar.
Adaptive voltage scaling (AVS)
A technique that measures each chip's speed and temperature on chip and approaches the minimum voltage at which it works, trimming wasted margin.
Break-even time
The idle duration needed for the energy saved in a power-saving state to exceed the energy spent entering and leaving it.
C-state
CPU idle states describing sleep depth. C0 is active; higher numbers use less power but take longer to wake.
Dynamic power
Power consumed in CMOS circuits charging and discharging capacitance when signals switch, expressed as αCV²f with activity factor α, capacitance C, voltage V and frequency f.
Energy-delay product (EDP)
A metric multiplying energy per operation by its duration, used to evaluate the balance between energy efficiency and speed.
Inrush current
The large transient current that flows to charge capacitance the instant power is connected to an unpowered circuit, which can sag the supply voltage.
Isolation cell
A cell placed at a boundary that passes a fixed value to running logic even when the outputs of a powered-off block are undefined.
Level shifter
A circuit that converts signal voltages between blocks running at different supply voltages to suit the receiving side.
Near-threshold computing
Operating with supply voltage near the transistor threshold, greatly cutting energy per operation at the cost of speed and greater sensitivity to variation.
P-state
Performance levels of a running CPU defined by frequency and voltage combinations, switched by DVFS to match load.
Power capping
Setting and enforcing an upper limit on server or CPU power, used in data centers to stay within power and cooling limits.
Power domain
A region of a chip sharing the same supply voltage and power on/off control, allowing voltage and power to be managed per block.
Power gating
A low-power technique that switches off the supply to unused blocks, eliminating nearly all their leakage. State saving and wake-up time must be considered.
Power management IC (PMIC)
An IC that generates multiple voltages from a battery or similar and manages power-up sequencing and voltage changes.
Power sequencing
The specified order for applying and removing multiple supply voltages to a chip; violating it can cause malfunction or latch-up.
Power usage effectiveness (PUE)
Total data center power divided by IT equipment power; values closer to 1 indicate higher efficiency.
Race to idle
The strategy of finishing work quickly at high performance and entering deep sleep early, often advantageous when static power or other components' power is large.
Retention register
A flip-flop that keeps its state in a small always-powered storage element while the block is power-gated, allowing operation to resume where it left off.
Self-refresh
A low-power DRAM state in which the DRAM refreshes itself to retain contents without commands from the memory controller, used during system sleep.
Static power
Power consumed continuously by leakage currents through off transistors even when signals do not switch, also called leakage power. It rises steeply with temperature.
Thermal throttling
Control that lowers frequency and voltage when a chip gets too hot, reducing heat to protect it.
Tickless kernel
An OS kernel that stops the periodic timer tick on idle CPUs so they can sleep until the next work.
Turbo mode
Operation in which a CPU or similar automatically raises frequency above its rated value when power and thermal headroom allow.
