NPN vs PNP Transistor: Structure, Current Flow, Symbols and Uses Compared

An NPN transistor has a p-type base sandwiched between an n-type emitter and collector; a PNP transistor is the reverse, with an n-type base between a p-type emitter and collector. In NPN, current is carried mainly by electrons; in PNP, by holes, and every voltage and current direction is reversed. In silicon, electrons have higher mobility than holes, so for the same dimensions an NPN is faster and more easily gets a high current gain, which is why NPN is used more often.

When to use which

NPN
The first choice when in doubt. Commonly used to switch LEDs or relays on the GND side from a microcontroller pin, and in general-purpose amplifiers.
PNP
When you want to switch a load on the supply side, or build a current source that pushes current out from the supply.
Together
A matched NPN and PNP pair (complementary pair) can form a push-pull amplifier in which each handles one half-cycle, positive or negative. Class B produces crossover distortion, so it is common to use class AB with a small bias current.

FAQ

How do you tell NPN and PNP apart in a circuit symbol?

By the direction of the arrow on the emitter. The arrow shows the direction of forward current through the base–emitter PN junction (from p-type to n-type): it points out of the emitter in NPN and into the emitter in PNP.

Why is NPN used more often?

In silicon, electron mobility is about 2–3 times hole mobility, so with the same structure an NPN more easily achieves higher current gain and speed. Also, in GND-referenced circuits, an NPN low-side switch is easy to build.

Can I replace an NPN with a PNP?

Not as a drop-in replacement. All voltage and current directions are reversed, so the circuit must be redesigned, including supply polarity and load position. If you flip the whole circuit upside down between the supply side and the GND side, it can perform the same function.

How do you turn on a PNP transistor?

Make the base about 0.6–0.7 V lower than the emitter and draw current out of the base. In a high-side switch with the emitter tied to the supply, pulling the base toward GND through a resistor turns it on, and bringing the base to the same voltage as the emitter turns it off.

Check your understanding

Which bias condition is correct when an NPN transistor is used for amplification (operated in the active region)?

  1. ABase-emitter forward-biased, base-collector reverse-biased
  2. BBase-emitter reverse-biased, base-collector forward-biased
  3. CBoth base-emitter and base-collector forward-biased
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Base-emitter forward-biased, base-collector reverse-biased

In the active region the base-emitter junction is forward-biased to inject electrons from emitter into base, and the base-collector junction is reverse-biased so its strong field sweeps electrons that cross the base into the collector. Collector current is then set by base current (or VBE) and barely depends on collector voltage, which enables amplification. Both junctions forward is saturation (switch on); both reverse is cutoff (switch off).

In a PNP transistor, which carriers are injected from the emitter into the base and mainly carry the current?

  1. AElectrons
  2. BHoles
  3. CIons
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Holes

In a PNP transistor, holes are injected from the p-type emitter into the n-type base, cross it and are collected by the p-type collector; in an NPN, electrons play this role. Since electron mobility is about three times hole mobility, NPNs of the same structure are faster with higher gain and dominate. PNPs are used with NPNs in push-pull stages and as high-side switches on the positive supply, with voltages and currents reversed from NPN.

In the circuit symbol of an NPN transistor, which way does the arrow on the emitter point?

  1. AFrom the collector toward the emitter
  2. BOutward, from the base out through the emitter
  3. CInward, from outside the emitter toward the base
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Outward, from the base out through the emitter

The arrow in the symbol shows the direction of forward current through the base-emitter PN junction (p to n). In an NPN, current flows from the p-type base to the n-type emitter, so the arrow points outward; in a PNP it points inward. A common mnemonic is "NPN: Not Pointing iN." Since only the emitter carries the arrow, it also tells you which lead is the emitter and which the collector.

Practice Bipolar transistors questions

Key terms explained

Current gain (hFE)
The ratio of collector current to base current (IC/IB) in a bipolar transistor.
Forward-active region
The state with the base-emitter junction forward-biased and the base-collector junction reverse-biased. A collector current proportional to the base current flows, and it is used for amplification.
Saturation region
The state of a bipolar transistor in which both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions are forward-biased. The collector-emitter voltage falls to about 0.1-0.3 V, corresponding to a switch in the on state.
Emitter follower
A common-collector amplifier. Its voltage gain is about 1, but its high input impedance and low output impedance make it a buffer that drives the next stage without loading the signal.
Crossover distortion
Distortion in a class-B push-pull amplifier near 0 V, where neither transistor conducts during the handover between positive and negative half-cycles. Class-AB biasing with a small idle current reduces it.
