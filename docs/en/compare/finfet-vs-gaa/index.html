FinFET vs GAA Transistors: Key Differences

In a FinFET the gate wraps a vertical fin of silicon on three sides. In a gate-all-around (GAA) transistor the gate surrounds the channel on all sides, usually as stacked horizontal nanosheets. Wrapping the channel more completely gives better control of leakage at short gate lengths, which is why leading chipmakers are moving from FinFET to GAA at the 3 nm and 2 nm class.

AspectFinFETGAA (nanosheet)
Gate structureThe gate covers three sides of a vertical finThe gate surrounds the channel on all four sides
Channel shapeA thin vertical fin of siliconHorizontal nanosheets (or nanowires), stacked on top of each other
Electrostatic controlGood, much better than planar transistorsEven better, which reduces leakage and supports shorter gates and lower supply voltage
Tuning the channel widthIn discrete steps: the width comes from the number of fins, because fin height is fixedFlexible: the sheet width and the number of sheets can be chosen per transistor
Main manufacturing stepsFin patterning (often with SADP or SAQP), fin isolation, gate formationEpitaxy of alternating Si and SiGe layers, selective removal of SiGe to release the sheets, inner spacers
Process complexityMature and well understoodMore complex, with new steps and tighter control
Self-heatingA concern, because heat escapes poorly from the thin channelA concern as well, since the sheets are surrounded by insulators
First volume productionIntel 22 nm (Tri-Gate), shipping in 2012First volume production at the 3 nm class, around 2022; other major makers followed
Vendor namesTri-Gate (Intel), FinFET (TSMC, Samsung and others)RibbonFET (Intel), MBCFET (Samsung), nanosheet (TSMC)

Figures are typical orders of magnitude and vary by process generation and product.

When to use which

FinFET
The workhorse of logic chips from about 22 nm to 5 nm and still used widely at 3 nm. It is mature, with well-known design rules and high yield.
GAA
The next step for the smallest nodes, where FinFETs struggle to control leakage and where designers want to tune the width of each transistor.
Beyond
Research continues on stacked complementary FETs (CFET), which place nMOS and pMOS on top of each other.
