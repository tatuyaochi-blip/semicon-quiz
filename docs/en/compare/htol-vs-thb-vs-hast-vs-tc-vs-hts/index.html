HTOL vs THB vs HAST vs TC vs HTS: Reliability Test Conditions

Most semiconductor reliability tests are accelerated tests: they apply stresses such as temperature, voltage, humidity and temperature changes more strongly than in actual use to speed up failures, checking lifetime and failure rate in a short time. HTOL operates devices at high temperature under voltage and evaluates degradation of the chip itself, such as TDDB and electromigration. The temperature-humidity-bias test (THB) and HAST are moisture-resistance tests that use humidity and voltage to accelerate moisture-driven failures such as corrosion; HAST uses pressurized steam to perform a similar evaluation in less time. Temperature cycling repeats high and low temperatures to evaluate fatigue caused by differences in thermal expansion between materials, and the high-temperature storage test evaluates degradation driven by temperature alone, without voltage. Because each targets different failures, product qualification combines them.

When to use which

HTOL
When you want to confirm, for a new product or a process change, that the chip design and process meet the target lifetime and failure rate. Results are converted to use conditions with the Arrhenius model and similar models, but they change greatly with the assumed activation energy, so treat them as estimates.
Temperature-humidity-bias (THB)
When you want to confirm the moisture resistance of plastic-encapsulated packages at a temperature closer to real environments. It takes about 1,000 hours, but avoids failures peculiar to high temperatures such as those of HAST.
HAST
When you want to finish moisture-resistance evaluation in a short period. For example, a test of about 96 hours at 130 °C/85% RH is used in place of 1,000 hours of THB at 85 °C/85% RH. Which one to use is decided by the applicable standard or agreement with the customer.
Temperature cycling
When strain from differences in thermal expansion is a concern, at solder joints, wire bonds or the interface between mold compound and die. It is important for applications with large temperature changes, such as automotive, and for products whose large dies or packages experience large strain.
High-temperature storage (HTS)
When you want to check degradation driven by temperature alone, such as intermetallic growth at wire-bond joints or data retention in nonvolatile memory, separately from the effects of voltage.
Combining them in qualification
Because different stresses act on different kinds of failure, product qualification combines these tests, generally running them in parallel on separate samples for each test. Before humidity tests (THB, HAST) and temperature cycling, preconditioning is often applied, soaking the parts in moisture and then applying the same heat as reflow, so they are evaluated in the state they would be in after mounting. For every test, electrical characteristics are measured during and after the test to judge pass or fail.

FAQ

What is the difference between THB and HAST?

Both apply voltage at about 85% relative humidity and evaluate corrosion and increased leakage current caused by moisture. THB takes about 1,000 hours at 85 °C and atmospheric pressure, while HAST speeds up moisture ingress with pressurized steam at about 110 to 130 °C, performing an equivalent evaluation in about 96 hours at 130 °C/85% RH, for example. HAST is faster, but because of its high temperature it can cause failures that never occur in actual use.

What is the difference between HTOL and burn-in?

Burn-in is a screen that applies short high-temperature, high-voltage stress to all or most of the products to be shipped, removing units prone to early failure. HTOL is a qualification test that operates sampled units for about 1,000 hours to confirm that the product design and manufacturing process meet the target lifetime and failure rate; tested units are not shipped.

What is the difference between the high-temperature storage test and HTOL?

The high-temperature storage test simply holds products at high temperature, for example 150 °C, without voltage, evaluating degradation driven by temperature alone, such as wire-bond joint degradation and data retention in nonvolatile memory. HTOL operates devices under voltage at around 125 °C, so it also accelerates degradation driven by electric field or current, such as TDDB and electromigration.

Check your understanding

Which stress is applied in HTOL but not in a high-temperature storage test?

  1. AHigh temperature
  2. BHigh humidity
  3. CElectrical bias such as supply voltage
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Electrical bias such as supply voltage

The high-temperature storage test (HTS) holds products at high temperature, for example around 150 °C, for a long time without applying voltage and compares their electrical characteristics before and after. HTOL operates products at high temperature under supply voltage, so it also accelerates degradation driven by electric field or current, such as TDDB and electromigration, whereas the high-temperature storage test isolates degradation driven by temperature alone. Typical targets are intermetallic growth and voiding at wire-bond joints, which raise resistance or cause the bond to lift, data retention in nonvolatile memory, and degradation of materials such as mold compound. The test that applies humidity and voltage together is the temperature-humidity-bias test (THB).

Which typical reliability test evaluates lifetime and failure rate by operating products at high temperature for a long time?

  1. ATemperature cycling test
  2. BHigh temperature operating life test (HTOL)
  3. CDrop test
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. High temperature operating life test (HTOL)

HTOL (high temperature operating life) testing runs products at around 125 °C at or slightly above their maximum operating voltage for long periods to observe failures. Automotive AEC-Q100 requires 77 units from each of three lots for 1000 hours with zero failures. Temperature and voltage acceleration allow field lifetime and failure rates to be estimated from short tests. Temperature cycling evaluates joint fatigue and drop testing evaluates shock resistance.

Which failures are mainly evaluated by temperature-humidity-bias tests (THB and HAST)?

  1. ASoft errors caused by cosmic rays
  2. BClock delay
  3. CElectrode corrosion and increased leakage current due to moisture ingress
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Electrode corrosion and increased leakage current due to moisture ingress

Applying bias in hot, humid conditions lets moisture and ionic impurities that penetrate the package corrode aluminum pads and drive ion migration, increasing leakage or causing opens. THB runs at 85 °C/85% RH for about 1,000 hours, while HAST uses pressurized steam around 130 °C to accelerate moisture ingress, evaluating humidity resistance in roughly 100 hours. Soft errors and clock delays are targets of other tests.

Key terms explained

HTOL (high-temperature operating life test)
A reliability test that operates products at high temperature under voltage for a long time to accelerate and evaluate chip lifetime and failure rate. A typical condition is 1000 hours at around 125 °C.
THB (temperature-humidity-bias test)
A reliability test that applies voltage in a hot, humid environment, typically 85 °C/85% RH, to evaluate corrosion and increased leakage caused by moisture entering the package. It is also called the 85/85 test.
HAST (highly accelerated stress test)
A test that applies bias in pressurized steam at high humidity and temperatures above 100 °C to evaluate moisture resistance quickly. For example, 96 hours at 130 °C/85% RH is treated as equivalent to 1000 hours of temperature-humidity-bias testing (THB) at 85 °C/85% RH.
Temperature cycling
A reliability test that repeatedly applies high and low temperatures to evaluate solder-joint fatigue and package cracking or delamination caused by differences in thermal expansion between materials.
High-temperature storage test (HTS)
A reliability test that keeps products at high temperature without applying voltage to evaluate degradation of wire-bond joints, data retention in nonvolatile memory and similar effects. It is also called HTS or HTSL.
Burn-in
A test that operates products under stress such as high temperature and high voltage to remove units prone to early failure before shipment.
Arrhenius model
A model in which reaction or degradation rates vary with temperature as exp(−Ea/kT). It is used to estimate lifetime at operating temperature from accelerated tests at higher temperatures.
Bathtub curve
A curve showing how the failure rate changes over time. It consists of the early-failure, random-failure and wear-out periods and is shaped like a bathtub.
AEC-Q100
The qualification standard for reliability testing of automotive integrated circuits, defined by the Automotive Electronics Council (AEC). It has operating temperature grades: Grade 1 is −40 to 125 °C and Grade 0 is −40 to 150 °C. Discrete semiconductors are covered by AEC-Q101 and passive components by AEC-Q200.
MSL (moisture sensitivity level)
A rating of how readily a surface-mount part absorbs moisture, defined by JEDEC's J-STD-020. It sets the floor life allowed between opening the moisture barrier bag and reflow: 168 hours at or below 30 °C/60% RH for MSL 3. Parts past their floor life are baked dry before mounting.
