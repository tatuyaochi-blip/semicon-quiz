Ripple-Carry vs Carry-Lookahead vs Carry-Save Adder: Delay, Area

All three are adders built from full adders, but they handle carries differently. A ripple-carry adder chains full adders so the carry passes from the lowest bit to the highest in turn, and the delay grows in proportion to the bit width n (O(n)). A carry-lookahead adder computes all carries in parallel from per-bit generate and propagate signals, and with a hierarchical lookahead the delay grows only as O(log n). A carry-save adder reduces three numbers to two, a sum word and a carry word, without propagating carries, so its delay is one full-adder delay regardless of width. Multipliers reduce partial products with trees of carry-save adders (such as Wallace trees) and add the final two numbers with a fast adder.

When to use which

Ripple-carry adder
Use it for narrow widths, or when area and power matter more than speed. Its simple, regular structure maps efficiently onto devices with dedicated fast carry paths, such as FPGAs.
Carry-lookahead adder
Use it when a single two-operand addition must finish quickly, as in CPU ALUs and adders on the critical path. The hierarchy and the choice of parallel-prefix structure trade stage count against area and wiring.
Carry-save adder
Use it when adding three or more numbers. It reduces the count without waiting for carries and does one final addition with a carry-lookahead or similar adder, so it pays off in multipliers that add many partial products and in MAC units that accumulate repeatedly.

FAQ

How big is the difference between O(n) and O(log n) in practice?

At 64 bits, the carry of a ripple-carry adder passes through 64 full adders, while a Kogge-Stone parallel-prefix adder computes carries in log₂64 = 6 stages. The delay per stage differs between circuits, so this is not a simple ratio, but the gap widens as the width grows, and a ripple-carry adder is too slow for 64-bit addition in a GHz-class processor.

How can the carry-save adder be checked with numbers?

Feed in the 4-bit values 5 (0101), 6 (0110) and 7 (0111). The word S made of each bit's sum is 0100 (4), and the word Cy made of each bit's carry shifted one bit left is 1110 (14). 4 + 14 = 18, which equals 5 + 6 + 7. Each bit is an independent full adder, so nothing waits for a carry.

How is the number of Wallace tree stages determined?

Each stage of carry-save adders turns three numbers into two, so the count shrinks to about two-thirds per stage. With h partial products, one stage leaves 2⌊h/3⌋ + (h mod 3); 8 take 4 stages, 16 take 6, 32 take 8 and 64 take 10 to reach two. The stage count grows roughly with the logarithm of the width, and adding the final two numbers with a fast adder completes the product.

Why isn't the full carry-lookahead equation used directly for wide adders?

Fully expanding the carry equations adds more terms for each higher bit and soon requires gates with too many inputs. Instead, groups of about 4 bits produce a group generate G and group propagate P, and the next level uses the same lookahead logic on them. Parallel-prefix adders generalize this idea.

What is the drawback of a ripple-carry adder made by chaining full adders?

  1. AIt cannot handle negative numbers
  2. BBecause the carry propagates from the lower bits in sequence, the delay grows in proportion to the number of bits
  3. CThe circuit size does not change even as the number of bits increases
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Because the carry propagates from the lower bits in sequence, the delay grows in proportion to the number of bits

In a ripple-carry adder, each full adder waits for the carry from below, so the carry ripples from the least to the most significant bit and delay grows linearly with width. At 64 bits that is 64 full-adder delays, too slow for GHz CPUs. Being small and simple, it is used where speed does not matter. Faster designs use carry-lookahead, carry-select and parallel-prefix (e.g., Kogge-Stone) adders.

Why is a carry-lookahead adder fast?

  1. AIt reduces the number of bits being added
  2. BIt doubles the clock frequency
  3. CIt computes the carry for each bit in parallel from the inputs without waiting for propagation from the lower bits
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. It computes the carry for each bit in parallel from the inputs without waiting for propagation from the lower bits

A carry-lookahead adder uses per-bit generate G = A·B (always produces a carry) and propagate P = A⊕B (passes a lower carry) to compute each carry directly from the inputs in parallel: C1 = G0 + P0·C0, C2 = G1 + P1·G0 + P1·P0·C0, and so on. Delay then grows only with the logarithm of the width. Because the expressions grow long, about 4-bit blocks are combined hierarchically.

In a carry-lookahead adder, which gives the definitions of each bit's generate signal g and propagate signal p (a and b are that bit's inputs) and the carry into the next bit, c(i+1)?

  1. Ag = a + b, p = a·b, c(i+1) = p + g·c(i)
  2. Bg = a·b, p = a ⊕ b, c(i+1) = g + p·c(i)
  3. Cg = a ⊕ b, p = a·b, c(i+1) = g·p·c(i)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. g = a·b, p = a ⊕ b, c(i+1) = g + p·c(i)

If a and b are both 1, a carry is produced regardless of the incoming carry, so the generate signal is g = a·b. If exactly one of them is 1, the incoming carry passes upward, so the propagate signal is p = a ⊕ b (OR is also used). Hence c(i+1) = g(i) + p(i)·c(i); expanding this lets every carry be computed in parallel with two-level logic from the g and p signals and the lowest carry alone. Formulas that swap generate and propagate do not compute correctly.

Key terms explained

Full adder
A circuit that adds two bits plus a carry-in from the lower digit and outputs a sum and a carry-out. A version without carry-in is a half adder. Chaining full adders makes a multi-bit adder.
Carry-lookahead adder
An adder that computes carries in parallel from each bit's generate signal g = a·b and propagate signal p = a ⊕ b, without waiting for carries to ripple. Delay stays small even for wide operands.
Carry-save adder
An adder that reduces three numbers to two, a sum and a carry, without propagating carries. Made of independent full adders per bit, its delay is constant, so multipliers use it to add many partial products.
Wallace tree
A multiplier structure that reduces many partial products in parallel with a tree of carry-save adders. Its depth grows only logarithmically with width, so it is used in fast multipliers; a final adder sums the last two numbers.
Booth's algorithm
A multiplication method that recodes the multiplier's bits, for example in pairs, into signed digits to reduce the number of partial products. Radix-4 Booth encoding roughly halves them.
Critical path
The path with the largest delay in a circuit, which sets the maximum clock frequency. In STA it is the path with the smallest slack, and speeding up a design starts by reducing its delay.
