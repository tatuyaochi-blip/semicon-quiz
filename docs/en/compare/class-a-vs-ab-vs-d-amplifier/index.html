Class A vs AB vs D Amplifiers: Key Differences Explained

An amplifier's class describes how its output transistors are operated. Class A keeps current flowing through the transistor over the whole cycle, so distortion is lowest, but it consumes power even with no signal, and its efficiency is 25% or 50% even in theory. Class AB is Class B, in which two transistors share the positive and negative half-cycles, with a small idle current added to suppress crossover distortion; its efficiency approaches Class B's theoretical maximum of about 78.5%. Class D switches the transistors on and off and filters pulses such as PWM back into the original signal, achieving high efficiency of around 90% at high output.

When to use which

Class A
When the output is small and low distortion is the top priority. Small-signal amplification, preamplifiers and driver stages that drive the following stage.
Class AB
When you want both low distortion and good efficiency. Audio power amplifiers, headphone amplifiers, and output stages of op-amps and buffers.
Class D
When you want long battery life, less heat or a smaller heat sink, or high output power. Speakers in smartphones and TVs, car audio.

FAQ

Why does Class A have two maximum efficiencies, 25% and 50%?

Because the load is connected differently. If the load resistor is connected in series with the transistor, DC current also flows through the load and wastes power, so the theoretical maximum is 25%. If the load is coupled through a transformer or choke, the DC power is not wasted in the load and the output voltage can swing up to twice the supply voltage, so the maximum is 50%.

What is crossover distortion?

In a Class B push-pull stage, when the signal passes near 0 there is a region where neither transistor is on, which distorts the output waveform. With bipolar transistors it occurs because no current flows until about 0.6 to 0.7 V is applied between base and emitter. Class AB removes this region by keeping a small idle current flowing.

Does the D in Class D stand for digital?

No. The name simply follows Class A, B and C and does not mean digital. Many Class D amplifiers work by comparing an analog input signal with a triangle wave in a comparator to convert it into PWM pulses.

Why is Class D so efficient?

Because the output transistors are used fully on or fully off. When on, the voltage across them is nearly zero, and when off, the current is nearly zero, so little power is turned into heat in the transistors. The remaining losses include conduction losses due to on-resistance, losses during switching transitions and the power needed to drive the gates.

Check your understanding

Compared with a class A amplifier, which is a characteristic of a class B push-pull amplifier?

  1. ALow efficiency but very low distortion
  2. BIt always conducts a large bias current
  3. CHigh power efficiency (theoretical maximum about 78.5%), but prone to crossover distortion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. High power efficiency (theoretical maximum about 78.5%), but prone to crossover distortion

Class A always draws a large bias current, giving low distortion but a theoretical maximum efficiency of only 25% with a resistive load (50% transformer-coupled), and it heats up even without a signal. Class B draws almost no current at idle, reaching about 78.5% (π/4), but suffers crossover distortion at the handover. In practice, class AB with a small bias current and the even more efficient class D are used.

Which distortion tends to occur in a class B push-pull amplifier in which NPN and PNP transistors handle the positive and negative half-cycles?

  1. AQuantization error
  2. BAliasing
  3. CCrossover distortion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Crossover distortion

In a class-B push-pull stage, near zero input neither transistor reaches its ~0.6 V VBE, so there is a dead zone where the waveform distorts at the handover—crossover distortion, most noticeable at low volume. Biasing the bases about 1.2 V apart with diodes or a VBE multiplier lets a small idle current flow (class AB) and reduces it. Most audio amplifier output stages are class AB.

Which value is closest to the maximum power efficiency of an ideal class B push-pull amplifier?

  1. AAbout 100%
  2. BAbout 78.5%
  3. CAbout 25%
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. About 78.5%

In class B, each transistor conducts for half a cycle and almost no current flows without a signal. At full sine-wave output the theoretical maximum efficiency is π/4 ≈ 78.5%, higher than class A, which always draws bias current (25% with a resistive load, 50% transformer-coupled). Where still higher efficiency is needed, class D uses the transistors as switches (over 90%) and is now standard in smartphones and flat-panel TVs.

Key terms explained

Crossover distortion
Distortion in a class-B push-pull amplifier near 0 V, where neither transistor conducts during the handover between positive and negative half-cycles. Class-AB biasing with a small idle current reduces it.
Pulse-width modulation (PWM)
A method that adjusts average voltage or power by varying the high time of an output while keeping the period constant. Used to dim LEDs and control motor speed.
Negative feedback
Returning part of the output to the input with opposite polarity. Gain drops, but it becomes stable, and distortion and bandwidth improve.
