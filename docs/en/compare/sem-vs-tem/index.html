SEM vs TEM: Electron Microscopes in Semiconductor Analysis

Both microscopes use a beam of electrons instead of light, but they look at samples in different ways. A scanning electron microscope (SEM) scans a focused beam over the surface and builds an image from the electrons that come back, so it shows the shape of the surface. A transmission electron microscope (TEM) sends electrons through an extremely thin slice, so it can show the inside of the structure down to individual atoms. SEM is fast and easy; TEM is far more powerful but slow and demanding.

AspectSEMTEM
PrincipleA focused beam scans the surface; secondary or backscattered electrons form the imageElectrons pass through a very thin sample; transmitted and diffracted electrons form the image
What it showsSurface shape and composition contrastInternal structure, crystal lattice and atomic-scale interfaces
ResolutionAbout 1 nm at best; typically a few nanometers or moreBelow 0.1 nm: individual atoms can be seen
Sample preparationLittle: bulk samples can be used (insulators need a conductive coating)Very demanding: the sample must be thinner than about 100 nm, often cut by focused ion beam (FIB)
Beam energyAbout 0.5 to 30 kVAbout 80 to 300 kV
SpeedFast: minutes per sampleSlow: hours, including sample preparation
CostLowerHigher
Typical usesLine-width measurement (CD-SEM), defect review, cross-section views of devicesGate-oxide thickness, thin-film stacks, crystal defects and interface structure

Figures are typical orders of magnitude and vary by process generation and product.

When to use which

SEM
For routine inspection and measurement, where speed matters, such as checking patterns and finding defects.
TEM
When you need to see inside a structure at the atomic scale, such as the thickness of a gate dielectric a few atoms thick.
Together
In failure analysis, SEM and FIB find and expose the problem spot, and a thin slice is then cut out for TEM.
