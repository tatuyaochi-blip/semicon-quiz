Reticle vs Photomask: Terminology, Reduction Ratio, Size and Pellicles Compared

Photomask is the general word for the master plate that carries a circuit pattern for exposure. A reticle is one kind of photomask: originally, a plate that holds the pattern for only part of the wafer (one or a few chips) and is exposed again and again as a stepper or scanner moves across the wafer. Today's production exposure tools shrink the pattern to 1/4 when projecting it, so the pattern on a reticle is four times its size on the wafer. A 1x photomask used in a mask aligner, by contrast, carries the pattern of the whole wafer at the same size and exposes the whole wafer at once. In production, people routinely call reticles "masks" as well, so the two words are often used interchangeably.

When to use which

Reticle
For volume production of fine patterns on steppers and scanners. Drawing the pattern at 4x makes the plate easier to make and shrinks the effect of its small errors.
1x photomask
For micrometer-scale features when tool and mask cost must stay low; a mask aligner exposes the whole wafer at once.
Choosing the word
For fine layers in production, reticle and photomask (mask) mean practically the same thing. When the distinction matters, call the reduction-projection plate a reticle and the 1x whole-wafer plate a mask.

FAQ

Are a reticle and a photomask the same thing?

In the broad sense, yes: a reticle is a kind of photomask. Originally, a plate holding the whole-wafer pattern at 1x was called a mask, and a plate holding the pattern for part of the wafer, exposed repeatedly in a stepper, was called a reticle. Because nearly all production exposure now uses reduction projection, reticles are usually just called masks too.

Why is the pattern on a reticle four times larger than on the wafer?

Because the exposure tool shrinks the pattern to 1/4 through its projection optics. Lines can be drawn four times wider on the plate, which makes it easier to make, and dimensional errors or small particles on the plate have less effect on the wafer. The trade-off is that one shot covers only about 26 mm × 33 mm on the wafer.

Why do 1x photomasks not use pellicles?

In contact printing the mask touches the resist, and in proximity printing the gap between mask and wafer is very small, so there is no room for a pellicle, which is a film mounted a few millimeters away from the mask. Such masks are inspected and cleaned regularly instead.

How many chips fit on one reticle?

It depends on the chip size. As many chips as fit in one shot (about 26 mm × 33 mm on the wafer) are placed on it: many small chips, or a single very large one. A chip larger than one shot cannot be exposed as it is.

Check your understanding

What is the typical reduction ratio when today's exposure tools (scanners) project the mask pattern onto the wafer?

  1. A1:1
  2. B1/4
  3. C1/100
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 1/4

Modern scanners project the mask pattern onto the wafer at 4× reduction. Mask features can therefore be four times larger, easing fabrication, and mask defects and dimensional errors shrink by four on the wafer. The standard mask is a 6-inch (152 mm) square quartz plate that holds a 4× image of the 26 × 33 mm field. High-NA EUV combines 4× and 8× reduction in different directions.

Which equipment is mainly used to write the patterns of photomasks (reticles)?

  1. AElectron-beam writers
  2. BSteppers
  3. CCMP tools
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Electron-beam writers

Photomask patterns are written by electron-beam writers that expose resist on the mask blank directly with a focused or rectangular shaped beam (variable shaped beam). They need no mask and resolve extremely fine features, but writing serially can take over ten hours per mask, so production transfers patterns in bulk through masks. Advanced masks use multi-beam writers with hundreds of thousands of beamlets to write OPC-heavy and curvilinear patterns quickly.

What is the role of the thin membrane (pellicle) stretched over a photomask at a fixed distance?

  1. ATo double the resolution
  2. BTo increase the exposure light
  3. CTo keep particles from landing directly on the mask, so that particles on the pellicle are out of focus and unlikely to print
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. To keep particles from landing directly on the mask, so that particles on the pellicle are out of focus and unlikely to print

A particle on a mask prints as a defect in every field of every wafer exposed with it. A pellicle is a transparent film under 1 µm thick stretched on a frame about 6 mm above the mask; particles landing on it are far out of focus and do not print. For EUV, ultrathin films tens of nm thick—polysilicon or carbon nanotubes—transmitting about 90% of EUV light and withstanding high temperatures have been developed.

Key terms explained

Photomask (reticle)
The master pattern used for exposure, typically a light-blocking pattern on a quartz substrate (reflective for EUV). For reduction scanners it is made four times larger than the pattern on the wafer.
Pellicle
A thin membrane stretched a short distance above a photomask. It keeps particles off the mask surface, and particles landing on the pellicle are out of focus and do not print.
Scanner
An exposure tool that moves the mask and wafer in sync under a slit of light. It is the mainstream production lithography tool and generally projects the mask pattern at 1/4 size.
EUV lithography
Lithography using extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nm. It uses reflective optics and a vacuum environment.
Photoresist
A light-sensitive material whose solubility in developer changes when exposed. In positive resist the exposed areas dissolve; in negative resist they remain.
