Thermal Diffusion vs Ion Implantation: Doping Methods Compared

Both methods put dopant atoms into silicon to control its electrical behavior. In thermal diffusion, dopants move in from a source at high temperature. In ion implantation, dopant ions are accelerated and shot into the wafer. Implantation gives far better control over how much dopant goes in and where, so it replaced diffusion for almost all doping in modern ICs, although it needs an annealing step to repair the crystal.

AspectThermal diffusionIon implantation
PrincipleDopant atoms diffuse into hot silicon (about 900 to 1,100 °C) from a gas, solid or film source, driven by the concentration gradientDopant ions are accelerated by an electric field (keV to MeV) and shot into the wafer
Dose controlHarder: depends on surface concentration, temperature and timePrecise: the dose is measured as ion current
Depth profileHighest concentration at the surface, falling with depthPeak below the surface at a depth set by the ion energy
DirectionIsotropic: also spreads sideways under the maskAlmost directional; a photoresist mask is enough
TemperatureHigh throughoutNear room temperature during implantation, but annealing at high temperature is needed afterwards
Crystal damageLittleDamages the lattice; annealing repairs it and activates the dopants
Shallow junctionsDifficult at small dimensionsWell suited: low energy places dopants near the surface
Equipment costLow: a simple furnaceHigh: an ion implanter is a large, expensive tool
Typical usesDrive-in of deep wells, solar-cell emitters, older processesSources and drains, wells, threshold-voltage adjustment in nearly all modern ICs

Figures are typical orders of magnitude and vary by process generation and product.

When to use which

Thermal diffusion
When the junction is deep, the structure is simple and cost matters, as in some solar cells and power devices.
Ion implantation
When you need precise dose, depth and position, which is the case for transistors in logic and memory.
Together
Implantation puts the dopants in; a high-temperature step then repairs the crystal, activates the dopants and drives them in a little further by diffusion.

FAQ

Why did ion implantation replace diffusion?

Implantation controls dose and depth precisely, works near room temperature with a simple photoresist mask and can place dopants in very shallow layers.

Why is annealing needed after ion implantation?

Implantation damages the crystal lattice. Annealing repairs the damage and moves the dopants into lattice sites where they become electrically active.

What is channeling?

Channeling is when implanted ions travel unexpectedly deep along gaps in the crystal. Tilting the wafer by a few degrees, or implanting through an amorphous layer, reduces it.

Is thermal diffusion still used?

Yes, for deep junctions and simple structures where cost matters, such as some solar cells and power devices, and to drive dopants deeper after implantation.

Check your understanding

What is the main advantage of ion implantation over thermal diffusion?

  1. AIt can be done at low temperature, with precise control of dose and depth
  2. BIt allows long processing at high temperature
  3. CIt causes no crystal damage at all
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. It can be done at low temperature, with precise control of dose and depth

Ion implantation runs near room temperature, so photoresist can serve as the mask, and dose is controlled to about ±1% by integrating beam current. Depth is set independently through energy, and doses spanning 10¹¹ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻² are accessible. Thermal diffusion ties surface concentration to depth and, being hot, required oxide masks. Implantation does damage the crystal, however, so a post-implant anneal is required.

What is the main purpose of annealing (heat treatment) after ion implantation?

  1. ATo evaporate and remove the implanted dopants
  2. BTo repair crystal damage and place dopants on lattice sites to activate them electrically
  3. CTo grow a thick oxide on the wafer surface
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. To repair crystal damage and place dopants on lattice sites to activate them electrically

Implanted ions disrupt the silicon lattice and mostly sit at interstitial sites where they do not act as donors or acceptors, so they are largely electrically inactive right after implantation. Annealing repairs the damage and moves dopants onto lattice sites, activating them. To avoid excessive diffusion, lamp-based RTA lasting seconds or flash-lamp and laser anneals lasting milliseconds or less are used.

Which is a common way to suppress channeling (ions traveling too deep through gaps in the crystal) in ion implantation?

  1. AUsing the maximum acceleration energy
  2. BCleaning the wafer after implantation
  3. CTilting the wafer a few degrees from the crystal axis during implantation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Tilting the wafer a few degrees from the crystal axis during implantation

Ions entering along crystal rows travel through open channels between atomic columns with few collisions and penetrate deeper than intended—channeling. It is suppressed by tilting the wafer about 7° from the crystal axis with a twist offset, implanting through a thin screen oxide that randomizes directions, or pre-amorphizing the surface. This trims the deep tail of the profile, giving shallower junctions.

Key terms explained

Ion implantation
A technique that ionizes impurities, accelerates them and implants them into the wafer. The dose and depth can be controlled precisely.
Thermal diffusion
A doping method that diffuses impurity atoms into a semiconductor at high temperature. They spread from regions of high concentration to low.
Transient enhanced diffusion (TED)
A temporary speed-up of boron and other dopant diffusion early in post-implant annealing, caused by excess silicon interstitials from the implant. It hinders the formation of shallow junctions.
