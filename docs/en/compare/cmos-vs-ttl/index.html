CMOS vs TTL Logic: Differences Explained

TTL (transistor-transistor logic) builds gates from bipolar transistors and was the standard digital family from the 1960s. CMOS builds gates from complementary MOS transistors and has since replaced it almost everywhere. The big difference is power: a CMOS gate draws almost no current when it is not switching, while a TTL gate draws current all the time. CMOS also tolerates a wide supply range, has larger noise margins and can be packed by the billions on a chip.

AspectTTLCMOS
TransistorsBipolar transistors, with multi-emitter input transistorsComplementary nMOS and pMOS transistors
Static powerHigh: current flows even when idleVery low: only leakage current
Supply voltageFixed 5 V (about plus or minus 5 percent)Wide range, from about 1 V to 15 V depending on the family
Logic levelsLow up to about 0.8 V and high from about 2.0 VAbout 30 percent and 70 percent of the supply voltage
Noise marginSmall (about 0.4 V)Large (about 30 percent of the supply)
Output swingDoes not reach the supply rails fullySwings fully between ground and the supply
InputsDraw current; an unconnected input floats highDraw almost no current, but unused inputs must be tied to a level
SpeedFast in its day (74S, 74F families)Equal or faster in modern families
Integration densityLow, because of its power drawVery high: the basis of VLSI
Typical usesLegacy systems, education and old equipmentNearly all modern digital chips, including processors and memories

Figures are typical orders of magnitude and vary by process generation and product.

When to use which

TTL
Mostly found in legacy designs and when repairing old equipment.
CMOS
The choice for essentially every new digital design.
Mixed
Some CMOS families (such as 74HCT) accept TTL input levels, so they can connect to older TTL parts.
