SSD vs HDD: Differences in Speed, Durability and Cost

A hard disk drive (HDD) stores data magnetically on spinning platters read by a moving head. A solid-state drive (SSD) stores data in NAND flash chips with no moving parts. SSDs are many times faster, silent and more resistant to shock; HDDs cost much less per terabyte and reach larger capacities, so the two are often used together: an SSD for the system and active files, and an HDD for bulk storage.

AspectHDDSSD
How it stores dataMagnetic platters spinning under a moving read/write headNAND flash memory chips, with no moving parts
Sequential speedAbout 100 to 250 MB/sAbout 500 MB/s (SATA) up to 14 GB/s (fast NVMe)
Random access and latencySlow: milliseconds for the head to move and the disk to rotateFast: tens of microseconds, and hundreds of thousands of operations per second or more
Shock resistanceVulnerable to drops and vibration while runningResistant, because nothing moves
NoiseAudible spinning and seekingSilent
PowerHigher: a motor and head actuatorLower, especially at idle
Cost per terabyteLowHigher, but falling
CapacityTens of terabytes per driveConsumer drives of 1 to 8 TB; larger enterprise drives
WearMechanical wear; no write-cycle limitLimited program/erase cycles, managed by wear leveling and rated in TBW
Special maintenanceFragmentation slows it downNeeds TRIM and spare area; fragmentation hardly matters
Best forBulk storage, backups, network storage, surveillance and archivesOperating system drives, laptops, phones, gaming, databases

Figures are typical orders of magnitude and vary by process generation and product.

When to use which

SSD
For anything where speed and responsiveness matter: the system drive, applications, laptops and mobile devices.
HDD
For large amounts of rarely accessed data at the lowest cost per terabyte: backups, media libraries, archives.
Both
A common setup is a fast SSD for the system and active work, and a large HDD for storage.
