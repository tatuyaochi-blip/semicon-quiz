ALD vs CVD: Atomic Layer Deposition Compared

ALD (atomic layer deposition) is a special form of CVD in which the precursor gases are pulsed in one at a time, and each pulse reacts only until the surface is covered. The film therefore grows one atomic layer per cycle, with perfect control of thickness and excellent coverage of very deep structures. The price is speed: ALD is much slower than ordinary CVD, so it is used for the thinnest and most critical films.

AspectALDCVD
How it worksPrecursors are pulsed in turn, separated by purges. Each reaction is self-limiting, so one cycle adds about one atomic layerPrecursors flow in together and react continuously on the surface
Thickness controlAtomic-level, set by the number of cyclesSet by deposition time and rate, so less precise
ConformalityExcellent, even in very deep, narrow holes and trenchesGood, but deep structures can pinch off before they are filled
Deposition rateSlow: a fraction of a nanometer per cycleFast: many nanometers per minute or more
Uniformity and qualityVery uniform, dense and with few pinholesGood uniformity
TemperatureOften low (about 100 to 350 °C)Wide range, often higher (plasma CVD is lower)
ThroughputLow per wafer (batch tools help)Higher
Typical thicknessAbout 1 to 50 nmTens of nanometers to micrometers
Typical usesHigh-k gate dielectrics (such as HfO2), thin barriers, spacers, liners in 3D structuresThick insulating films, polysilicon, silicon nitride, tungsten plugs

Figures are typical orders of magnitude and vary by process generation and product.

When to use which

ALD
When the film must be extremely thin, perfectly uniform and must coat complex 3D structures: gate stacks of FinFETs and GAA transistors, DRAM capacitors.
CVD
When you need thicker films quickly and at lower cost, and the structure is not too demanding.
Trade-off
Engineers often combine them: ALD for the first thin conformal layer, then faster CVD to fill or thicken.
