MBE vs MOCVD vs HVPE: Epitaxial Growth Methods Compared by Vacuum, Growth Rate, Sources, Materials and Uses

MBE, MOCVD and HVPE all grow compound semiconductor crystals that follow the substrate crystal (epitaxial growth), but they deliver the sources differently and grow at very different speeds. MBE aims beams of evaporated source material at the substrate in ultra-high vacuum. It is slow, typically about 1 µm per hour, but controls thickness down to single atomic layers. MOCVD sends metal-organic and hydride gases to a heated substrate in a flowing gas at reduced to atmospheric pressure and grows at typically 1 to a few µm per hour. It can process many wafers at once and is the workhorse for LED and laser production. HVPE uses gallium chloride, made from gallium and hydrogen chloride, together with ammonia to grow thick GaN at tens to hundreds of µm per hour, and is used to make free-standing substrates.

When to use which

MBE
When thickness and composition must be set at the atomic-layer level: HEMTs and quantum structures whose performance depends on abrupt interfaces, and research on new materials.
MOCVD
When thin multilayer device structures must be produced on many wafers. It is the main production method for GaN LEDs, lasers and HEMTs.
HVPE
When thick GaN must be grown fast. Growing a thick layer on a foreign substrate and removing the substrate gives a free-standing GaN substrate; device layers are then grown on it by MOCVD or similar.

FAQ

Why is MBE done in ultra-high vacuum?

So that source atoms and molecules travel to the substrate as a beam without colliding with other gas molecules. It also keeps impurities out and allows RHEED, which uses an electron beam, to watch the surface during growth.

Why does HVPE grow faster than MOCVD?

The group III source can be supplied in large amounts as gallium chloride and reacts quickly on the hot substrate. Growth rates are typically tens to hundreds of µm per hour, tens of times faster than MOCVD, but making accurate layers a few nanometers thick is difficult.

Why is MOCVD used to make LEDs?

It can grow layers only a few nanometers thick, such as quantum wells, while switching composition, and it processes many wafers per run. MBE offers better control but grows slowly, and HVPE is fast but poor at controlling thin layers.

How are free-standing GaN substrates made?

Thick GaN, hundreds of micrometers to millimeters, is grown by HVPE on a foreign substrate such as sapphire, and the original substrate is removed. As the film thickens, threading dislocations meet and annihilate, so the dislocation density is lower closer to the surface.

Check your understanding

What is MOCVD (metal-organic chemical vapor deposition) mainly used for?

  1. AThermal oxidation of silicon
  2. BPlating copper wiring
  3. CEpitaxial growth of compound semiconductors such as GaN
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Epitaxial growth of compound semiconductors such as GaN

MOCVD reacts metal-organic precursors such as trimethylgallium with hydride gases such as ammonia on a heated substrate to grow compound-semiconductor crystals. Switching gases changes composition on a nanometer scale, and reactors process dozens of wafers per run, giving high throughput. It is widely used for GaN blue and white LEDs, laser diodes, RF and power devices, and solar cells.

Which is a characteristic of MBE (molecular beam epitaxy)?

  1. AIt grows thick films quickly at atmospheric pressure
  2. BSources are supplied to the substrate as molecular beams in ultra-high vacuum, allowing precise control of thickness and composition at the atomic-layer level
  3. CWafers are dipped into liquid sources to grow films
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Sources are supplied to the substrate as molecular beams in ultra-high vacuum, allowing precise control of thickness and composition at the atomic-layer level

MBE heats source materials in ultra-high vacuum (about 10⁻⁸ Pa) to form molecular (atomic) beams that strike the substrate and build the crystal. Shutters switch sources instantly, and RHEED monitors growth in situ, enabling atomic-layer control of thickness and composition. Growing only about 1 µm per hour, it is less productive than MOCVD but serves R&D, high-performance HEMTs and quantum devices.

In MBE, which technique observes growth in situ by directing an electron beam at the surface at a grazing angle and examining the diffraction pattern?

  1. ASIMS
  2. BRHEED (reflection high-energy electron diffraction)
  3. CXPS
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. RHEED (reflection high-energy electron diffraction)

RHEED directs a 10–30 keV electron beam at a grazing angle of about 1–3° onto the growing surface and displays the diffraction pattern on a phosphor screen. It reveals surface flatness and reconstruction, and because roughness cycles with each completed monolayer, the intensity oscillates, letting operators count layers in real time. It is uniquely suited to MBE, whose ultra-high vacuum keeps the beam from scattering off gas molecules.

Key terms explained

MBE (molecular beam epitaxy)
Epitaxial growth in ultra-high vacuum by supplying source materials to the substrate as molecular beams. It controls thickness and composition at the atomic-layer level and is used in compound-semiconductor research and quantum structures.
MOCVD (metal-organic chemical vapor deposition)
A method that grows crystals using metal-organic compounds and source gases such as ammonia. It is widely used to grow compound semiconductors such as GaN and GaAs.
HVPE (hydride vapor phase epitaxy)
Vapor-phase growth using gallium chloride, formed by reacting HCl with gallium, together with ammonia. Its very high growth rate is used to grow thick GaN crystals for free-standing substrates.
Epitaxial growth
A technique for growing a single-crystal thin film on a single-crystal substrate with the same crystal orientation as the substrate.
GaN (gallium nitride)
A III-V compound semiconductor with a band gap of about 3.4 eV. Used in blue LEDs and in high-frequency and power devices.
Quantum well
A structure in which a thin layer, a few nanometers thick, with a small band gap is sandwiched between layers with a larger band gap. It confines carriers to promote recombination and is used as the active layer of LEDs and lasers.
HEMT (high-electron-mobility transistor)
A transistor that uses the high-mobility two-dimensional electron gas formed at a heterojunction interface as its channel. It is used in high-frequency and high-power applications.
Threading dislocation
A dislocation that runs up through the grown layer to the surface. GaN on sapphire or silicon has high densities (about 10⁸-10⁹ cm⁻²), which degrade device performance and reliability.
