Four-Point Probe vs Hall Effect Measurement: Quantities and Uses

Both methods measure the electrical properties of a semiconductor layer, but they tell you different things. A four-point probe presses four probes in a line onto the wafer, passes current through the outer two and measures voltage across the inner two to get the sheet resistance. A Hall effect measurement puts contacts on a small cut sample and, besides the resistance, measures the Hall voltage that appears under a magnetic field. That reveals whether the carriers are electrons or holes, and separates their concentration from their mobility. The four-point probe is used for fast, simple process monitoring; Hall measurements are used to characterize the material itself.

When to use which

Four-point probe
For quick, routine checks of implantation, annealing and deposition. Changes in sheet resistance against a reference wafer reveal problems with dose, activation or metal thickness.
Hall effect
When you need carrier type, concentration and mobility separately, for example for epitaxial layers or new materials. Resistance alone cannot tell whether there are many carriers or whether they move easily.

FAQ

Why is a four-point probe unaffected by contact resistance?

It uses separate probes for current and voltage. Almost no current flows through the inner voltage probes, so there is no voltage drop across their contact resistance, and only the voltage in the layer itself is measured.

How does a Hall measurement give the mobility?

The Hall voltage gives the carrier concentration and the resistance measurement gives the conductivity. Since conductivity equals charge times carrier concentration times mobility, combining the two gives the mobility. This value is called the Hall mobility and can differ slightly from the drift mobility, depending on the scattering mechanisms.

What is the van der Pauw method?

A method that measures resistivity and the Hall coefficient of a sample of any shape, as long as it has uniform thickness and no holes, using four small contacts on its edge. Measurements are repeated with the current and voltage contacts swapped and then averaged.

Can a four-point probe measure implant dose?

Not directly. Sheet resistance depends on the amount of activated dopant and on mobility, so under the same anneal it falls as the dose rises. Dose and activation are therefore monitored by comparison with reference wafers or with a pre-established calibration.

Check your understanding

What is mainly measured by the four-point probe method?

  1. ASheet resistance
  2. BFilm stress
  3. CSurface roughness
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Sheet resistance

The four-point probe places four collinear needles on the sample, passes current through the outer pair and measures voltage across the inner pair. Since almost no current flows through the voltage probes, sheet resistance is measured without contact-resistance error. For a sufficiently large thin film, Rs = (π/ln2)×V/I ≈ 4.53×V/I. It is used for diffused layers and metal films. Stress is measured from wafer bow and roughness by AFM and similar tools.

What can be learned from a Hall effect measurement?

  1. AThe type of carrier (electron or hole) and the carrier density
  2. BThe size of the band gap
  3. CThe melting point of the crystal
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. The type of carrier (electron or hole) and the carrier density

When a magnetic field is applied perpendicular to a current-carrying sample, the Lorentz force pushes carriers to one side and a transverse Hall voltage appears. Its sign tells whether carriers are electrons or holes, and its magnitude (V_H = IB/qnt) gives the carrier density. Combined with resistivity it also gives mobility (μ = 1/qnρ), and together with the van der Pauw method it is widely used to characterize thin films. Band gap is measured by optical absorption and melting point by thermal analysis.

With electron density n, hole density p, electron mobility μn, hole mobility μp and elementary charge q, which expression gives the conductivity σ of a semiconductor?

  1. Aσ = q(nμn − pμp)
  2. Bσ = (nμn + pμp) / q
  3. Cσ = q(nμn + pμp)
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Electrons and holes move in opposite directions but also have opposite charges, so their currents add and the conductivity is the sum σ = q(nμn + pμp); resistivity is its inverse. In doped material the majority-carrier term alone is a good approximation, e.g. σ ≈ qnμn for n-type. In intrinsic silicon n = p = ni, so σ = q·ni(μn + μp), giving a high resistivity of about 2–3×10⁵ Ω·cm.

Practice Metrology and analysis questions

Key terms explained

Four-point probe method
A method that passes current through the outer two of four collinear needles and measures voltage across the inner two to find the sheet resistance of wafers and thin films.
Mobility
How easily carriers move when an electric field is applied. In silicon, electron mobility is higher than hole mobility.
Ion implantation
A technique that ionizes impurities, accelerates them and implants them into the wafer. The dose and depth can be controlled precisely.
Implant dose
The number of ions implanted per unit area in ion implantation, expressed in cm⁻². It is set by beam current and implant time.
Annealing
Heat treatment. Used to repair crystal damage after ion implantation and to activate dopants, among other purposes.
Ohmic contact
A metal-semiconductor contact with low resistance in both directions and a linear current-voltage relation. It is made by heavily doping the semiconductor surface so carriers can tunnel through the barrier.
Monitor wafer
A wafer run through a process separately from products to check equipment or measure film thickness and particles. Also called a test wafer, it is often stripped, repolished and reused as a reclaimed wafer.
