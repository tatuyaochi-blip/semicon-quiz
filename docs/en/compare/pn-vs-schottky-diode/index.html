PN Junction vs Schottky Diode: Key Differences Explained

A PN junction diode rectifies with a junction between p-type and n-type semiconductors, while a Schottky diode rectifies with a junction between a metal and a semiconductor. In a PN junction, minority carriers are injected and stored under forward bias, so a reverse recovery current flows when the diode is switched to reverse bias. A Schottky diode runs on majority carriers only, so it has almost no reverse recovery, and in silicon its forward voltage of about 0.2 to 0.4 V is also lower than the roughly 0.6 to 0.7 V of a PN junction. The trade-off is a larger reverse leakage current and, in silicon, difficulty reaching high breakdown voltages.

When to use which

PN junction diode
For rectification under high reverse voltage, or when leakage current must stay small even at high temperature. Used in mains rectifiers and high-voltage circuits.
Schottky diode
For rectifying large currents at low voltage, or for fast switching. Used for output rectification in switching power supplies, freewheeling diodes and protection against reverse battery connection.
Combined
For high-voltage, high-speed applications, SiC Schottky diodes or fast-recovery diodes with lifetime control are chosen. There are also JBS diodes that combine PN and Schottky junctions to balance leakage current against forward voltage.

FAQ

Why is the forward voltage of a Schottky diode low?

The thermionic emission current over the Schottky barrier has a saturation current orders of magnitude larger than the diffusion current of a PN junction, so the same current flows at a lower voltage. The barrier height depends on the metal: a lower barrier lowers the forward voltage but increases the reverse leakage current.

Why does a Schottky diode have almost no reverse recovery?

Current is carried by majority carriers only, and minority carriers are not injected and stored as in a PN junction. When the diode is switched to reverse bias, the only current is the one that charges and discharges the junction capacitance, so switching losses and noise can be kept small.

Why are there few high-voltage silicon Schottky diodes?

A higher breakdown voltage requires a thick, lightly doped drift layer, and because a Schottky diode has no conductivity modulation, that resistance adds directly to the forward voltage drop. Leakage current also grows, so silicon parts are generally limited to about 200 V. SiC, with its high critical electric field, reaches 600 V or more with a thin drift layer.

What should I watch for when using a Schottky diode at high temperature?

The reverse leakage current rises sharply with temperature. Heat from the leakage current can raise the temperature further and lead to thermal runaway, so check the high-temperature leakage in the datasheet and allow margin in heat dissipation and reverse voltage.

What is the forward voltage (the approximate voltage at which current starts to flow) of a typical silicon PN junction diode?

  1. AAbout 3 V
  2. BAbout 0.1 V
  3. CAbout 0.6–0.7 V
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. About 0.6–0.7 V

A silicon diode starts conducting significantly above about 0.6–0.7 V, the voltage at which a practical current (around mA) flows at room temperature; wider-bandgap materials need more. Schottky barrier diodes are about 0.2–0.4 V, SiC Schottky diodes about 1–1.5 V (SiC PN junctions about 2.5–3 V), and GaN blue LEDs about 3 V. Because rectification loss is forward voltage × current, a low VF is prized in high-current power supplies.

Which is a characteristic of a Schottky barrier diode?

  1. AIt is a diode made by joining two p-type semiconductors
  2. BIt uses a metal-semiconductor junction, with a low forward voltage and fast switching
  3. CIt emits strong light when forward current flows
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. It uses a metal-semiconductor junction, with a low forward voltage and fast switching

A Schottky barrier diode uses a metal-semiconductor (Schottky) junction and operates with majority carriers only. With no minority-carrier storage it has almost no reverse recovery and switches fast, and its forward voltage of about 0.2–0.4 V is lower than a PN junction's, so it is used for rectification in switching power supplies. Its weakness is larger reverse leakage that rises with temperature; silicon parts reach only about 200 V, while SiC versions above 1 kV are in practical use.

Why does a reverse current flow briefly (a reverse recovery time exists) when a PN junction diode is switched from forward to reverse?

  1. ABecause the metal electrode resistance is large
  2. BBecause minority carriers stored during forward conduction do not disappear immediately
  3. CBecause the package heats up
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Because minority carriers stored during forward conduction do not disappear immediately

While a diode conducts forward, injected minority carriers accumulate on both sides of the junction. After switching to reverse, current keeps flowing backward until those carriers are swept out or recombine. This interval is the reverse recovery time (trr), and the reverse recovery current causes loss and noise. Remedies include fast-recovery diodes with lifetime control, and Schottky or SiC diodes that do not rely on minority carriers.

Key terms explained

Schottky barrier diode
A diode that uses a metal-semiconductor junction (Schottky junction). It has a low forward voltage and switches fast because there is no minority-carrier storage.
Reverse recovery time
The time during which reverse current keeps flowing after a conducting diode is reverse-biased, until the stored minority carriers are removed. It causes switching loss and noise.
Forward bias
Applying a positive voltage to the p side and a negative voltage to the n side. The barrier drops and current flows readily. The opposite is reverse bias, under which almost no current flows.
Conductivity modulation
A large drop in the resistance of a lightly doped layer when many minority carriers are injected into it. PIN diodes and IGBTs use it to combine high breakdown voltage with low on-state voltage.
Work function
The energy difference between the vacuum level and the Fermi level: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from a material. It affects the threshold voltage of metal gates and the height of Schottky barriers.
