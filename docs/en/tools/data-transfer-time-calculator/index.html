The basics of transfer time

Transfer time is the data size in bits divided by the effective speed in bits per second. File sizes are normally given in bytes and line speeds in bits per second (bps), so first multiply the data size by 8 to convert it to bits. For example, sending a 100 MB file over a 100 Mbps line means sending 100 × 10⁶ × 8 = 8 × 10⁸ bits at 10⁸ bits per second, which takes 8 seconds. Mixing up bytes and bits makes the answer off by a factor of 8.

Transmission efficiency

In real communication, headers and control information are sent along with the data, and data with errors is sent again, so not all of the line speed is available for data. The share that can be used for data is the transmission efficiency, and the effective speed is the line speed × the transmission efficiency. In the example “1 GB over 100 Mbps at 80% efficiency”, 8 × 10⁹ bits are sent at 80 Mbps, which takes 100 seconds. With TCP/IP over Ethernet, the IP and TCP headers take 40 bytes of each 1500-byte packet, and the Ethernet header and inter-frame gap add more, so the efficiency is about 95%.

KB versus KiB

The k, M and G of communication speeds always step by 1000. The KB, MB and GB of data sizes, however, have been used both for steps of 1000 and for steps of 1024, so steps of 1024 are written KiB, MiB and GiB to tell them apart. 10 GiB is about 10.7 × 10⁹ bytes, so it takes about 7% longer than a calculation that treats it as 10 GB. Exam questions usually define the unit in the question, for example 1 MB = 10⁶ bytes, so follow that.

Latency and throughput

Transfer-time calculations often consider only the time needed to send the data out, but in practice propagation delay and time spent waiting for replies are added. If small pieces of data are sent one at a time and each waits for a reply, the round-trip time (RTT) dominates and a faster line hardly shortens the transfer. Sending large amounts of data together gives a throughput close to the line speed.

Practice networking questions

Roughly how long does it take to send a 100 MB file over a 100 Mbps link? Ignore overhead such as headers and latency, and take 1 MB = 10^6 bytes.

  1. AAbout 1 s
  2. BAbout 8 s
  3. CAbout 80 s
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. About 8 s

Link speeds are usually given in bits per second (bps) and file sizes in bytes (B), so first align the units. One byte is 8 bits, so 100 MB = 100 × 8 = 800 megabits. Sending this at 100 Mbps takes 800 ÷ 100 = 8 seconds. In practice it takes longer because of overhead such as headers and the waiting introduced by TCP's control mechanisms.

On 1 Gbps Ethernet, TCP data is sent continuously with IP packets filling the 1500-byte MTU. Taking the IPv4 header as 20 bytes, the TCP header as 20 bytes, the Ethernet header and FCS together as 18 bytes, and the preamble and inter-frame gap together as 20 bytes, which is closest to the upper limit on application-data throughput?

  1. AAbout 949 Mbps
  2. BAbout 973 Mbps
  3. CAbout 1000 Mbps
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. About 949 Mbps

Each frame carries 1500 − 20 − 20 = 1460 bytes of application data. On the wire, however, each frame takes the time of 1500 + 18 + 20 = 1538 bytes. The efficiency is therefore 1460 ÷ 1538 ≈ 0.949, and the throughput limit is 1000 × 0.949 ≈ 949 Mbps. Using the TCP timestamp option adds 12 bytes to the header and lowers efficiency slightly further. The smaller the packets, the larger the share of headers and other overhead, and the lower the efficiency.

Over a link with an RTT of 50 ms and a bandwidth of 100 Mbps, a request that returns a 10 KB response is repeated 100 times, each sent only after the previous response has been fully received. Which is closest to the total time? Take the time per request as the RTT plus the response transfer time (data size ÷ bandwidth), 1 KB = 1000 bytes, and ignore server processing time.

  1. AAbout 0.08 s
  2. BAbout 2.6 s
  3. CAbout 5.1 s
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. About 5.1 s

Each request takes one round trip, 50 ms, from sending the request until the response starts to arrive, plus the time to receive 10 KB = 80000 bits at 100 Mbps: 80000 ÷ (100 × 10^6) = 0.8 ms. The total is (50 + 0.8) × 100 = 5080 ms ≈ 5.1 s, almost all of it waiting for round trips. Even with ten times the bandwidth, 1 Gbps, it only drops to (50 + 0.08) × 100 ≈ 5.0 s. Light in optical fiber travels about 200 km per millisecond, so distance sets a lower bound on RTT. To speed up this kind of processing, batch requests, send several requests in parallel without waiting for responses, or place servers close to users.

FAQ

How long does a 1 GB file take over a 1 Gbps line?

With 100% transmission efficiency and 1 GB = 10⁹ bytes, it takes 8 × 10⁹ bits ÷ 10⁹ bits per second = 8 seconds. At 80% efficiency it takes 10 seconds.

What is the difference between Mbps and MB/s?

Mbps means megabits per second and MB/s means megabytes per second, so 1 MB/s = 8 Mbps. Download speeds are often shown in MB/s and contracted line speeds in Mbps, so be careful.

What transmission efficiency should I assume?

In exams it is given in the question. In practice, TCP over a wired LAN often exceeds 90%, while on wireless LANs it can drop a lot depending on signal conditions and congestion.

How does this appear on exams?

IT certification exams such as Japan's Fundamental Information Technology Engineer Examination often ask for the transfer time from the data size, line speed and transmission efficiency, or for the line speed needed to finish within a given time. Watch the byte-to-bit conversion and the unit prefixes (k, M, G).
