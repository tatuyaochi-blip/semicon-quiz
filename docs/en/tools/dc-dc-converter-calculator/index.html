Duty cycle and output voltage

A switching DC-DC converter turns its switch on and off every period T, and the fraction of time it is on, the duty cycle D, sets the output voltage. In steady state the average inductor voltage over a cycle is zero, so in ideal continuous conduction mode Vout = D·Vin for a buck, Vout = Vin/(1 − D) for a boost and |Vout| = D·Vin/(1 − D) for a buck-boost. For the 12 V to 3.3 V buck example, D = 3.3/12 = 27.5%. In practice D is slightly larger to make up for switch and diode voltage drops and losses.

Inductor ripple current and choosing L

While the switch is on, a constant voltage is applied across the inductor, so its current rises linearly. In a buck converter the peak-to-peak ripple is ΔI = (Vin − Vout)·D/(L·f). In the example it is (12 − 3.3) × 0.275/(4.7 µH × 500 kHz) ≈ 1.02 A, about 51% of the 2 A average. A ripple of about 20–40% of the average current is a common target, and the tool also shows the inductance for 30%. Choose an inductor whose saturation current exceeds the peak current, the average plus half the ripple.

Continuous and discontinuous conduction

As the load gets lighter, the average current falls; once the bottom of the ripple reaches zero, the inductor current stays at zero for part of each cycle. This is discontinuous conduction mode (DCM), and for a buck the boundary output current is ΔI/2. In DCM the duty cycle needed for a given output voltage depends on the load and is smaller than in CCM. Reducing the example buck's load to 0.2 A takes it below the boundary of about 0.51 A, so it enters DCM with D of about 17%. A synchronous converter can carry negative inductor current, so it can also be run in CCM even at light load.

Output voltage ripple and the capacitor

In a buck converter the inductor ripple current flows into the output capacitor, so the capacitive part of the output ripple is ΔV = ΔI/(8·f·C). In the example it is 1.02 A/(8 × 500 kHz × 22 µF) ≈ 11.6 mV. In boost and buck-boost converters the capacitor alone supplies the output current while the switch is on, so ΔV = Iout·D/(f·C) and the ripple tends to be larger. The capacitor's equivalent series resistance (ESR) times the current swing adds to this, which is why low-ESR ceramic capacitors are common. Some ceramic capacitors lose capacitance under DC bias, so check the effective capacitance.

Practice power electronics questions

For an ideal step-up chopper (boost converter) with an input voltage of 5 V and a switch duty cycle of 0.6, which of the following is the output voltage in continuous conduction mode?

  1. A3 V
  2. B8 V
  3. C12.5 V
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. 12.5 V

The output voltage of an ideal boost converter is Vout = Vin/(1 − D). 5 V ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 5 ÷ 0.4 = 12.5 V. The formula follows from the balance of volt-seconds across the inductor, with Vin applied while the switch is on and (Vout − Vin) while it is off: Vin × D = (Vout − Vin) × (1 − D). The 3 V of D × Vin is the formula of the buck converter.

For an ideal buck-boost chopper (inverting buck-boost converter) with an input voltage of 12 V and a duty cycle of 0.6, which of the following is the magnitude of the output voltage in continuous conduction mode?

  1. A7.2 V
  2. B18 V
  3. C30 V
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 18 V

In an inverting buck-boost converter, energy from the input is stored in the inductor while the switch is on and released to the output through the diode while it is off. From the volt-second balance of the inductor, Vin × D = Vout × (1 − D), the magnitude of the output voltage is Vout = Vin × D/(1 − D) = 12 × 0.6/0.4 = 18 V. The output polarity is opposite to that of the input (negative with respect to ground). It works as a step-down converter for D < 0.5 and a step-up converter for D > 0.5, and at D = 0.5 the output magnitude equals the input.

For an ideal buck converter with an input voltage of 12 V, an output voltage of 3 V, an inductance of 15 µH and a switching frequency of 300 kHz, which of the following is the peak-to-peak inductor ripple current in continuous conduction mode?

  1. A0.25 A
  2. B0.5 A
  3. C2 A
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. 0.5 A

The duty cycle is D = 3/12 = 0.25, so the on-time is D/f ≈ 0.833 µs. During the on-time 12 − 3 = 9 V appears across the inductor, so the current rises by ΔI = (Vin − Vout) × D/(L × f) = 9 × 0.25/(15 µH × 300 kHz) = 2.25/4.5 = 0.5 A. During the off-time it falls by Vout × (1 − D)/(L × f) = 3 × 0.75/4.5 = 0.5 A, confirming that it returns to its starting value every period. Doubling either the inductance or the frequency halves the ripple.

In an ideal buck converter (freewheeling through a diode) with an input voltage of 12 V, an output voltage of 3 V, an inductance of 15 µH and a switching frequency of 300 kHz, the load current is gradually reduced. Which of the following is the minimum output current that keeps continuous conduction mode (the boundary with discontinuous conduction mode)?

  1. A0.25 A
  2. B0.5 A
  3. C1 A
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 0.25 A

The inductor current is a triangular wave around its average (the output current) with a peak-to-peak ripple of ΔI = 9 × 0.25/(15 µH × 300 kHz) = 0.5 A. Its valley is "output current − ΔI/2", so when the output current falls below ΔI/2 = 0.25 A, the valley reaches zero and the converter enters discontinuous conduction mode. For the 3 V output this happens when the load resistance exceeds 12 Ω (a lighter load). In a synchronous buck converter the inductor current can also flow in the negative direction, so it can keep operating in continuous conduction mode even at light load.

Assume that the entire AC component of the inductor ripple current (0.6 A peak to peak) flows into the output capacitor of a buck converter. With a switching frequency of 300 kHz and an output capacitance of 25 µF (ignore the ESR), which of the following is the peak-to-peak output voltage ripple?

  1. A10 mV
  2. B40 mV
  3. C80 mV
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 10 mV

The current into the capacitor is a triangular wave with an amplitude of ΔI/2. The charge that enters the capacitor during the half period T/2 when this current is positive is the area of a triangle, (1/2) × (T/2) × (ΔI/2) = ΔI × T/8. The voltage ripple is therefore ΔV = ΔI/(8fC) = 0.6/(8 × 300 kHz × 25 µF) = 10 mV. In a real capacitor the voltage ΔI × ESR from the ESR is added, which is why ceramic capacitors with low ESR are often used.

FAQ

What is the duty cycle formula for a buck converter?

In ideal continuous conduction mode, D = Vout/Vin; for 12 V to 3.3 V it is 27.5%. For a boost converter D = 1 − Vin/Vout, and for an inverting buck-boost D = |Vout|/(Vin + |Vout|). Real circuits need a slightly larger duty cycle to cover losses.

How much inductor ripple current should I design for?

About 20–40% of the average current is a common target. Lower ripple needs a larger inductor and slows the response to load changes; higher ripple increases the peak current, output voltage ripple and core loss. Choose an inductor with a saturation current comfortably above the peak current.

Is discontinuous conduction mode bad?

Not necessarily. Some controllers deliberately switch to DCM or pulse skipping at light load to keep efficiency up. However, the relation between duty cycle and output changes with load, and so do the control dynamics, so the design must be stable in both modes.
