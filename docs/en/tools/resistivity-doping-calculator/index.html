Resistivity and doping formulas

The conductivity of a semiconductor is the product of how many carriers there are and how easily they move: σ = q(nμn + pμp). In n-type material with fully ionized dopants, n ≈ ND and the minority-carrier contribution is negligible, so the resistivity is ρ = 1/(q·ND·μn). Likewise for p-type, ρ = 1/(q·NA·μp). Because the mobility μ itself depends on concentration, however, resistivity is not simply inversely proportional to doping. This tool calculates mobility with the Caughey–Thomas form μ(N) = μmin + (μmax − μmin)/(1 + (N/Nref)^α), and when converting resistivity to concentration it solves this equation for the concentration numerically.

Assumptions and valid range

The calculation assumes silicon at 300 K containing a single dopant species that is fully ionized, and ignores the minority-carrier contribution. As a guide, it is intended for 10¹³–10¹⁹ cm⁻³. In compensated crystals that contain both donors and acceptors, the carrier density is set by the net concentration while the mobility is reduced by scattering from the total concentration, so the resistivity is higher than this tool gives. Above 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, incomplete ionization and band-gap narrowing come into play, and the deviation from empirical conversion tables grows.

Worked example

With the default p-type, NA = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, the hole mobility is about 462 cm²/V·s, so ρ = 1/(1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁵ × 462) ≈ 13.5 Ω·cm. For n-type at the same concentration, the electron mobility is about 1362 cm²/V·s, roughly three times larger, giving ρ ≈ 4.58 Ω·cm. Going the other way, a wafer specified as "p-type 10 Ω·cm" works out to about 1.36×10¹⁵ cm⁻³, and "n-type 1 Ω·cm" to about 4.9×10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Raising the concentration 100-fold to 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ gives a p-type resistivity of about 0.197 Ω·cm, not the 0.135 Ω·cm that a simple 1/100 scaling would suggest, because Coulomb scattering from the dopants lowers the mobility to about 317 cm²/V·s.

Values used and their sources

The coefficients of the mobility formula are representative fit values matched to measured silicon mobility, and are widely used in semiconductor device textbooks and tutorials. The elementary charge is q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. For real wafers, resistivity is usually measured by methods such as the four-point probe and converted to concentration using a conversion standard such as SEMI MF723 (formerly ASTM F723). Treat this tool's results as estimates, and check against a conversion standard or measurements when you need accurate values.

Practice semiconductor physics questions

What is the approximate resistivity of n-type silicon with an electron density of 1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and an electron mobility of 1000 cm²/V·s? (Ignore holes; elementary charge 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C)

  1. AAbout 0.06 Ω·cm
  2. BAbout 0.6 Ω·cm
  3. CAbout 60 Ω·cm
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. About 0.6 Ω·cm

Conductivity is σ = qnμ = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 1×10¹⁶ × 1000 = 1.6 S/cm, and resistivity is its inverse, ρ ≈ 0.63 Ω·cm. ρ ≈ 1/(qnμ) is the basic formula for estimating dopant concentration from wafer resistivity. In practice, conversion tables (such as ASTM standards) that include the doping dependence of mobility are used; for example, a 1–10 Ω·cm p-type wafer corresponds to boron at about 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻³.

Which correctly describes the relationship between the resistivity of a silicon wafer and its doping concentration?

  1. AThe higher the doping, the higher the resistivity
  2. BDoping and resistivity are unrelated
  3. CThe higher the doping, the lower the resistivity
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The higher the doping, the lower the resistivity

More dopant means more carriers and lower resistivity, so wafer resistivity is specified as a proxy for doping concentration: 1–10 Ω·cm p-type corresponds to boron at about 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻³, and 0.01 Ω·cm to about 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. At high concentrations, ionized-impurity scattering lowers mobility, so the relationship is not simply inverse; standard conversion tables such as ASTM's are used.

Which correctly describes how the resistivity of an intrinsic semiconductor depends on temperature?

  1. AResistivity decreases as temperature rises
  2. BResistivity increases as temperature rises
  3. CResistivity is constant regardless of temperature
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Resistivity decreases as temperature rises

In an intrinsic semiconductor, the number of carriers excited across the band gap rises exponentially with temperature, so resistivity falls (a negative temperature coefficient). In metals the carrier count is nearly fixed and lattice-vibration scattering increases, so resistivity rises. NTC thermistors exploit this behavior. Note that in doped silicon near room temperature the carrier count is roughly constant, so there is a range where resistance actually rises as mobility drops.

Which method is used to measure wafer resistivity without contact?

  1. ASIMS
  2. BTEM observation
  3. CMeasurement using eddy currents
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Measurement using eddy currents

An AC magnetic field induces eddy currents in a wafer whose magnitude depends on its conductivity (sheet resistance); detecting them with a coil measures resistivity without contact. Unlike four-point probing, it neither scratches nor contaminates the surface, so it is used for incoming wafer inspection and for metal-film thickness (derived from sheet resistance), and mapping reveals in-wafer distributions.

FAQ

Why do n-type and p-type silicon have different resistivities at the same concentration?

Because in silicon the electron mobility is about three times the hole mobility. At low concentrations electrons have about 1400 cm²/V·s and holes about 470 cm²/V·s, so at the same concentration n-type has roughly one third the resistivity of p-type.

To make the resistivity 10 times higher, should I reduce the concentration to one tenth?

At low concentrations of 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ or below, mobility is nearly constant, so roughly one tenth is enough. At high concentrations, lowering the doping raises the mobility, which slows the increase in resistivity, so you need to reduce the concentration by more than a factor of ten. Check the change in slope on the chart.

Can I use this to find the doping of a real wafer from its resistivity?

Yes, as an estimate. However, compensation from both donors and acceptors, or thermal donors that form when CZ silicon is annealed around 450 °C, change the relationship between resistivity and concentration. For accurate values, check against a conversion standard or a concentration measurement such as SIMS.
